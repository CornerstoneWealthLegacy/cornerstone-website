Asset Protection in Florida: Planning for Your Specific Situation

Quick Answer

Florida offers some of the strongest asset-protection tools in the country—homestead protection, tenancy by the entireties, and protected retirement accounts—but they work differently for snowbirds, blended families, business owners, and special-needs families, so the plan has to fit your situation.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney June 26, 2026

Why does Florida have a reputation for protecting assets?

In my practice, new clients often arrive having heard that Florida is a "debtor-friendly" state, and there's real truth to it—though the protections are more specific than the rumors suggest. Three pillars do most of the work.

First is the homestead exemption under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, which protects your primary residence from most creditors regardless of its value (subject to acreage limits and certain exceptions like mortgages, taxes, and construction liens). Second is tenancy by the entireties, a form of ownership available only to married couples that can shield jointly held property from the creditors of just one spouse. Third, Florida law strongly protects retirement accounts, certain annuities, and life insurance proceeds from creditors.

What I tell Florida clients is this: these protections are powerful, but they are not automatic shields against every debt, and they don't substitute for a thoughtful estate plan. Asset protection and estate planning are two sides of the same coin—you want to protect what you have during life and direct it cleanly at death.

Snowbirds and new residents: is your Florida residency 'real' enough?

This is the issue I see most often with part-time Floridians. To claim Florida's homestead creditor protection and the homestead property-tax exemption (and the Save Our Homes cap that limits how fast your assessed value can rise), Florida has to genuinely be your permanent residence—your domicile—not just where you spend the winter.

Establishing Florida domicile generally involves consistent, documentable steps that all point the same direction:

  • Filing a Declaration of Domicile with the clerk of court
  • Registering to vote and actually voting in Florida
  • Getting a Florida driver's license and registering your vehicles here
  • Applying for the homestead property-tax exemption with your county property appraiser
  • Updating your estate planning documents to recite Florida residency
Snowbird tip: If you owned a home in another state, ask whether Florida's portability rules let you transfer accumulated Save Our Homes tax savings to your new Florida homestead. Timing and prior-residency facts matter, so this is worth confirming with the property appraiser and your advisor.

Just as important: documents that were valid up north—your will, power of attorney, and healthcare directives—should be reviewed for Florida law. A durable power of attorney (Chapter 709) and healthcare surrogate designation (F.S. § 765.202) drafted out of state may be honored here, but Florida has its own formalities, and a Florida-executed set avoids arguments at the worst possible moment.

Blended families: protecting a spouse without disinheriting your kids

For second marriages and blended families, Florida's protections can quietly collide with your wishes if you don't plan. Two rules surprise people.

First, Florida's elective share (F.S. § 732.2065) gives a surviving spouse the right to claim a portion of the deceased spouse's estate—even if the will leaves them less. You generally cannot simply write a spouse out without their informed agreement.

Second, the homestead is special. Florida's homestead devise restrictions (F.S. § 732.4015) limit how you can leave your home if you're survived by a spouse or minor child. A common shock: a surviving spouse may receive only a life estate (or an elective interest) in the homestead, with the children taking the remainder—an outcome people don't always intend.

In my practice, the tools that solve this are usually a properly drafted revocable trust (Chapter 736), sometimes paired with a marital trust that provides for the surviving spouse during life while preserving the remainder for children from a prior marriage, and—when entered knowingly and voluntarily—a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement that waives certain spousal rights.

Business owners and special-needs families: the situations that need extra care

Business owners face a different worry: keeping personal and business creditors separated. Operating through a properly maintained entity, holding real estate thoughtfully, coordinating buy-sell arrangements, and being deliberate about how you title assets between spouses all matter. Florida's protection of certain entity interests can be meaningful, but the analysis is fact-specific—and protections you set up after a claim arises can be challenged as a fraudulent transfer. The honest rule is: asset protection planning works best when it's done early, before any cloud is on the horizon.

Special-needs families have perhaps the highest stakes. Leaving money outright to a loved one who receives needs-based government benefits like SSI or Medicaid can unintentionally disqualify them. The solution is typically a special needs trust (also called a supplemental needs trust), built under Florida's Trust Code and the governing federal rules, so the funds supplement rather than replace public benefits.

⚠ Don't use a generic form here: Special needs trusts and self-settled trusts have technical requirements, and a single wrong clause can cost a beneficiary their benefits. This is one area where a Florida-specific, professionally drafted trust truly matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Florida's homestead exemption protect a vacation home?
No. Florida's homestead creditor and tax protections apply to your permanent primary residence, not a second home or investment property. A part-time or seasonal home generally doesn't qualify.
Can I leave my Florida home to anyone I want in my will?
Not always. If you are survived by a spouse or a minor child, Florida's homestead devise restrictions (F.S. § 732.4015) limit how you can leave the property, which can override what your will says.
Does tenancy by the entireties protect a married couple from all creditors?
It can protect jointly owned property from a creditor of only one spouse, but not from a joint debt the couple owes together. It also requires the specific married-couple ownership form, so titling matters.
I'm a snowbird—do I need new Florida estate documents?
Often yes. Out-of-state wills, powers of attorney, and healthcare directives may be honored but can run into Florida's formalities. Having Florida-executed documents reduces delays and disputes.
Can I set up asset protection after I've been sued?
Transfers made to dodge an existing or anticipated creditor can be unwound as fraudulent transfers. Asset protection is most effective when established well before any claim arises.
Will an inheritance hurt my child's government benefits?
It can, if left outright. A properly drafted special needs trust under Florida and federal rules lets you provide for a loved one without disqualifying them from needs-based benefits.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida gives families genuinely strong tools—homestead protection, tenancy by the entireties, protected retirement accounts, and flexible trusts—but the right combination depends entirely on your situation. A snowbird needs to nail down domicile and Florida documents; a blended family needs to reconcile spousal rights with children from a prior marriage; a business owner needs early, deliberate planning; and a special-needs family needs a precisely drafted trust.
