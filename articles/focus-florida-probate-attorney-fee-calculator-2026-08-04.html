Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator: What You'll Really Pay in 2026

Quick Answer

A Florida probate attorney fee calculator estimates fees using the statutory percentage schedule in F.S. § 733.6171 — roughly 3% of the first $1 million in estate assets — but that schedule is a legal ceiling that's presumed reasonable, not a mandatory price. Most straightforward Florida estates are actually handled for a flat fee well below that percentage, and the law now requires attorneys to tell you, in writing, that the fee is negotiable.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 4, 2026

How a Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator Actually Works

In my practice, the first thing families ask when they hear the word "probate" is: what is this going to cost me? A Florida probate attorney fee calculator is just a tool that takes the value of the probate estate and runs it through the tiered percentage schedule set out in F.S. § 733.6171(3). That statute defines "compensable value" as the inventory value of the probate assets plus any income the estate earns during administration — and it generally excludes homestead property, since homestead usually passes outside the probate estate under Florida's constitutional homestead protections.

The statutory schedule is tiered, roughly like this:

  • A flat starting fee for small estates — around $1,500 for estates under $40,000
  • Approximately 3% on the first $1 million of compensable value
  • Declining percentages on value above $1 million, stepping down further for value above $3 million, $5 million, and $10 million, eventually reaching around 1% for the largest estates

So for a $500,000 estate, a calculator using this schedule would produce a statutory estimate of roughly $15,000. That number is real — it's what the statute says is presumed reasonable — but presumed reasonable is not the same as required.

Presumed Reasonable Doesn't Mean Mandatory

This is the single most misunderstood part of Florida probate fees, and it's the reason so many families overestimate their costs before they ever call a lawyer. Under F.S. § 733.6171(2), the percentage schedule creates a fee that courts will presume is reasonable if it's challenged — but the statute explicitly allows the personal representative and the attorney to agree to a different fee arrangement entirely.

Effective July 1, 2026, Florida law reinforces this point further: attorneys handling probate matters must now disclose in writing that the statutory fee is negotiable, and must provide clients a summary of the services performed or hours worked. This change was part of a broader 2026 probate reform package (CS/HB 1337) aimed at giving families more transparency and more leverage in fee discussions.

What this means in practice: In my experience, most uncontested, reasonably organized Florida estates — the kind with a clear will, cooperative beneficiaries, and no litigation — are handled on a flat fee, often in the range of a few thousand dollars, rather than a percentage of estate value. The statutory schedule tends to apply more often in larger, more complex, or contested estates where the percentage genuinely reflects the time and risk involved.

What Drives the Real Cost of Florida Probate

A calculator can only estimate attorney fees off a formula. The real-world cost of Florida probate depends on several factors that no calculator can fully capture:

  • Type of administration — formal administration (the standard process), summary administration (a faster, cheaper track available for smaller or older estates), or ancillary administration (needed when a non-Florida resident owned Florida property)
  • Estate complexity — multiple properties, business interests, out-of-state assets, or unclear title all add attorney time
  • Family dynamics — a will contest, a disputed personal representative, or a creditor claim can move a matter from a flat-fee arrangement into hourly, litigation-driven billing
  • County practice — local probate courts and clerks have their own filing procedures and timelines that can affect how efficiently a case moves

Notably, as of July 1, 2026, the asset threshold to qualify for Florida's faster summary administration doubled — from $75,000 to $150,000 (excluding exempt property and, in most cases, the homestead). That change alone will move a meaningful number of small and mid-size Florida estates into the cheaper, faster probate track this year.

Who Pays the Attorney, and When

Probate attorney fees are paid from the assets of the estate, not out of the personal representative's own pocket, and they are paid before any distributions go out to beneficiaries. The probate court has to approve the fee before it's paid — typically the attorney (or the personal representative) files a petition describing the services performed and the fee requested, and interested parties have an opportunity to object under the Florida Probate Rules before the court signs off.

⚠ A word of caution about calculators: Every online Florida probate fee calculator — including the statutory percentage figures above — produces an estimate only. It cannot account for your specific estate's assets, whether a will is being contested, whether a bank or title company is creating complications, or whether summary administration is available to you. Treat any number a calculator gives you as a starting point for a conversation with a Florida probate attorney, not a quote.

A New Protection: Banks That Won't Honor Letters of Administration

One notable change from the 2026 reform addresses a frustration I've heard from personal representatives for years: banks and financial institutions that drag their feet or outright refuse to honor validly issued Letters of Administration. Under the updated law, a personal representative can now sue a bank that improperly refuses to recognize the letters, and if the bank loses, it — not the estate — must pay the attorney's fees for that fight. This gives families a real remedy where before there was mostly frustration and delay.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Florida statutory probate fee (3% of the estate) mandatory?
No. F.S. § 733.6171 sets a fee schedule that is presumed reasonable if challenged in court, but the attorney and personal representative can agree to a different arrangement, and as of 2026 the attorney must disclose in writing that the fee is negotiable.
Does a Florida probate attorney fee calculator include homestead property?
Generally no. Homestead property is typically excluded from the compensable value used to calculate statutory attorney fees, since it usually passes outside the formal probate estate under Florida's homestead protections.
What is summary administration, and does it cost less?
Summary administration is a faster, simplified Florida probate process available for smaller estates, and it's generally less expensive than formal administration because it involves less court supervision and paperwork. As of July 1, 2026, the qualifying asset threshold increased from $75,000 to $150,000, opening this cheaper option to more estates.
Who actually pays the probate attorney's fee in Florida?
The estate pays it, out of estate assets, before beneficiaries receive their distributions — not the personal representative personally, and not the beneficiaries directly out of pocket.
Can I negotiate a flat fee with a Florida probate attorney instead of a percentage?
Yes. Florida law explicitly allows fee arrangements other than the statutory percentage schedule, and many straightforward, uncontested estates are handled on a flat-fee basis that is lower than the statutory formula would produce.
Does the probate court have to approve attorney fees?
Yes. The court must approve attorney compensation before it's paid from the estate, typically through a fee petition that interested parties can review and, if appropriate, object to under the Florida Probate Rules.

The Truestead Takeaway

A statutory calculator can give you a rough ceiling on what Florida probate attorney fees could be, but it can't tell you what your family will actually pay — that depends on the size and complexity of the estate, whether summary administration is available, and the fee arrangement you negotiate with your attorney. In my practice, I encourage families to use these calculators as a starting point for a conversation, not as a final number, and to ask directly about flat-fee options before assuming the statutory percentage applies. If you're facing a Florida probate matter, the sensible next step is a candid conversation with a Florida probate attorney about your specific estate.
