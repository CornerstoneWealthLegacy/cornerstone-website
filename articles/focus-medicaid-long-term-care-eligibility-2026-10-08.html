Medicaid Long Term Care Eligibility in Florida: What You Actually Need to Qualify

Quick Answer

To meet Florida Medicaid long term care eligibility in 2026, an applicant generally must need help with at least two daily living activities, have countable assets under $2,000 (for a single applicant), and have gross monthly income at or below $2,982, with higher income handled through a Qualified Income Trust. A spouse who is not applying is protected by separate, more generous rules.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney October 8, 2026

The Three-Part Test Florida Uses to Decide Eligibility

In my practice, I tell families that Medicaid long term care eligibility in Florida comes down to three separate questions, and you have to clear all three. Florida is what's called an income-cap state, which means you cannot simply spend your way down to the asset limit the way you might in some other states. There is a hard income ceiling, and if you cross it without planning, the application gets denied outright unless you've set up the right legal tool first.

The three parts are:

  • Medical (clinical) eligibility: the applicant must be assessed as needing a nursing-facility level of care, generally meaning help with two or more Activities of Daily Living such as bathing, dressing, toileting, or eating.
  • Asset (resource) eligibility: countable assets must fall at or below the state's resource limit.
  • Income eligibility: gross monthly income must fall at or below the state's income cap, or be redirected through a Qualified Income Trust.

Florida's Department of Elder Affairs conducts the clinical review through its CARES program (Comprehensive Assessment and Review for Long-Term Care Services), which involves a face-to-face assessment of the applicant's functional needs.

The 2026 Asset Limit: $2,000, With Important Exceptions

For a single applicant, Florida's countable resource limit for long term care Medicaid in 2026 is $2,000. That number sounds alarmingly low to most of my clients, and I understand why, but it only applies to countable assets. Florida law exempts several important categories of property from that count, including, under F.S. § 409.901:

  • The applicant's homestead, up to a home equity cap of $752,000 in 2026, as long as the applicant's spouse lives there, or the applicant intends to return home, or certain other conditions are met.
  • One vehicle, regardless of value.
  • Prepaid burial contracts and a small amount of funds set aside for burial.
  • Certain personal and household belongings.

For a married couple where only one spouse needs care, Florida law allows the non-applicant spouse, often called the community spouse, to retain a separate, larger share of the couple's joint assets. That protection exists specifically so the healthy spouse is not left destitute while the other receives nursing-home-level care. I'd encourage any married couple facing this situation to have their specific numbers reviewed, because the community spouse resource allowance changes periodically and the math is rarely intuitive.

The 2026 Income Cap and Why the Qualified Income Trust Matters

This is the part of Medicaid long term care eligibility that trips up the most families, because it is counterintuitive. In 2026, if an applicant's gross monthly income exceeds $2,982, they are disqualified from the Institutional Care Program and the Statewide Medicaid Managed Care (SMMC) Long-Term Care waiver, even if that income isn't nearly enough to pay for care. A nursing home in Florida can easily cost several times that amount per month.

The fix, authorized under F.S. § 409.706(15), is a Qualified Income Trust, often called a Miller Trust. This is a specific type of irrevocable trust. The applicant's excess income is deposited into the trust each month, and because Florida law treats that deposited income as unavailable to the applicant for eligibility purposes, it no longer counts against the income cap. The trust must be drafted correctly, funded on time, and administered according to strict rules, including how the funds inside it may be spent. A trust that's set up wrong, or funded late, can delay or derail an otherwise-qualified application.

Why timing matters: A Qualified Income Trust generally needs to be established and funded before, or in the same month as, the Medicaid application, because eligibility is evaluated month by month. Getting the paperwork and bank account set up in advance avoids an unnecessary gap in coverage.

The Five-Year Look-Back Period on Gifts and Transfers

Florida enforces a five-year look-back period, consistent with federal Medicaid law and codified at F.S. § 409.9101. When someone applies for long term care Medicaid, the state reviews financial records going back five years to check for transfers made for less than fair market value, meaning gifts, below-market sales, or certain trust transfers.

If the state finds such a transfer, it doesn't deny the application outright. Instead, it calculates a penalty period: a span of months during which Medicaid will not pay for the applicant's long term care, based on the value of what was transferred divided by Florida's average private-pay cost of care. This is one of the most common and most painful surprises I see in my practice, often involving a well-meaning gift to a child or grandchild years earlier that the family never connected to a future Medicaid need.

⚠ A Common Misconception Many Floridians believe they can simply give away assets and apply for Medicaid once five years have passed. That is generally true in concept, but any transfer inside that five-year window can trigger a penalty period, and the rules around exempt transfers (for example, to a disabled child, or in certain caregiver situations) are narrow and fact-specific. This is an area where a short conversation well before a crisis can prevent a much longer delay later.

How to Actually Apply: ICP vs. the SMMC Waiver

Once medical, asset, and income eligibility are established, Florida offers long term care Medicaid through two main pathways:

  • The Institutional Care Program (ICP), which covers care in a licensed nursing facility.
  • The Statewide Medicaid Managed Care Long-Term Care (SMMC LTC) Waiver, under F.S. § 409.979, which can cover care at home, in assisted living, or in other community settings, as an alternative to a nursing facility.

Both require the CARES determination of nursing-facility level of care under F.S. § 409.985(3). The SMMC waiver, however, has limited enrollment slots and often involves a waitlist, so timing and early application matter even when institutional placement isn't the immediate goal.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Florida Medicaid asset limit for long term care in 2026?
For a single applicant, the countable resource limit is $2,000 in 2026. A home (with equity up to $752,000) and one vehicle are generally exempt, and married couples have additional protections for the non-applicant spouse.
What happens if my income is too high to qualify?
If gross monthly income exceeds the 2026 cap of $2,982, the applicant generally cannot qualify for Florida's ICP or SMMC Long-Term Care waiver unless they establish and properly fund a Qualified Income Trust (Miller Trust) to redirect the excess income.
Can I give my house or savings to my children before applying for Medicaid?
Any transfer for less than fair market value made within the five-year look-back period can trigger a Medicaid penalty period, delaying coverage. Some transfers are exempt under specific circumstances, so this should be reviewed individually before any gifting happens.
Will my spouse lose everything if I need nursing home care?
No. Florida law allows the non-applicant community spouse to retain a protected share of the couple's combined assets and, in many cases, a portion of income, specifically so the spouse remaining at home isn't impoverished.
Does Medicaid cover assisted living or only nursing homes in Florida?
Florida's SMMC Long-Term Care Waiver can cover care in assisted living facilities and at home, not just skilled nursing facilities, though enrollment is limited and a waitlist may apply.
How is medical eligibility for Medicaid long term care determined?
The Florida Department of Elder Affairs, through its CARES program, conducts a face-to-face assessment to determine whether the applicant needs a nursing-facility level of care, generally meaning assistance with two or more activities of daily living.

The Truestead Takeaway

Medicaid long term care eligibility in Florida is genuinely workable for most families, but it rewards planning and punishes guesswork. The asset limit, the income cap, and the five-year look-back period all interact in ways that aren't obvious from a quick search, and a Qualified Income Trust or a poorly timed gift can make the difference between prompt approval and months of denied coverage. If you or a family member may need long term care in the next few years, the sensible next step is to have your specific income, assets, and any past transfers reviewed by a Florida elder law attorney before you apply, not after.
