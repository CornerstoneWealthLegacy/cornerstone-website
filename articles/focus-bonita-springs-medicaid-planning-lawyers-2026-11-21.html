Bonita Springs Medicaid Planning Lawyers: What Florida Families Need to Know

Quick Answer

In Florida, only a licensed attorney may legally provide Medicaid planning advice; non-attorneys who do so may be committing a crime. If you or a loved one in Bonita Springs needs nursing home or long-term care Medicaid, an elder law attorney can help you understand the current income and asset limits, use tools like a Qualified Income Trust, and protect assets for a spouse or family within the rules of Florida law.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney November 21, 2026

Why Bonita Springs Families Search for Medicaid Planning Lawyers

When a parent or spouse needs nursing home care in Bonita Springs or anywhere in Collier or Lee County, the cost can be staggering, and many families don't realize Florida Medicaid can help pay for that care if the applicant's income and assets fall within strict limits. The trouble is that the rules are precise, the paperwork is unforgiving, and a single misstep (a gift to a grandchild, an improperly titled account, an income dollar over the cap) can delay eligibility for months. That's where a Medicaid planning lawyer comes in: someone who understands how to structure a family's finances, legally and transparently, so a loved one qualifies for benefits without losing everything they've worked for.

In my practice, I find that most families come to this search already stressed and confused, often after a hospital discharge planner or nursing home admissions coordinator has given them a deadline. The good news is that Florida law provides real, lawful tools to protect a spouse's home, income, and savings. The key is using the right ones, correctly, and early enough to matter.

Why It Matters That You Hire an Actual Attorney

This is the single most important thing to understand before you hire anyone for Medicaid planning in Florida: the Florida Supreme Court has ruled that Medicaid planning is the practice of law, and it may only be performed by a Florida Bar licensed attorney. This ruling came out of a formal advisory opinion issued by the Florida Supreme Court in 2015, and it remains in force today.

What does that mean in practice? A non-attorney 'Medicaid planner,' financial advisor, or document preparation service can legally help you gather paperwork, fill out the Medicaid application itself, and act as a go-between with the state Medicaid agency (the Department of Children and Families in Florida). What they cannot legally do is advise you on how to restructure your assets, draft trusts or transfer strategies, or tell you which legal tools will get you qualified. Doing so without a law license is the unlicensed practice of law, which Florida treats as a serious offense.

⚠ Buyer Beware If someone who is not a licensed Florida attorney is advising you on how to move assets, create trusts, or restructure income to qualify for Medicaid, that advice falls outside what they are legally permitted to provide. Ask directly whether the person helping you is a Florida Bar member, and consider asking whether they are board certified in elder law, a credential held by a relatively small number of attorneys statewide.

Florida Medicaid's Income and Asset Rules (Current as of 2026)

Florida Medicaid for long-term care (sometimes called the Institutional Care Program, or ICP) has firm numerical limits. These figures are adjusted periodically, so they should always be verified with your attorney at the time you apply, but as of 2026 the general framework works like this:

  • Income limit: A single applicant's gross monthly income from all sources generally cannot exceed a set cap (roughly in the high $2,900s per month in 2026). If income exceeds that cap, Florida law does not disqualify the applicant outright; instead, it requires the use of a Qualified Income Trust, also called a Miller Trust, which redirects excess income so the applicant can still qualify.
  • Asset limit: A single applicant may generally hold no more than $2,000 in countable assets on the date of application. Certain assets, most notably the homestead up to a substantial equity cap, are exempt from this count.
  • Community spouse protection: When one spouse needs nursing home care and the other remains at home, Florida law allows the at-home (community) spouse to retain a protected share of the couple's combined assets, up to a set ceiling that is updated periodically, without jeopardizing the institutionalized spouse's eligibility.

These numbers change, sometimes annually, so treat any dollar figure you read online (including here) as a starting point for a conversation with your attorney, not a guarantee.

The Five-Year Look-Back Period

Under Florida law, the Medicaid agency reviews the applicant's financial transactions going back 60 months (five years) from the date of application. This is often called the Medicaid look-back period, and it exists to prevent people from giving away assets right before applying for benefits. If the agency finds uncompensated transfers (gifts, below-market sales, or certain trust transfers) during that window, it can impose a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay for care.

The length of that penalty is calculated by dividing the value of the improper transfer by a state-determined 'penalty divisor,' a dollar figure representing the average monthly cost of care in Florida, which is also updated periodically. This is precisely why Medicaid planning done well in advance, often years before care is needed, tends to produce far better outcomes than planning done in a crisis. That said, even families facing an urgent need (a parent already in a nursing home, for example) often still have legal options; crisis planning is a real and valuable part of elder law practice, it's just more constrained than advance planning.

Plan Ahead When You Can Tools like irrevocable Medicaid Asset Protection Trusts are generally most effective when created well outside the five-year look-back window, which is why families who think ahead often preserve significantly more for the next generation.

Other Documents Every Bonita Springs Family Should Have

Medicaid planning rarely happens in isolation. In my practice, I routinely pair it with the broader legal framework that protects someone as they age, including:

  • A Durable Power of Attorney under Florida Statute Chapter 709, so a trusted person can manage finances if the applicant becomes incapacitated.
  • A Health Care Surrogate designation under Florida Statute § 765.202, so medical decisions can be made by someone the applicant trusts.
  • Awareness of Chapter 744 guardianship law, which becomes relevant if no advance directives are in place and a court must appoint a decision-maker.
  • Knowledge of nursing home residents' rights under Florida Statute Chapter 400, including the right to manage one's own finances or have a trusted representative do so, and the right to be free from abuse, neglect, or exploitation.

Having these documents in place before a health crisis makes Medicaid planning, and every other part of a family's long-term care journey, considerably smoother.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do Medicaid planning myself without a lawyer in Florida?
You can fill out and submit the Medicaid application yourself, but Florida's Supreme Court has ruled that advising on how to legally restructure assets and income to qualify is the practice of law, meaning non-attorneys are not permitted to provide that advice.
What is a Qualified Income Trust and do I need one?
A Qualified Income Trust, also called a Miller Trust, is required under Florida Medicaid rules whenever an applicant's gross monthly income exceeds the current income cap; it redirects the excess income so the applicant can still become eligible.
Will my home be taken to pay for nursing home care in Florida?
Generally, a Florida homestead is exempt from Medicaid's countable asset limit up to a substantial equity cap, though Florida's Medicaid estate recovery program may make a claim against the estate after death, which is why advance planning with an attorney matters.
How far back does Florida Medicaid look at my financial transactions?
Florida applies a 60-month (five-year) look-back period on asset transfers; gifts or uncompensated transfers made during that window can trigger a penalty period of Medicaid ineligibility.
What happens if my spouse needs care but I still live at home?
Florida law allows the at-home spouse, known as the community spouse, to retain a protected portion of the couple's combined assets without that amount counting against the institutionalized spouse's Medicaid eligibility, within limits set by the state.
How do I know if a Bonita Springs attorney is qualified to do Medicaid planning?
Confirm the person is a licensed Florida Bar member in good standing, and ask whether they hold board certification in elder law, a credential awarded to a relatively small number of Florida attorneys who have demonstrated specific experience and knowledge in this field.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida Medicaid planning is a precise, document-driven area of law where small mistakes carry real consequences, and the state has made clear that this kind of advice must come from a licensed attorney, not a document preparer or financial planner without a law license. Income limits, asset limits, and the five-year look-back period all shift periodically, so any numbers you see online, including in this article, should be confirmed with a Florida attorney before you make decisions. If you or a loved one in Bonita Springs is facing a long-term care need, the sensible next step is a candid conversation with a Florida elder law attorney who can review your specific finances, family situation, and timeline, and explain which lawful tools actually apply to you.
