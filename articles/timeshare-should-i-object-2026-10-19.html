Should I Sign the Objection Form in a Florida Timeshare Foreclosure Notice?

Quick Answer

Signing the objection form forces the lienholder into judicial foreclosure instead of a trustee sale, which can be useful if you have a real defense, but it also gives up the trustee sale's automatic no-deficiency protection and exposes you to a possible money judgment. It is a decision to make with advice, not a form to sign or ignore out of fear.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Real Estate Attorney October 18, 2026

What the objection form actually does

In my practice, I hear from a lot of owners like Patricia: 70 years old, living in Tulsa, holding a Disney Vacation Club interest at Vero Beach, and staring at an objection form she's afraid to touch. That fear is understandable, but the form itself is simply a legal switch. Flip it, and you change which track the foreclosure travels on.

Under Florida's timeshare statute, when a lienholder starts a trustee foreclosure, the owner has the right to object to that procedure. The statute requires the notice to include a form stating: "The undersigned obligor exercises the obligor's right to object to the use of the trustee foreclosure procedure contained in section 721.855, Florida Statutes." The same objection right applies to mortgage lien foreclosures under F.S. § 721.856.

If you sign and return that form, the trustee cannot proceed with the non-judicial trustee sale for that specific default. The lienholder's only remaining path is to file a judicial foreclosure lawsuit in a Florida court. You can object any time before the trustee's sale actually happens, so the window is wider than most owners realize.

The trade-off: two columns, one decision

This is the heart of it. There is no universally right answer, only a trade-off that depends on your facts.

What you gain by objecting:

  • The matter moves into court, where you can raise real defenses (for example, disputes over the amount owed, notice defects, or assessment errors) before a judge.
  • You gain the procedural protections of a civil lawsuit: formal service, discovery, and the ability to negotiate from inside a court case rather than outside one.
  • It can buy time and structure if you have a genuine dispute or a specific negotiation strategy already in motion.

What you give up by objecting:

  • You lose the benefit of a completed trustee sale, which, once finished, releases the owner from the amounts secured by that lien and bars the lienholder from pursuing a deficiency afterward.
  • Judicial foreclosure under F.S. § 721.16 exposes you to a money judgment, and the statute allows for recovery of attorney's fees in that court action, costs you simply do not face in an uncontested trustee sale.
  • If you have no real defense and no negotiation plan, objecting can turn a clean, self-limiting process into a longer, costlier one with a judgment at the end instead of a release.
The plain trade: Objecting trades the speed and finality of trustee foreclosure for the protections and exposure of a lawsuit. Neither path is automatically better. The right choice depends on whether you actually have something to fight about.

What happens if you do nothing and let the trustee sale proceed

I want to say this plainly, once, because it matters: a completed trustee sale releases the owner from the amounts secured by the lien being foreclosed, and the lienholder may not seek a deficiency judgment against the owner after that sale. This is one of the real protections built into Florida's timeshare trustee foreclosure statute, and it is the outcome many owners actually want. If a clean release from the debt, with no further liability, is your goal, an owner who wants only that outcome may not need a lawyer to achieve it.

You also keep your cure rights right up until the sale. F.S. § 721.855 and § 721.856 both allow an owner to cure the default in the manner described in the notice at any time before the trustee's sale. Once the trustee issues the certificate of sale, however, there is no right of redemption, so that window does close.

When an objection genuinely makes sense

I don't tell every client to sign, and I don't tell every client to leave the form alone. An objection tends to make sense when there is something specific behind it: a real dispute about whether the default notice was accurate, a billing or assessment error, a question about whether the lien was properly created or assigned, or a deliberate strategy to negotiate a resolution while the matter sits in a judicial posture rather than a trustee's calendar. It rarely makes sense as a reflex, something signed simply because the form arrived and signing felt like doing something.

⚠ A word of caution: Do not stop making any payments you are otherwise obligated to make based on anything in this article, and do not sign or decline to sign the objection form without understanding what it changes for your specific notice. The consequences run in both directions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does objecting to the trustee foreclosure stop the foreclosure entirely?
No. It stops the non-judicial trustee sale process for that default, but it does not stop foreclosure itself. The lienholder may then pursue the matter through a judicial foreclosure lawsuit under F.S. § 721.16.
Can I still cure the default after I object?
Florida law allows an owner to cure the default in the manner set forth in the notice at any time before the trustee's sale. Once you object and the matter shifts to judicial foreclosure, the cure mechanics are governed by that court process instead, so this should be reviewed with an attorney for your specific notice.
If I never sign anything and the trustee sale happens, can Disney or the managing entity still come after me for money?
A completed trustee sale releases the owner from the amounts secured by the lien being foreclosed, and the lienholder may not seek a deficiency judgment after that sale. This no-deficiency protection is specific to the trustee foreclosure process, not to judicial foreclosure.

The Truestead Takeaway

Patricia's instinct to be cautious before signing anything is a good one. The objection form is a real legal choice with real consequences in both directions: it can open the door to defenses and negotiation, or it can trade away a clean, no-deficiency outcome in favor of court exposure you didn't need. I never tell a client to sign or not sign as a blanket rule, because the right answer depends entirely on the specific notice, the specific lien, and what the owner actually wants at the end. The sensible next step is to have a Florida attorney review your actual notice before the deadline to object or cure passes, so the decision is made on purpose, not out of fear.
