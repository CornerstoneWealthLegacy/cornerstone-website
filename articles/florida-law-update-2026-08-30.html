Florida's Probate Law Just Changed: What Chapter 2026-57 Means for Your Family

Quick Answer

As of July 1, 2026, Florida raised the threshold for simplified summary administration from $75,000 to $150,000, and gave personal representatives new legal tools — including a fee-shifting enforcement statute — to force banks and financial institutions to cooperate with valid estate administration.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 30, 2026

What Actually Changed on July 1, 2026

In my practice, I tell clients that probate law in Florida doesn't change often — but when it does, it's worth paying attention. This year it changed in a meaningful way. Governor DeSantis signed CS/HB 1337 into law as Chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida, and its provisions took effect on July 1, 2026. The new law amends several parts of the Florida Statutes governing banking, estates, and probate procedure.

The headline change is this: summary administration — Florida's simplified, faster alternative to full probate — is now available when the value of a decedent's Florida property, excluding property that's exempt from creditor claims (like protected homestead), is $150,000 or less. That's double the prior $75,000 threshold that had been in place for years.

It's important to understand who this applies to. The new, higher threshold only applies to people who passed away on or after July 1, 2026. If a loved one died before that date, the old $75,000 threshold still governs their estate, even if you're just now getting around to opening probate.

Why the Summary Administration Threshold Matters to Your Family

Summary administration is one of the most useful tools in Florida probate. It generally allows an estate to be settled without appointing a formal personal representative, without months of court supervision, and with far less expense than formal administration. It's typically reserved for smaller estates or estates where the decedent has been gone for a long time.

By doubling the dollar threshold to $150,000, the Legislature has made this faster, cheaper path available to significantly more Florida families. Consider a modest home held outside of a trust, a car, a bank account, and some personal belongings — many mid-size estates that previously required full administration may now qualify for summary administration instead.

  • Faster resolution for beneficiaries who need access to assets
  • Lower attorney's fees and court costs relative to formal administration
  • Less ongoing court supervision and fewer filings

This doesn't mean summary administration is automatic or always the right tool — an estate still needs to meet the statutory requirements, and there are situations (disputed claims, complicated assets, contested wills) where formal administration remains necessary regardless of dollar value.

New Power to Enforce a Personal Representative's Authority

The second major piece of this year's reform addresses a frustration I've heard from clients for years: a personal representative is legally authorized to act, but a bank or financial institution drags its feet, demands unnecessary paperwork, or simply won't cooperate. Chapter 2026-57 and companion legislation, CS/SB 1500, created a new statute — § 733.6125, Florida Statutes — that gives personal representatives a clearer path to ask the probate court to enforce their statutory authority against institutions that won't comply.

Why this matters: The new law also includes a fee-shifting provision for personal representatives who prevail in these enforcement proceedings — meaning an uncooperative institution may bear the cost of the fight, not just the estate.

This is a practical, meaningful change. Personal representatives have always had authority under Florida law to marshal and manage estate assets, but until now, enforcing that authority against a stonewalling institution could be slow and expensive. Giving personal representatives a defined enforcement mechanism — and a financial disincentive for institutions that resist — should make administration smoother for everyday Florida families.

The Florida Supreme Court Updated the Probate Rules to Match

Statutes and court rules have to move together, and the Florida Supreme Court didn't waste time. On July 16, 2026, the Court issued Opinion No. SC2026-0690, amending the Florida Probate Rules to align with Chapter 2026-57.

  • Rule 5.025 was amended to add a new classification: proceedings to enforce a personal representative's authority under section 733.612 are now treated as adversarial in nature, unless the court orders otherwise — meaning they follow more formal litigation-style procedures.
  • Rule 5.530(a)(7) was amended to raise the summary administration estate-value threshold from $75,000 to $150,000, matching the new statutory language in section 735.201.

For attorneys and personal representatives navigating these matters, this rule alignment means the procedural roadmap for enforcement actions and summary administration petitions is now settled, not just the underlying statutes.

What About Trusts? A Reminder on 2025's Changes

While 2026's headlines belong to probate, it's worth remembering that Florida's trust law also saw meaningful updates a bit more recently than some clients realize. Senate Bill 262 took effect June 20, 2025, and it remains current law. Under F.S. § 736.04117, an "authorized trustee" — generally a trustee other than the settlor or a beneficiary who has power to invade trust principal — now has broader flexibility to modify an irrevocable trust or move its assets into a new trust, even one created specifically for that purpose. Separately, F.S. § 736.1110 clarifies when lifetime gifts from a settlor can be counted against, or satisfy, a beneficiary's future trust distributions.

If your estate plan relies on an irrevocable trust drafted before mid-2025, it may be worth a review to understand how these trustee powers could apply to your situation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the new $150,000 summary administration threshold apply to estates already in probate?
No. The higher threshold only applies to decedents who died on or after July 1, 2026. Estates of people who passed away before that date are governed by the prior $75,000 limit.
Does homestead property count toward the $150,000 summary administration limit?
Generally, protected homestead and other property exempt from creditor claims are excluded when calculating whether an estate qualifies for summary administration, consistent with how the exemption has historically worked under Florida law.
What is the new enforcement power under F.S. § 733.6125 actually for?
It gives a personal representative a clearer statutory path to ask the probate court to compel a bank or financial institution to honor the personal representative's legal authority, and it allows the personal representative to potentially recover fees if they win that enforcement action.
Do I need to update my estate plan because of these 2026 changes?
Not necessarily — these changes mainly affect probate procedure rather than how you should draft a will or trust. But if you have questions about how your estate would be administered under the new rules, it's worth a conversation with a Florida attorney.
Are these probate law changes the same everywhere in Florida?
Yes. Chapter 2026-57 and the related Florida Supreme Court rule amendments apply statewide; they are not county-specific, though local probate court procedures can still vary somewhat in practice.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida's 2026 probate reform is genuinely good news for most families: more estates now qualify for the faster, less expensive summary administration process, and personal representatives finally have a real enforcement tool when banks or institutions won't cooperate. But the details matter — the July 1, 2026 effective date, the treatment of homestead property, and how these enforcement proceedings actually play out in court are all fact-specific. If you're currently administering an estate, drafting a will, or wondering how these changes touch your own plan, it's worth sitting down with a Florida attorney to review your situation under the current law.
