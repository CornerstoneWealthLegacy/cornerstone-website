Signing a Florida Lady Bird Deed Correctly: What Has to Be on the Page

Quick Answer

A valid Florida lady bird deed needs the correct legal description, clear life estate and reserved powers language, named remainder beneficiaries, two witnesses with printed names and addresses, a notary acknowledgment, and recording in the county where the property sits. Miss any one of these and the deed can be rejected at recording or challenged later.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 25, 2026

Yolanda's project: getting her mother's deed right the first time

Yolanda is 62, lives in Kendall, and has spent the last month researching how to put her mother's house into a lady bird deed so it passes to Yolanda and her brother without probate. She is the kind of person who reads the instructions before assembling the bookshelf, and she wants to know exactly what has to appear on this document before she brings it to her mother to sign. Yolanda is a composite of the many adult children Truestead works with, not an actual client, but her question is the one we hear constantly: what actually has to be on the page for this thing to hold up?

A quick refresher, since Truestead covers the basics elsewhere: a lady bird deed lets Yolanda's mother keep full control of her home for life, including the right to sell it, mortgage it, or change her mind entirely, while naming who inherits the property automatically at her death, outside of probate. This article stays narrowly on the mechanics: what language, whose signatures, and what recording steps make that deed enforceable.

The legal description, not the street address

This is where Yolanda almost went wrong. She assumed writing her mother's street address on the deed would be enough. It is not. Florida deeds require the full legal description of the property, the lot and block, metes and bounds, or condominium unit description that appears on the county's official records, not just the mailing address.

The safest source for this language is the deed by which Yolanda's mother currently holds title. Truestead pulls the legal description directly from that prior recorded deed or from the county property appraiser's records, then copies it precisely, including any easements or exceptions listed. A single dropped digit in a lot number or an incorrectly copied metes-and-bounds call can create a defective deed that a title company will flag years later, often when the family is trying to sell or refinance.

The language that makes it a lady bird deed and not just a life estate

Florida has no statute that creates the lady bird deed by name. It is not codified anywhere; it rests on longstanding Florida common law, title industry practice, and acceptance by title insurers. That means there is no official state form, and the deed's effectiveness depends entirely on how it is drafted. The document needs to do three things clearly:

  • Name the grantor (Yolanda's mother) and convey the property to herself for life, creating a traditional-looking life estate
  • Expressly reserve broad powers to the life tenant, typically the right to sell, mortgage, lease, or otherwise dispose of the property during her lifetime without needing the remainder beneficiaries' consent, and the right to revoke or change the remainder designation
  • Name the remainder beneficiaries (here, Yolanda and her brother) who receive the property automatically at the life tenant's death

It is this reserved-powers language, distinguishing it from an ordinary life estate deed, that makes the arrangement work the way families expect: full control now, automatic transfer later. If that language is vague or missing, a title company may treat it as a standard life estate instead, which limits the owner's ability to sell or mortgage the home without the remainder beneficiaries joining in.

Homestead note: If Yolanda's mother were married, Florida homestead law would require her spouse to join in signing the deed, regardless of whose name is on title. A homestead cannot be conveyed or devised away from a surviving spouse without that spouse's participation.

Two witnesses, a notary, and the address rule that trips people up

Florida law requires two subscribing witnesses for a deed conveying real property, in addition to a notary's acknowledgment. Both witnesses must be present when the grantor signs (or appear via approved audio-video technology, discussed below), and their signatures must be accompanied by their printed or typed names directly beneath the signature line.

Since a 2024 change to Florida's recording statute, each witness must also have their post office address printed, stamped, or typed on the deed beneath their name. This addition was meant to help curb property and deed fraud, but in practice it has become one of the most common reasons county clerks reject deeds for recording. Yolanda's checklist, then, looks like this:

  • Grantor's signature, matching the name on the deed exactly
  • Two witnesses' signatures, each with printed name and address
  • A notary's signature, seal, and proper acknowledgment certificate
  • The grantee's (beneficiary's) mailing address, which Florida recording law requires be listed on the face of the deed
⚠ Common rejection point: A missing witness address is now flagged frequently by Florida county clerks. If that line is left blank, or if a witness's signature appears without a printed name beneath it, the clerk can refuse to record the deed until it is corrected.

Can the witnesses or notary appear remotely?

Yes. Florida permits remote online notarization, and witnesses may also participate remotely through approved audio-video communication technology rather than being physically present in the room. This can matter for a family like Yolanda's if her mother is out of state for the winter, or if a witness cannot travel. The technology has to meet Florida's specific requirements for online notarization, so this is not simply a video call: it involves an approved online notary platform with identity verification and a recorded session. Once properly executed and recorded, a deed signed this way cannot later be challenged solely on the basis that the witnessing or notarization happened remotely.

Recording it in the right county, and what happens if you don't

A lady bird deed does nothing until it is recorded in the county where the property is located. For Yolanda's mother's home in Kendall, that means the Miami-Dade County Clerk of Courts, not any other county, and not simply keeping the signed original in a drawer. Recording places the deed into the county's official records, indexed under the grantor's and grantee's names, so that title searchers, future buyers, and title insurers can find it.

An unrecorded deed can still be valid between the parties who signed it, but it offers no protection against later claims, and Florida title standards generally treat lady bird deeds as needing to be recorded before the grantor's death to reliably avoid probate. Recording fees are calculated per page and per name, and because a lady bird deed conveying a homestead to family members typically involves no consideration, it generally does not trigger documentary stamp tax the way a sale would.

If a defect turns up after recording, such as a legal description error or a missing witness address, Florida practice generally calls for either a corrective deed, referencing and fixing the original, or a scrivener's affidavit for minor clerical issues. It is not something to patch informally; a defective deed in the chain of title can complicate a future sale or refinance for the very people it was meant to help. Frequently Asked Questions

Does a Florida lady bird deed have to be on a special state form?
No. Florida has no official lady bird deed form because the deed is not created by statute. It is drafted according to general Florida deed law and long-standing title industry practice, so accurate drafting matters more than following any particular template.
What happens if a witness signs but forgets to print their address?
Since a 2024 change to Florida's recording law, county clerks routinely reject deeds missing a witness's printed post office address. The deed generally needs to be corrected and re-recorded, or a corrective document filed, before it will be accepted.
Can Yolanda's mother's deed be signed if she is temporarily living out of state?
Florida allows remote online notarization and permits witnesses to participate through approved audio-video technology, which can make it possible to sign a valid deed while the owner is out of state, as long as the specific technology requirements are met.
Is a street address enough, or does the deed need the full legal description?
A street address alone is not sufficient. Florida deeds require the full legal description as it appears in the county's official records, typically copied from the property's prior recorded deed.
What if Yolanda's mother is married? Does her husband need to sign too?
If the home is homestead property and the owner is married, Florida homestead law requires the spouse to join in signing the deed, regardless of whose name appears on title.
Where does the deed get recorded?
It must be recorded with the Clerk of Courts in the county where the property is physically located, not the county where the owner happens to live if that differs.

The Truestead Takeaway

Yolanda's instinct to get every detail right was the correct one: a lady bird deed lives or dies on its details, since there is no Florida statute spelling out a required form. The legal description has to be copied precisely, the reserved powers language has to be clear enough that a title company recognizes it as a lady bird deed rather than a plain life estate, two witnesses need printed names and addresses, a notary has to properly acknowledge the signature, and the deed has to be recorded in the right county before it can do its job. If Yolanda's mother is married, her spouse's signature would also be required because of Florida's homestead protections. None of this is difficult once it is laid out, but small omissions, like a missing witness address, are exactly what cause deeds to bounce back from the clerk's office or cause title problems years later. Anyone preparing this kind of deed for a parent should have it reviewed by a Florida attorney familiar with lady bird deeds before it is signed and recorded.
