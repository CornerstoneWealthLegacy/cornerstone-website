Dad Wants to Stay in His House: Can Florida Medicaid Pay for Care at Home?

Quick Answer

Yes. Florida's Statewide Medicaid Managed Care Long-Term Care waiver can pay for personal care, homemaker help, and other services delivered right in a person's own home, but unlike nursing home Medicaid it is not guaranteed and usually involves a screening, a priority score, and a wait for an opening.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney September 24, 2026

Meet Art: A Korean War Veteran Who Isn't Leaving His House

Art is 89, lives in Holly Hill, and built the house he still calls home with his own hands after coming back from Korea. His daughter drives over every day to help him bathe, manage his medications, and get a hot meal on the table. She estimates she's putting in about four hours a day, on top of her own job and family. She has heard the words "nursing home" and watched her father's face fall every time. Art is a composite example, not an actual Truestead client, but his situation is one I hear described almost word for word in my office most weeks.

The good news for families like Art's is that Florida Medicaid does not force a choice between "pay privately for someone in the home" and "move to a facility." There is a real, funded path called the Long-Term Care (LTC) waiver, part of Florida's Statewide Medicaid Managed Care program, built specifically to pay for services that let someone who needs nursing-facility-level care stay in the community instead. I won't repeat our general eligibility guide here (income, assets, and the five-year lookback are covered in other articles), but I do want to walk through exactly what happens for someone like Art from the first phone call to services showing up at his door.

Step One: The ADRC Screening Call

Everything starts with a call to Florida's Aging and Disability Resource Center (ADRC), reachable through the statewide Elder Helpline. A family member can make this call on the person's behalf, which is exactly what Art's daughter did.

The screening itself is done by phone with the applicant and/or caregiver and typically takes 45 minutes to an hour. The ADRC specialist asks about the person's medical conditions, ability to perform daily activities like bathing, dressing, and toileting, cognitive status, current living arrangement, and the caregiver support already in place. For Art, that meant describing his mobility limitations, his daughter's four hours of daily help, and the fact that without her, he could not safely stay in his house alone.

This single call generates two things: a record in the system, and the raw information used to calculate what comes next.

Step Two: The Priority Score and the Waitlist

The information from the ADRC screening feeds into a priority score, calculated under a methodology set out in Florida Statutes, section 409.979, and administered by the Department of Elder Affairs. The score is meant to rank need, not need alone but urgency: someone with a fragile or absent caregiver, significant functional decline, or an unstable living situation typically scores higher than someone with stronger informal support already in place.

Here is the part families need to hear clearly: the Long-Term Care waiver is not an entitlement the way institutional nursing home Medicaid is. There is a statewide waitlist, and a higher priority score moves someone up the list, but it does not guarantee a specific wait time. Release from the waitlist happens when the state has an opening to enroll someone, and it is based on priority rank and available slots, not simply on how long a name has been sitting there.

What this meant for Art: Because his daughter's daily caregiving, while significant, was still holding things together, his initial priority score reflected real need but not a crisis-level gap in care. His daughter was told to expect a wait, and to keep documenting how the four hours a day was affecting her own ability to keep working.

Step Three: Enrollment in a Managed Care Plan

When an opening becomes available, the ADRC contacts the person (or family) and enrollment begins. Under Florida's Statewide Medicaid Managed Care structure, the LTC waiver is administered through managed care plans, not a single state office writing checks. Once financial Medicaid eligibility is confirmed through the ACCESS Florida system, the applicant selects (or is assigned) a managed care plan, and that plan takes over authorizing and coordinating the actual services.

This is also the point where a case manager gets assigned. For a family like Art's, the case manager becomes the point person: the one who helps build a plan of care, decides how many hours of personal care make sense, and adjusts services as needs change over time.

Step Four: What Services Actually Show Up At Home

Once enrolled, the LTC waiver can cover a genuinely wide range of home and community-based services, including:

  • Personal care and homemaker help with bathing, dressing, meals, and light housekeeping
  • Attendant care and skilled or intermittent nursing
  • Adult day health care and adult companion services
  • Respite care to give family caregivers a break
  • Home delivered meals
  • Personal emergency response systems (medical alert devices)
  • Home accessibility adaptations, such as ramps or grab bars
  • Physical, occupational, speech, and respiratory therapy
  • Medical equipment, supplies, and medication management
  • Case management and caregiver training

For Art, the realistic scenario is a combination of personal care and homemaker hours to fill the gaps his daughter can't always cover, a personal emergency response system for the hours he is alone, and possibly home accessibility work so a walker or wheelchair can move safely through the house he built decades ago.

The Participant-Directed Option: Can Art's Daughter Get Paid?

Many families ask whether a daughter or son who is already providing care can be paid through Medicaid instead of a stranger showing up at the door. Florida's answer is the Participant-Directed Option (PDO). Under PDO, the enrollee (or a representative) essentially becomes the employer, choosing and directing their own workers rather than accepting only agency-assigned staff.

Florida does not require a special license or certification to provide personal care, homemaker, or adult companion services under this option, though attendant care does require licensure. Family members, including legally responsible individuals in some circumstances, can be hired as paid caregivers as long as they meet the plan's qualifications, sign a PDO work agreement, and pass required background checks. The managed care plan sets the pay rate and handles payment, not the family directly.

For a family like Art's, this can mean his daughter's daily four hours of hands-on help is no longer entirely unpaid and invisible. It also means the plan of care can be built around the caregiving relationship that already exists, rather than replacing it with an unfamiliar aide.

Combining Medicaid Waiver Services With VA Benefits

Because Art is a veteran, his family should also look at whether VA benefits, such as Aid and Attendance, can run alongside the Medicaid waiver. In many cases these programs can work in a coordinated way, with VA benefits covering costs the Medicaid waiver does not, or filling gaps while someone waits for a waiver opening. The specifics depend on the veteran's service history, discharge status, and current VA enrollment, so this is an area where I always recommend a conversation with both a VA-accredited representative and an elder law attorney familiar with how the two systems interact in Florida.

⚠ Set expectations on timing. Because the LTC waiver has a waitlist and release depends on statewide priority and available openings, families should not assume services will start quickly. Planning early, and lining up other supports (VA benefits, private respite care, family help) while waiting, is realistic and often necessary.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Art have to go on Medicaid to get any help at all?
No. Families can pay privately for home care or use VA benefits while a Medicaid application and ADRC screening are in process; many families do both at once.
How long is the wait for the Long-Term Care waiver in Florida?
There is no fixed timeline. Waitlist release depends on the person's priority score and when the state has an available enrollment slot, not simply how long the name has been on the list.
Can my father stay in his own house the whole time, or does he need to move in with a family member?
Under the Participant-Directed Option and standard LTC waiver services, the enrollee can remain in their own home; family caregiving arrangements are built around that home, not around requiring a move.
If my sister already helps our father for free, can she become a paid caregiver through Medicaid?
Often yes, through Florida's Participant-Directed Option, as long as she meets the plan's qualifications, signs a required work agreement, and passes background checks; the managed care plan, not the family, sets pay and handles payment.
Does applying for the waiver affect eligibility for nursing home Medicaid later?
The financial eligibility rules are largely the same program, so applying for the waiver does not create a separate five-year lookback; however, every family's asset and income picture should be reviewed individually before applying.
What if my parent's condition gets worse while on the waitlist?
Families should contact the ADRC to report a change in condition or loss of caregiver support, since this can affect the priority score and potentially move the person up the list.

The Truestead Takeaway

For a family like Art's, the path to staying at home runs through a real but imperfect system: an ADRC screening call, a priority score, a waitlist with no fixed timeline, and eventually enrollment in a managed care plan that can bring personal care, respite, home modifications, and even paid family caregiving through the Participant-Directed Option right into the house he built. It is not instant, and it is not guaranteed on any particular schedule, which is exactly why I encourage families to start the ADRC screening early, document the caregiver's hours and strain honestly, and look at VA benefits and private options as a bridge while waiting. Every family's income, assets, and care needs are different, so before applying it is worth sitting down with a Florida elder law attorney to make sure the whole plan, Medicaid and VA benefits together, fits the specific situation.
