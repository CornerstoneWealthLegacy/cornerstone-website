The Online Lady Bird Deed That Failed: A Florida Case Study

Quick Answer

A generic online lady bird deed form built for another state can fail in Florida because it omits a required witness, uses the wrong legal description, or leaves out enhanced life estate language, and the family may not find out until after the parent has died.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 25, 2026

Scene One: The Download

The Kowalski family is a composite I am using to illustrate a pattern I see often in my practice, not an actual client. Their story is typical enough that many Florida families will recognize pieces of it.

Stanley Kowalski, a retired mechanic in Spring Hill, wanted his house to go straight to his three adult children without probate. He had heard the term lady bird deed from a neighbor and searched online. He found a fill-in-the-blank form for $29, marketed as usable in "all 50 states." It was not written for Florida.

The form had a single signature line for one witness. Florida has required two subscribing witnesses on a deed for as long as anyone has practiced real estate law here, under section 689.01, Florida Statutes. The template also asked only for the property's street address, not its legal description, and it used life estate language patterned after Texas practice rather than Florida's enhanced life estate structure, the feature that lets an owner keep the right to sell or mortgage the home during life. Stanley signed it at his kitchen table with only his neighbor watching, and a notary stopped by later that week to notarize it.

Scene Two: The Recording

Stanley's daughter, Diane, drove the deed to the Hernando County Clerk of Court to have it recorded, which is a required step; a lady bird deed that is signed but never recorded generally is not effective to pass the property outside probate. The clerk's office is required to check the deed for certain formalities before accepting it, including a legible preparer's name and address and, since a rule change effective January 1, 2024, a printed post office address beneath each witness's signature under section 695.26, Florida Statutes.

⚠ What Went Wrong The deed had only one witness, no address block for that witness, and a street address instead of a legal description matching the recorded deed already on file for the property. The clerk's office rejected it and returned it to Diane with a short list of defects.

This is actually the better of two possible outcomes. A rejected deed tells a family something is wrong while there is still time to fix it. A deed that slips past the counter and gets recorded despite hidden defects can sit quietly for years, telling no one anything is wrong until it is too late to correct.

Scene Three: The Death

Stanley meant to take the corrected deed back to the clerk. He did not get to it. He passed away a little over a year later, and the family found the rejected paperwork in a drawer along with the notary's business card.

Because the deed was never validly recorded, it did the family no good at all. Florida does not have a transfer-on-death deed for real estate the way some other states do, so there was no backup mechanism waiting in reserve. The house was still titled solely in Stanley's name at the moment he died, exactly as if he had never tried to plan for it.

There was a second problem the family did not discover until later. Stanley's form also had no contingent beneficiary listed, only his three children as primary remaindermen with no instruction for what should happen if one of them predeceased him. Had the deed actually been recorded, that gap could have created its own dispute among the siblings or their heirs down the road.

Scene Four: The Probate Nobody Expected

Because no valid deed ever transferred the house, the property became part of Stanley's probate estate. His children had to open a probate case in Hernando County to have the court determine who inherited the home and to clear title before they could sell it or refinance it. This is the exact outcome a lady bird deed is designed to avoid, and it came with real costs: court filing fees, the time of a personal representative, and months of delay before the house could be sold.

Florida's homestead rules add another layer worth understanding. A homestead owned by a married person cannot be conveyed or devised away from a surviving spouse without that spouse joining in the deed, regardless of whose name is on the title. Stanley was widowed, so that issue did not arise for his family, but it is one of the most common reasons a DIY deed fails for married Florida homeowners, and generic online forms almost never address it at all.

What a Correct Florida Lady Bird Deed Requires

There is no Florida statute that creates the lady bird deed by name. It rests on common law, the Florida Bar's title standards, and decades of accepted practice among title examiners and clerks. That makes precision in drafting even more important, because there is no single statute to fall back on if something is missing.

  • Two subscribing witnesses in addition to the notary, each with a legibly printed name and post office address beneath the signature
  • The full legal description of the property, matching the description on the current deed of record, not just the street address
  • Correct enhanced life estate language that actually reserves the owner's right to sell, mortgage, or revoke during life
  • Spousal joinder where the property is homestead and the owner is married
  • Named contingent beneficiaries in case a primary beneficiary does not survive the owner
  • Recording in the county where the property sits, which is what makes the deed effective against future title claims

Florida does have a curative statute, section 95.231, that can heal certain defects like a missing witness or a defective acknowledgment automatically five years after a deed is recorded, absent fraud or pending litigation. But that protection only applies to a deed that actually gets recorded. It does nothing for a deed rejected at the counter or never brought back, and it does not cure a wrong legal description or a missing spousal signature on homestead property.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did the clerk reject Stanley's lady bird deed?
It had only one witness where Florida law requires two, it lacked the post office address required beneath each witness's signature since a rule change effective January 1, 2024, and it described the property by street address rather than its full legal description.
If the deed had been recorded despite these problems, would it have worked?
Not necessarily. A recorded deed with the wrong legal description or missing required language can still fail to accomplish its purpose, and that kind of defect may not surface until after the owner has died, when it is much harder to fix.
Does Florida have a transfer-on-death deed as a backup?
No. Florida does not recognize a transfer-on-death deed for real estate, so if a lady bird deed is never validly recorded, there is no alternative mechanism and the property passes through probate under the owner's will or Florida's intestacy law.
Can a missing witness ever be fixed after the fact?
Florida's curative statute can treat certain defects, including a missing witness, as cured five years after a deed is recorded, absent fraud or a pending lawsuit, but this only helps deeds that were actually recorded, and it does not fix an incorrect legal description or missing spousal joinder.
What if the homeowner is married?
A Florida homestead owned by a married person generally cannot be conveyed or left to someone else without the spouse joining in the deed, no matter whose name is on the title, so that signature needs to be addressed before recording.
