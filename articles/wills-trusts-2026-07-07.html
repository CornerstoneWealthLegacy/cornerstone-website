Will vs. Revocable Living Trust in Florida: What Every Resident Should Know

Quick Answer

A will and a revocable living trust serve different purposes in a Florida estate plan — and most families benefit from having both. The key difference is whether your assets pass through probate or transfer privately and immediately to your loved ones.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney July 7, 2026

Why Floridians Face a Choice Most Other States Make Simpler

Florida's probate process — governed by F.S. Chapter 732 (for wills) and the Florida Probate Code — is a court-supervised procedure that, even in straightforward cases, typically takes several months and involves filing fees, publication costs, and attorney fees. That reality shapes a lot of the planning decisions I have with clients every week.

At the same time, Florida's Trust Code (F.S. Chapter 736) gives residents a powerful, flexible alternative: the revocable living trust. But a trust is not automatically better than a will for every family. The right answer depends on what you own, where you own it, who your beneficiaries are, and what you want the administration of your estate to feel like for the people you leave behind. Let me walk you through both tools — and then the critical step most people skip.

What a Florida Will Does (and Doesn't Do)

A last will and testament is a legal document that directs where your probate assets go after you die. Under F.S. § 732.502, a Florida will must be in writing, signed by you in the presence of two witnesses, and those witnesses must sign in your presence. Notarization is not required for validity, but a self-proved will — one with a notarized affidavit — speeds up the probate process by eliminating the need to locate witnesses later.

Here is what a will cannot do on its own:

  • Avoid probate. Every asset that passes under your will must go through Florida's court-supervised process before your family receives it.
  • Control assets with beneficiary designations. Life insurance, IRAs, 401(k)s, and payable-on-death accounts pass outside the will entirely — straight to the named beneficiary, regardless of what your will says.
  • Protect a minor beneficiary's inheritance. Unless your will creates a testamentary trust or a guardian of the property is appointed, a minor who inherits outright will have a court-supervised guardianship until they turn 18 — and then receive a lump sum with no strings attached.

A will is still essential, even if you have a trust. It handles assets that were never titled into the trust and, most importantly, it names a guardian for minor children — something a trust cannot do.

What a Revocable Living Trust Does Differently

A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement you create during your lifetime. You transfer assets into the trust, typically serving as your own trustee while you are alive and competent. When you die (or become incapacitated), a successor trustee you named steps in and administers or distributes the assets — without a probate court's involvement.

Under F.S. Chapter 736, a revocable trust can be amended or revoked at any time while you are living and have capacity. That flexibility, combined with privacy (trust administrations are generally not public record the way probate proceedings are), makes this a popular choice for Florida homeowners and retirees.

Key advantages of a properly funded revocable trust:

  • Probate avoidance for assets held in the trust — including real estate, brokerage accounts, and business interests.
  • Immediate access for your family. Your successor trustee can act without waiting for court approval.
  • Incapacity planning. If you become unable to manage your finances, your successor trustee takes over seamlessly — no court-ordered guardianship of your property is needed.
  • Multi-state property. If you own real estate in Florida and another state, a trust can help you avoid two separate probate proceedings.
  • Privacy. The terms of your trust generally do not become part of the public record.
In My Practice: I find that Florida homeowners who own property in more than one state, have blended families, own a small business, or simply value privacy and speed for their loved ones tend to benefit most from a revocable trust as the centerpiece of their plan.

The Pour-Over Will: The Essential Partner to Your Trust

If you create a revocable living trust, you still need a will — specifically a pour-over will. This document acts as a safety net: any asset you forgot to transfer into your trust, or that comes into your estate unexpectedly (say, a personal injury settlement or an inheritance), is "poured over" into the trust at your death to be distributed according to the trust's terms.

Without a pour-over will, those assets would pass under Florida's intestacy laws — meaning the state's default inheritance rules, not your wishes, control the outcome. A pour-over will also allows you to name a personal representative (Florida's term for executor) and, critically, to name a guardian for your minor children.

⚠ Important: A pour-over will does not eliminate probate for the assets it captures — those still go through the court process before landing in the trust. That is why funding the trust during your lifetime matters so much.

Trust Funding: The Step Most People Miss

A revocable living trust that has not been funded — meaning assets have not been retitled into it — is essentially an empty shell. I have reviewed estates where families discovered, too late, that their loved one signed a beautiful trust but never transferred a single account or deed into it. The result: full probate anyway.

Funding your trust means taking specific, legal steps for each type of asset:

  • Florida real estate: Recording a new deed transferring the property from your name to the trust. Be aware that Florida's documentary stamp tax (F.S. § 201.02) and homestead rules require careful handling — an attorney should prepare this deed to preserve your homestead exemption (Art. X, § 4, Fla. Const.; F.S. § 196.031) and your Save Our Homes assessment cap (F.S. § 193.155).
  • Bank and brokerage accounts: Retitling the account in the name of the trust, or adding the trust as a payable-on-death beneficiary — whichever the institution permits.
  • Retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s): Generally, you should not name your trust as the primary beneficiary of a retirement account without specific planning, because of the tax implications. Name individuals as primary beneficiaries and coordinate with your attorney on the trust's role as a contingent or special-needs beneficiary.
  • Life insurance: Review your beneficiary designations. These pass outside both your will and your trust unless the trust is specifically named.
  • Vehicles: Florida allows transfer-on-death for motor vehicle titles — your attorney can advise whether retitling or a TOD designation makes more sense for your situation.

Funding is ongoing. Each time you acquire a new asset — open a new account, buy property, receive an inheritance — you need to ask: does this belong in the trust?

Beneficiary Designations: The Overlooked Piece of Every Plan

No matter how carefully drafted your will or trust is, beneficiary designations override them. A life insurance policy payable to an ex-spouse will go to that ex-spouse even if your will and trust name your children. This is one of the most common — and most heartbreaking — mistakes I see in Florida estates.

What I tell every Florida client: treat your beneficiary designations as part of your estate plan, not a separate administrative task. Review them when you create your plan, and revisit them after every major life event — marriage, divorce, birth of a child, death of a named beneficiary.

Florida law does provide some protection in the context of divorce — under certain circumstances, a divorce can automatically revoke a beneficiary designation on some assets — but that rule has limits and does not apply to all asset types. Do not rely on it as a fallback. Update your designations proactively.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a revocable living trust save on estate taxes in Florida?
Florida has no state estate tax, and a basic revocable trust does not, by itself, reduce federal estate taxes. For estates large enough to trigger federal estate tax concerns, more sophisticated planning tools — such as irrevocable trusts — are worth discussing with a Florida estate planning attorney.
Can I be my own trustee of a revocable living trust in Florida?
Yes. Most Florida residents serve as their own trustee during their lifetime, retaining full control over the trust assets. You simply name a successor trustee — a trusted person or a professional corporate trustee — to take over at your death or incapacity.
What happens to my Florida homestead if I transfer it into a revocable trust?
Florida law permits you to hold your homestead in a revocable trust without losing your homestead exemption or your Save Our Homes assessment cap, provided the trust meets certain requirements. The deed must be carefully drafted — this is one area where working with a Florida real estate or estate planning attorney is especially important.
Is a will or a trust more private in Florida?
A trust is generally more private. When a will is admitted to probate, it becomes a public court record. A revocable trust, by contrast, is typically administered outside of court and its terms remain private — your beneficiaries and asset details are not disclosed in a public filing.
How often should I review my Florida will or trust?
A good rule of thumb is to review your plan every three to five years, and immediately after major life events: marriage, divorce, birth or adoption of a child, death of a named beneficiary or trustee, significant change in assets, or a move to or from Florida.
Do I need both a will and a trust in Florida?
For most Florida residents who create a revocable living trust, yes — a pour-over will is still necessary to capture any assets outside the trust at death and, importantly, to name a guardian for minor children. A trust alone cannot appoint a guardian.

The Truestead Takeaway

A will and a revocable living trust are not competing documents — for most Florida families, they work together. The will handles guardianship, captures stray assets, and names your personal representative; the trust holds your titled assets, avoids probate, and protects your family's privacy and time. But a trust that never gets funded is just paper, and beneficiary designations that haven't been reviewed can unravel even the best-drafted plan. What I'd want my own family to understand is this: the document is only the beginning. The real work — and the real protection — comes from keeping your plan coordinated, funded, and current. If you haven't reviewed your Florida estate plan recently, or if you're not sure whether a will or a trust better fits your situation, that conversation with a Florida-licensed estate planning attorney is the most useful next step you can take.
