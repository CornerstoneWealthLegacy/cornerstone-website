Living Trust Preparation in Apollo Beach, Florida: What You Need to Know

Quick Answer

Living trust preparation in Apollo Beach means working with a Florida attorney to draft, sign, and properly fund a revocable trust under Chapter 736 of the Florida Statutes, so your home, accounts, and other assets pass to your family without probate. The trust must be signed with the same formality as a Florida will and then actually retitled into the trust to work.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 11, 2026

What Does Living Trust Preparation in Apollo Beach Actually Involve?

In my practice, I find that most people searching for living trust preparation in Apollo Beach already know they want to avoid probate for their family. What they're less sure about is what the process actually requires. A Florida living trust isn't a form you download, sign, and forget. It's a legal document governed by Chapter 736 of the Florida Statutes (the Florida Trust Code) that has to be drafted correctly, executed correctly, and then funded correctly to do its job.

A properly built living trust names four roles:

  • The grantor (sometimes called the settlor), who creates the trust and typically funds it with their own assets
  • The trustee, who manages the trust assets, usually the grantor themselves during their lifetime
  • The successor trustee, who steps in if the grantor becomes incapacitated or passes away
  • The beneficiaries, who receive the trust assets according to the instructions in the document

For homeowners along the water in Apollo Beach, or anyone with a Hillsborough County homestead, a rental property, or a boat titled in their name, the trust becomes the vehicle that holds those assets so your family can avoid a court-supervised probate process after you pass.

How Does Florida Law Require a Living Trust to Be Signed?

This is the part people most often get wrong when they try to do this themselves. Under Florida Statutes section 736.0403(2)(b), the testamentary provisions of a revocable trust made by a Florida resident are not valid unless the trust is signed with the same formalities required for a Florida will. In practice, that means the grantor must sign the trust in the presence of two witnesses and a notary, just as you would sign a will under Florida Statutes Chapter 732.

I've reviewed trusts brought in by clients who purchased generic online templates, signed them alone at the kitchen table, and assumed they were done. If those documents don't meet Florida's witness and notary requirements, the trust's instructions for what happens at death can be challenged or disregarded entirely, sending the estate right back into probate.

The grantor also has to have the legal capacity to create a trust: at least 18 years old, and of sound mind, meaning they understand the nature of what they're signing. Florida Statutes section 736.0402 sets out this capacity standard, and Florida courts have long looked closely at mental competence when a trust is later contested.

Why Funding the Trust Is the Step Most People Miss

Here's what I tell every Apollo Beach client, without exception: signing the trust is only half the job. A trust only controls the assets that have actually been retitled into its name. If your home, bank accounts, or investment portfolio are still titled in your individual name when you pass away, the trust has no legal effect on them, even if it was drafted and signed perfectly.

⚠ The Most Common Mistake A living trust that was properly drafted and signed but never used to retitle the family home, the bank accounts, or the brokerage account controls none of those assets at death. They pass through probate exactly as if the trust never existed, because the trust was never funded.

Funding typically involves recording a new deed transferring your homestead into the trust, retitling bank and brokerage accounts, and updating beneficiary designations where appropriate. This is not something the statute does automatically for you. It takes affirmative paperwork, asset by asset.

What Happens to My Apollo Beach Homestead If I Put It in a Trust?

Homeowners in Apollo Beach often worry that transferring their homestead into a revocable living trust will trigger a tax reassessment or cost them their homestead exemption. Under Florida law, that's generally not the case. Florida Statutes section 196.041 treats a home held in a properly drafted revocable trust as still being the resident's own equitable property for homestead purposes, so long as the trust reserves the grantor's beneficial interest in the home for their lifetime.

That means your Save Our Homes assessment cap and your homestead exemption under Article VII, Section 6 of the Florida Constitution generally continue uninterrupted after the transfer, as long as the trust is drafted with the right language. This is exactly the kind of detail that should be confirmed by an attorney reviewing your specific trust document and deed, not assumed.

What Changed Under Florida's 2025 Trust Law Update?

Florida made meaningful updates to the Trust Code effective in 2025 through Senate Bill 262. Among other things, the update expanded the authority of what the statute calls "authorized trustees" under Florida Statutes section 736.04117, giving them broader flexibility to modify an existing irrevocable trust or move assets into a new trust under certain conditions, a process sometimes called decanting.

For most people preparing a straightforward revocable living trust in Apollo Beach for the first time, this change matters less directly. But it's a good reminder that trust law is not static. If you have an older Florida trust, particularly an irrevocable one, or a trust that names a corporate or professional trustee, it's worth having it reviewed to see whether these newer provisions offer more flexibility than the original document assumed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a Florida attorney to prepare a living trust, or can I use an online template?
Florida requires specific witness and notary formalities for a revocable trust's testamentary provisions to be valid, and the trust must also be properly funded to work. Many self-prepared or template trusts fail on one or both requirements, so most Florida residents work with an attorney to get both the signing and funding done correctly.
Will putting my Apollo Beach home in a living trust affect my homestead exemption?
Generally no, as long as the trust is drafted to reserve your beneficial interest in the home for your lifetime, Florida law treats the home as still being yours for homestead exemption and Save Our Homes purposes. The specific trust language should be reviewed by an attorney to confirm it qualifies.
What's the difference between a living trust and a will in Florida?
A will, governed by Florida Statutes Chapter 732, only takes effect after death and must go through probate. A properly funded revocable living trust, governed by Chapter 736, can hold and manage assets during your lifetime and typically allows your family to avoid probate on those assets after you pass.
Can I still sell or change assets after they're in my living trust?
Yes. A revocable living trust is presumed revocable under Florida law unless the document says otherwise, and as trustee, you generally keep full control to buy, sell, or manage trust assets during your lifetime, the same as before.
Does a small estate still need a living trust, or can it just go through probate?
Florida offers a simplified process called summary administration when the estate (minus exempt property) is valued at $75,000 or less, or when two years have passed since death. Whether a trust makes sense depends on your specific assets and goals and should be discussed with a Florida attorney.
What happened with Florida's trust laws in 2025?
Senate Bill 262 updated the Florida Trust Code effective in 2025, including expanding the authority of certain trustees to modify or transfer assets from an existing irrevocable trust into a new one under Florida Statutes section 736.04117. This mainly affects existing irrevocable trusts rather than someone preparing a new revocable trust for the first time.

The Truestead Takeaway

Living trust preparation in Apollo Beach isn't complicated once you understand the two things that actually make it work: signing the document with the formality Florida law requires, and then actually funding it by retitling your home, accounts, and other assets into the trust's name. Skip either step and the trust may not protect your family the way you intended. If you're considering a living trust, or you already have one and aren't sure it was ever properly funded, it's worth having a Florida estate planning attorney review your full plan, including your will, power of attorney, and healthcare documents, to make sure everything works together the way you expect.
