Young Adult Legal Forms Every Florida 18-Year-Old Should Have

Quick Answer

In Florida, once your child turns 18, you lose automatic legal authority over their finances and medical care — so "young adult legal forms" refers to a small set of documents (a durable power of attorney, a health care surrogate designation, a living will, and a HIPAA authorization, plus sometimes a simple will) that let a trusted person step in for them in an emergency.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 26, 2026

What Are "Young Adult Legal Forms," Exactly?

In my practice, I hear a version of the same surprised question from parents every August, right around the time their child leaves for college or moves into their first apartment: "Wait, I can't just call the hospital and find out what's going on?" Once your son or daughter turns 18, the answer under Florida law is no — not automatically. They are a legal adult, and you no longer have inherent authority to make medical or financial decisions for them, even if you're still paying their bills.

"Young adult legal forms" is the shorthand people search for online, and it refers to a compact set of documents that restore some of that safety net — voluntarily, on the young adult's own terms. The core forms are:

  • A Durable Power of Attorney for financial and legal matters
  • A Designation of Health Care Surrogate for medical decision-making
  • A Living Will addressing end-of-life care
  • A HIPAA Authorization releasing medical information to named people
  • In some cases, a simple Last Will and Testament if the young adult has income, a car, a business, or other assets in their own name

None of these documents take away the young adult's independence. They simply name someone the young adult trusts — usually a parent, but it could be a sibling or spouse — to step in if the young adult can't speak or act for themselves.

Why This Matters More at 18 Than People Expect

The scenario I walk families through is a simple one: an 18- or 19-year-old is in a car accident, or comes down with a serious illness while away at school, and is unconscious or sedated. Without a signed Designation of Health Care Surrogate, hospital staff may not be able to discuss the patient's condition with the parents at all, even though the parents are the ones on the phone asking what happened and the ones who will be driving to the hospital.

Without a Health Care Surrogate designation or living will in place, a family sometimes has no choice but to petition a Florida court for guardianship in order to get legal authority to make medical decisions — a process that takes time, costs money, and unfolds during what is already a frightening moment. A properly executed health care surrogate designation is generally intended to avoid that court process entirely.

The financial side works the same way. If a young adult is hospitalized, traveling abroad, or simply unreachable, a parent with no power of attorney generally cannot access their bank account, deal with their landlord, manage student loan paperwork, or handle a lease — even to help. A Durable Power of Attorney solves that by naming an agent in advance.

Why "durable" matters: A durable power of attorney remains valid even if the young adult later becomes incapacitated — which is precisely the scenario the document is meant to cover. Florida's power of attorney statute, F.S. Chapter 709, also requires that certain powers (like the authority to make gifts or change beneficiary designations) be separately initialed by the signer to be effective, so a generic downloaded form that skips this step may not actually grant full authority when it's needed.

The Core Documents, One at a Time

Durable Power of Attorney. This document lets the young adult name an agent to manage financial and legal affairs — banking, tuition payments, tax filings, dealing with a landlord, or applying for benefits — if they become unable to handle these matters themselves. Florida law under Chapter 709 sets specific formalities for execution, including that certain enumerated powers must be individually initialed to be valid.

Designation of Health Care Surrogate. Governed by F.S. § 765.202, this document names someone to make medical decisions on the young adult's behalf if they're unable to communicate their wishes. It's the medical counterpart to the power of attorney — one covers money and legal matters, the other covers health care, and Florida treats them as separate documents with separate signing formalities.

Living Will. Under F.S. § 765.301, a living will lets a person state their wishes about life-prolonging treatment in advance, in case of a terminal condition, end-stage condition, or persistent vegetative state. It's a difficult document to think about at 18, but it takes the guesswork — and potential family disagreement — out of an already painful situation.

HIPAA Authorization. Federal medical privacy law generally prevents providers from sharing a patient's health information with anyone, including parents, unless the patient has authorized it. A signed HIPAA release names who can receive updates and records, which is often what a family needs most urgently in the first hours of a crisis, even before decision-making authority comes into play.

Last Will and Testament. Once a young adult has a car, a bank account, a business, valuable personal property, or life insurance, a basic will (governed by Florida Statutes Chapter 732, with execution requirements under F.S. § 732.502) lets them decide who receives those assets, rather than leaving it to Florida's intestacy laws.

Signing Requirements Florida Families Often Get Wrong

Florida is particular about how these documents must be executed, and this is where I see the most costly mistakes from generic online templates. Durable powers of attorney and wills generally must be signed in the presence of two witnesses, and health care documents typically require witnessing or notarization as well, depending on the document. A power of attorney that omits the specific initialing required by F.S. Chapter 709 for enumerated powers, or a will that's missing a witness signature, can be rejected by a bank, hospital, or court at exactly the moment it's needed most.

⚠ A Word of Caution: A single missing signature, an un-initialed power, or an improperly witnessed page can render a young adult legal form ineffective. These documents are inexpensive and quick to execute correctly the first time — but very hard to fix during an actual emergency.

This is also why I generally caution families away from relying solely on free online forms found through a search engine. They aren't wrong to want something simple and affordable — that instinct is right — but a form that hasn't been checked against Florida's current execution requirements can create a false sense of security.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my 18-year-old really need these forms if they're healthy and just going to college?
Most young adults are healthy, but these documents exist for the unexpected — a car accident, a sudden illness, or an injury while away from home. Because they only matter in an emergency, the practical approach is to sign them once, before they're needed, rather than waiting.
Can I still make medical decisions for my child once they turn 18 without any paperwork?
Generally, no. Once a person turns 18 in Florida, they are a legal adult, and parents lose automatic authority over their medical and financial decisions unless the young adult has signed documents naming them as an agent or surrogate.
What's the difference between a power of attorney and a health care surrogate?
A durable power of attorney covers financial and legal matters, while a health care surrogate designation covers medical decision-making. Florida treats them as two separate documents with their own signing requirements, so both are typically needed for full coverage.
Do these documents need to be notarized?
Florida generally requires witnesses for these documents, and notarization is required or commonly used for certain health care directives and powers of attorney. Because the exact formalities depend on the specific document, it's worth having them reviewed for proper execution.
Does my young adult need a full will, or just the incapacity documents?
If your young adult has income, a vehicle, a bank account, or other assets titled in their own name, a simple will can be useful. If they own little to nothing yet, many families start with just the power of attorney, health care surrogate, living will, and HIPAA release, and add a will later as circumstances change.
I heard Florida might let 17-year-olds sign certain contracts starting in 2026 — is that true?
There have been discussions about possible changes to contracting rules for minors, but as of this writing that has not been confirmed as enacted Florida law. Readers should verify the current status of any such legislation with a Florida attorney before relying on it.

The Truestead Takeaway

Turning 18 is a legal milestone that quietly changes who can act for your child in a crisis — and the fix is a short list of inexpensive, properly executed documents, not a complicated estate plan. What I tell Florida families is to treat these forms the same way they treat a car seat or a first apartment lease: routine preparation, not a sign that something bad is expected to happen. If your young adult doesn't yet have a durable power of attorney, health care surrogate designation, living will, and HIPAA authorization on file, it's worth having a Florida attorney review their situation and put proper, correctly executed documents in place.
