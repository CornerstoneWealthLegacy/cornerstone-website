Florida Wills and Trusts: The Trust You Never Funded Doesn't Work

Quick Answer

A revocable living trust only avoids probate for the assets you actually retitle into it during your lifetime. In Florida, a properly drafted pour-over will backs up the trust for anything left out, but the trust must already exist when you sign your will for that safety net to work.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 5, 2026

Why a trust alone doesn't finish the job

In my practice, I meet a lot of Florida clients who signed a revocable living trust years ago, filed the paperwork away, and assumed their estate plan was complete. It usually isn't, and it's the single most common estate planning failure I see.

A revocable living trust is governed in Florida by Chapter 736 of the Florida Statutes, known as the Florida Trust Code, and F.S. § 736.0403 sets out what makes a trust valid. But creating the trust document is only step one. The trust has to be funded, meaning your home, bank accounts, investment accounts, and other property need to actually be retitled into the name of the trust. If an asset is still titled in your individual name when you pass away, that asset goes through probate anyway, whether or not you have a trust sitting in a drawer.

Why this matters: A trust does three things a will alone cannot: it avoids probate for funded assets, it keeps your financial affairs private (probate files are public record), and it provides a plan for managing your property if you become incapacitated, not just after you die.

What funding your trust actually looks like

Funding is simply the process of changing how your assets are titled or naming your trust as beneficiary. In my experience, this is where clients need the most hand-holding, because each asset type is handled a little differently.

  • Real estate: Your home, vacation property, or rental property should be transferred into your trust by recording a new deed with the clerk of court in the county where the property sits. Florida law addresses the requirements for these deed transfers, and when the grantor and the trustee are the same person, documentary stamp tax is typically minimal or exempt under F.S. § 201.02.
  • Bank and investment accounts: These are generally retitled directly into the trust's name through your bank or brokerage.
  • Life insurance and retirement accounts: These pass by beneficiary designation, not by title, so funding here usually means reviewing (and sometimes updating) who is named as beneficiary rather than retitling the account itself.

Florida homestead property deserves special attention when it comes to trust funding, since Florida's constitutional homestead protections and creditor exemptions interact with how (and whether) you title your primary residence. This is exactly the kind of detail that should be reviewed with a Florida attorney rather than handled generically.

The pour-over will: your trust's safety net

Because almost no one manages to retitle absolutely everything before they die, Florida law provides a backstop called the pour-over will. This is explicitly addressed in F.S. § 732.513. A pour-over will is a regular Florida will, meeting all the same execution requirements as any other will (in writing, signed by the testator, and witnessed by two individuals under F.S. § 732.502), except that instead of naming individual people as beneficiaries, it names your trust as the beneficiary of whatever assets were left out.

Florida law gives this arrangement real flexibility. Under F.S. § 732.513(2)(b), a devise to your trust through a pour-over will isn't invalidated just because you later amended the trust, so you can update your trust's distribution plan after signing your will and those changes flow through automatically. And under F.S. § 732.513(2)(c), a pour-over transfer to a trust that wasn't yet funded is still valid, as long as the trust exists for the purpose of receiving that transfer.

⚠ Timing matters: Under F.S. § 732.513(1), a pour-over will cannot validly transfer assets into a trust created after the will was signed. The trust needs to be created before, or at the same time as, your will. This is a drafting detail your attorney should confirm is done correctly, since getting the order wrong can undermine the whole plan.

Probate still touches pour-over assets

One distinction I make sure every client understands: assets that pass through a pour-over will still go through probate before they land in the trust. The will has to be admitted, and the probate process has to run its course, and only then does the property pour into the trust for distribution under its terms. This is different from assets that were already properly titled in the trust during your lifetime, which bypass probate entirely.

This is why I tell Florida clients that the pour-over will is meant to be a safety net, not a primary distribution plan. If most of your estate ends up flowing through the pour-over will rather than already sitting in the trust, you haven't really avoided probate, you've just added an extra layer of paperwork. I also recommend a self-proving affidavit under F.S. § 732.503 for any Florida will, including a pour-over will, since it allows the will to be admitted to probate without requiring your witnesses to appear in court later.

A note on electronic wills

Clients occasionally ask whether they can sign estate planning documents electronically, especially after growing comfortable with e-signatures for other purposes. As of this writing, traditional Florida will formalities generally do not accept electronic signatures the way some other document types do, though remote online notarization workflows exist for certain authorized circumstances. Because this is an evolving and technical area, I would not rely on assumptions here. It should be discussed directly with a Florida attorney before you sign anything electronically.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need both a will and a trust in Florida?
Most Floridians with a revocable living trust also need a pour-over will, since the will catches any assets that were never retitled into the trust and directs them there through probate.
What happens if I forget to fund my trust before I die?
Any asset still titled in your individual name typically has to go through Florida probate, and a pour-over will can direct it into your trust afterward, but this adds time and cost your family could have avoided if the asset had been funded during your lifetime.
Does putting my Florida homestead into a trust affect its protections?
Florida's constitutional homestead exemption and creditor protections involve specific rules that interact with trust ownership, so this should always be reviewed individually with a Florida attorney rather than assumed.
Can I change my trust after I've already signed my pour-over will?
Yes. Florida law specifically allows you to amend your trust after your will is signed, and those changes are incorporated automatically because the will simply directs assets into whatever the trust says at your death.
Is a handwritten or emailed will valid in Florida?
Florida has strict execution requirements for wills, including two witnesses signing in the testator's presence, and electronic signatures generally aren't accepted under traditional formalities, so any nonstandard document should be reviewed by a Florida attorney before you rely on it.

The Truestead Takeaway

A revocable living trust is one of the most useful tools available to Florida families, but the trust document itself is only half the plan. The other half is the unglamorous work of actually retitling your home, your accounts, and your other property into the trust's name, backed up by a properly drafted pour-over will for anything left over. If you signed a trust years ago and aren't certain everything was retitled, that's worth a review. I'd encourage you to sit down with a Florida attorney, bring your deed and account statements, and confirm your plan actually does what you think it does.
