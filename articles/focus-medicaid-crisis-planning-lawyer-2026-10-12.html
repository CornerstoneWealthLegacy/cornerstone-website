Medicaid Crisis Planning Lawyer: What Florida Families Need to Know in 2026

Quick Answer

A Medicaid crisis planning lawyer helps a Florida family legally restructure a loved one's assets, often within weeks, so they can qualify for nursing home Medicaid even though no advance planning was done. It is not too late just because a crisis has already started.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney October 12, 2026

What Does a Medicaid Crisis Planning Lawyer Actually Do?

In my practice, the phone call usually sounds like this: "Mom fell, she's in rehab, the nursing home says private pay is about $13,000 a month, and we have no idea how we're going to afford this." That is a Medicaid crisis, and it is exactly what a Medicaid crisis planning lawyer is trained to handle.

Crisis planning is different from the kind of planning we do for clients years ahead of time. There is no five-year runway here. The family needs a legal strategy now, using tools that are still available even after the need for care has already arrived. A Florida elder law attorney who handles crisis cases will typically:

  • Review all of the applicant's assets, income, and any transfers made in the last five years
  • Identify which assets are "countable" toward the $2,000 Medicaid asset limit and which are exempt
  • Use legal planning tools, such as annuities, personal services contracts, or asset restructuring, to convert countable assets into exempt or protected ones
  • Prepare a Qualified Income Trust (QIT) if the applicant's income exceeds Florida's income cap
  • Handle the actual Medicaid application and respond to the Department of Children and Families (DCF)

Florida's Supreme Court has made clear that this kind of legal advice, specifically advising a family on how to restructure assets to qualify for Medicaid, can only be given by a licensed attorney. Non-lawyers who provide this advice can face criminal penalties under Florida Statute 454.23. That is one reason families searching for a "Medicaid crisis planning lawyer" should be cautious of facilities or placement services offering to do this work for free.

Florida's 2026 Medicaid Numbers You Need to Know

Florida Medicaid's financial eligibility rules are updated each year, and the 2026 figures matter a great deal in crisis cases because they define exactly how much room a family has to work with.

  • Countable asset limit: A single Medicaid applicant can keep only $2,000 in countable assets.
  • Community Spouse Resource Allowance: As of 2026, the healthy spouse at home (the "community spouse") can generally keep up to $162,660, on top of the applicant's $2,000.
  • Income cap: As of January 1, 2026, if the applicant's gross monthly income exceeds $2,982, a Qualified Income Trust (Miller Trust) is required to still qualify.
  • Home equity limit: As of January 1, 2026, a single applicant can have up to $752,000 in home equity and still potentially qualify, as long as the home remains exempt under Medicaid's homestead rules.
  • Penalty divisor: As of January 1, 2026, Florida's penalty divisor is $10,645. This number is used to calculate how many months of ineligibility result from gifts or transfers made during the five-year look-back period.

These numbers change the math every year, which is exactly why crisis planning strategies need to be built around the current figures, not last year's.

The Five-Year Look-Back: Why It Doesn't Always Mean You're Stuck

Under Florida Statute 409.9101 and the corresponding federal rule (42 U.S.C. § 1396p(c)(1)), Medicaid looks back 60 months from the application date to see whether the applicant gave away assets or sold them for less than fair market value. If so, Medicaid calculates a penalty period by dividing the value of the transfer by Florida's current penalty divisor ($10,645 as of 2026).

A lot of families assume that once a gift or transfer has happened, there is nothing left to do. That is often not true. In my experience, a crisis planning attorney can frequently reduce or reposition a family's exposure even after transfers have already occurred, through strategies such as converting remaining countable assets into an income stream, using a properly structured promissory note or personal services contract, or coordinating the timing of the application itself. Every case is different, and these strategies depend heavily on the applicant's specific facts, so this is genuinely not a do-it-yourself project.

Why timing matters: Crisis planning often has to happen within days or weeks, while someone is in rehab or a skilled nursing facility, because Medicaid coverage and private-pay bills don't wait for a family to get organized.

What's Changing for Florida Medicaid Recipients in Late 2026

Starting in December 2026, certain categories of Florida Medicaid enrollees will move from annual renewals to 6-month renewal cycles. In practical terms, this means some families will need to re-verify a loved one's continued eligibility twice a year instead of once. If your family member is approved for nursing home Medicaid this year, it's worth building a habit of gathering updated bank statements, income documentation, and asset records every six months so the renewal process goes smoothly and coverage isn't interrupted.

⚠ A Word of Caution: Florida remains one of roughly ten states that has not adopted Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act. This primarily affects general adult Medicaid eligibility categories, not long-term care Medicaid for seniors needing nursing home or home and community-based services, but it's a reminder that Florida's Medicaid rules can differ meaningfully from what you may read in national articles written for other states.

Crisis Planning vs. Advance Planning: Which Do You Need?

If a loved one is already in the hospital, in rehab, or in a nursing facility and the family is worried about the bill, that is a crisis planning situation, and time matters. If, on the other hand, you are reading this because you want to protect a parent's or your own assets years before care is ever needed, that is proactive, or pre-planning, and it opens up a wider range of strategies, often including irrevocable trusts and gifting strategies that simply aren't available once a crisis has already started.

Both paths lead to the same overall goal: getting quality long-term care covered by Medicaid without losing everything a family has worked for. The strategies and the timeline, however, look very different, which is why the first step in either case is an honest conversation with a Florida elder law attorney about where your family currently stands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it too late to do Medicaid planning if my parent is already in a nursing home?
No. This is precisely what Medicaid crisis planning is for. Even after someone is already receiving care and paying privately, a Florida elder law attorney can often still implement legal strategies to help the family qualify for Medicaid, though the available options are typically narrower than if planning had been done years in advance.
How much can my spouse keep if I need nursing home Medicaid in Florida?
As of 2026, the healthy spouse (the community spouse) can generally keep up to $162,660 in countable assets, in addition to the Medicaid applicant's own $2,000 asset allowance, under Florida's Community Spouse Resource Allowance rules.
What is a Qualified Income Trust and do I need one?
A Qualified Income Trust, also called a Miller Trust, is a legal tool used when an applicant's monthly income exceeds Florida's Medicaid income cap, which is $2,982 as of January 1, 2026. Excess income is directed into the trust, which allows the applicant to still qualify for Medicaid despite having income above the cap.
Will Medicaid take my parent's house?
Not automatically. A primary residence is generally exempt from the countable asset limit up to Florida's home equity cap, which is $752,000 as of January 1, 2026. However, Medicaid estate recovery rules may apply after the recipient passes away, so this should be reviewed with an elder law attorney as part of the overall plan.
Can a nursing home or placement agency help me with Medicaid planning instead of hiring a lawyer?
They can help with general information and applications, but under Florida law, only a licensed attorney can advise a family on legal strategies to restructure assets for Medicaid eligibility. Non-attorneys who give this specific legal advice can face criminal penalties under Florida Statute 454.23.
How far back does Florida Medicaid look at my parent's financial transactions?
Florida Medicaid applies a 60-month (five-year) look-back period on asset transfers under Florida Statute 409.9101. Gifts or transfers made for less than fair value during that window can create a period of Medicaid ineligibility, calculated using the current penalty divisor.

The Truestead Takeaway

If your family is facing a sudden long-term care need and feels like it's too late to protect any savings, take a breath. Florida law still offers real, legal tools to help a loved one qualify for nursing home Medicaid even after a crisis has already begun, but the right strategy depends entirely on your family's specific assets, income, and timeline, and the 2026 dollar figures that apply to your case. The sensible next step is a direct conversation with a Florida elder law attorney who handles crisis cases regularly, before more private-pay bills come due.
