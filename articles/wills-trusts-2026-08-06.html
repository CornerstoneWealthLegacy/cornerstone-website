Wills vs. Trusts in Florida: Why Funding Is the Part Everyone Forgets

Quick Answer

A revocable living trust avoids probate only for assets actually retitled into the trust's name; a pour-over will exists as a backup to catch anything left out, but those leftover assets still go through probate first. A 2026 change to Florida law raised the threshold for the simpler summary probate process to $150,000, which matters if your trust funding isn't complete.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 6, 2026

Why "I Have a Trust" Doesn't Always Mean "I Avoid Probate"

In my practice, I meet a lot of Floridians who signed a revocable living trust years ago and assume their family is fully protected from probate. Often, they're half right. Under Florida's Trust Code (Chapter 736), a revocable trust is a valid and powerful planning tool that you can amend or revoke during your lifetime. But a trust document by itself doesn't move anything — it's just an empty container until you actually put assets into it. Only property that is titled in the name of the trust avoids the probate court process when you pass away. If your house, bank accounts, or investment accounts are still titled in your individual name, they generally have to go through probate regardless of how well-drafted your trust is.

What It Actually Takes to "Fund" a Florida Trust

Funding is the unglamorous, paperwork-heavy step that makes a trust work — and it's the step people most often skip. What I tell Florida clients is to think of it as retitling, not just signing:

  • Real estate must be transferred into the trust by recording a new deed with the county clerk's office. This typically means a properly executed deed — often a quitclaim deed under Fla. Stat. § 689.025 — signed before a notary and two witnesses, then recorded in the county where the property sits.
  • Bank and investment accounts need to be retitled directly in the trust's name. Banks and brokerages will usually want a copy of the trust (or a certification of trust) and documentation showing who the trustee is and what authority they have.
  • Other assets — vehicles, business interests, valuable personal property — may need their own transfer or assignment documents depending on the asset type.
A Trust That Owns Nothing Protects No One I've seen families discover, only after a loved one's death, that the trust never actually held the house or the accounts. That family still ends up in probate court — the very outcome the trust was meant to prevent.

This is why I recommend a funding review every few years, especially after you buy or sell real estate, open new accounts, or refinance a mortgage (refinancing sometimes requires temporarily moving the property out of the trust, and it needs to go back in afterward).

The Pour-Over Will: Your Trust's Safety Net

Because funding is rarely 100% perfect — people forget accounts, acquire new assets, or simply run out of time — Florida law provides a backstop called a pour-over will. This is a will, executed with all the same formalities as any other Florida will (in writing, signed by you, witnessed by two people in your presence, per Fla. Stat. § 732.502), that directs any assets left in your individual name at death into your revocable trust.

Florida Statute § 732.513 gives this arrangement real teeth. Under § 732.513(2)(c), a pour-over gift to a trust is valid even if that trust was never separately funded during your lifetime, as long as the trust's purpose is to receive that pour-over transfer. And under § 732.513(2)(b), the devise remains valid even if you amended your trust after signing the will — so you're not stuck re-executing your will every time you update trust provisions.

The catch: assets that pass through a pour-over will still have to go through the probate process before they land in the trust. The pour-over will doesn't avoid probate — it's a safety net that funnels stray assets to the right place after probate, rather than leaving them to pass by Florida's intestacy laws.

A Meaningful 2026 Change: The Summary Administration Threshold Just Doubled

Here's a development worth knowing about if you're relying on a pour-over will as your backup plan. Effective July 1, 2026, Florida raised the threshold for summary administration — the simplified, faster form of probate — to $150,000 in non-exempt probate assets, roughly double the prior limit.

Why this matters: if your revocable trust is mostly funded but a modest account or two got left out, those leftover assets funneling through your pour-over will are now more likely to qualify for the streamlined summary administration process rather than full formal administration. That generally means less time, less cost, and less court involvement for your family to get those remaining assets into the trust where they belong. It's not a substitute for good funding — but it's a meaningful cushion for the small gaps that inevitably show up in even well-maintained plans.

Beneficiary Designations: The Assets That Skip the Trust Entirely

Not everything belongs in a trust, and not everything needs to be. Life insurance policies, retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s), and certain payable-on-death or transfer-on-death accounts pass by beneficiary designation, not by will or trust terms. This is often faster and simpler than probate — but only if the designations are current and coordinated with the rest of your plan.

I've seen otherwise excellent estate plans undermined because a beneficiary form still named an ex-spouse, or listed minor children directly (which can create real complications, since minors generally can't receive funds outright). Reviewing beneficiary designations alongside your will and trust — not in isolation — is one of the simplest, highest-value things a Florida family can do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need both a will and a trust in Florida?
Most Floridians who set up a revocable living trust also sign a pour-over will as a backup, since it's very difficult to retitle every single asset during your lifetime. The trust and will are meant to work together, not as either-or alternatives.
If I have a trust, why would my estate still go through probate?
Probate is typically required for any assets titled in your individual name at death, even if you have a trust. Only assets actually retitled into the trust's name avoid the probate process.
What is a pour-over will and does it avoid probate?
A pour-over will directs leftover individually-owned assets into your trust after death, but those assets still pass through Florida probate first. It's a safety net for what the trust didn't catch, not a probate-avoidance tool on its own.
How does the new $150,000 summary administration threshold help my family?
Effective July 1, 2026, Florida raised the value limit for the simplified summary administration process to $150,000 in non-exempt assets. This can mean a faster, less expensive probate process for smaller leftover assets that end up outside your trust.
How do I actually move my house into my Florida trust?
Real estate is transferred into a trust by recording a new deed with the county clerk in the county where the property is located, properly signed before a notary and two witnesses. This should be handled carefully, since errors in deed preparation can create title problems later.
Do retirement accounts and life insurance need to be put in my trust?
Generally no — these assets pass by beneficiary designation rather than through your will or trust, so keeping those designations current is often more important than retitling the accounts themselves.

The Truestead Takeaway

A revocable living trust is one of the most useful tools in Florida estate planning, but it only works if it's actually funded — meaning your home, accounts, and other assets are properly retitled in the trust's name. A pour-over will and Florida's improved summary administration process (now covering up to $150,000 in assets as of July 2026) provide a helpful cushion for the gaps that show up in real life, but they're not a substitute for a funding review. If it's been more than a couple of years since you looked at your trust, your deed, or your beneficiary forms, it's worth sitting down with a Florida estate planning attorney to make sure the plan you signed is actually the plan that's in place today.
