Florida Just Raised the Summary Probate Threshold to $150,000 — Here's What That Means for Your Family

Quick Answer

As of July 1, 2026, Florida estates worth $150,000 or less (after exempt property) may qualify for the faster, cheaper summary administration process instead of full formal probate — nearly double the old $75,000 limit.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 18, 2026

What Actually Changed on July 1, 2026

In my practice, I've spent a lot of years explaining to Florida families why their loved one's estate had to go through months of formal probate even though, on paper, it didn't feel like a large estate. That conversation just got easier for a lot of people.

For deaths occurring on or after July 1, 2026, Florida law raised the ceiling for summary administration under F.S. § 735.201 from $75,000 to $150,000. This came out of legislation signed in the spring of 2026, and it's the most significant change to Florida's probate process in years.

Here's why it matters: summary administration is a streamlined, lighter-touch version of probate. No personal representative has to be appointed, there's no mandatory published notice to creditors, no full inventory, and no formal final accounting. It's simply faster and less expensive. Doubling the dollar threshold means a much larger share of Florida estates — especially modest homes, small savings accounts, and simple estates — will now qualify.

Summary vs. Formal Administration: What's the Real Difference

Summary administration is available under F.S. § 735.201 when either:

  • The value of the estate subject to administration, not counting exempt property, is $150,000 or less (for deaths on or after July 1, 2026); or
  • The decedent has been dead for more than two years, regardless of estate size.

It generally wraps up in a matter of a couple of months once the paperwork is in order, and the court issues an Order of Summary Administration under F.S. § 735.206 directing how assets are distributed — without ever appointing an executor.

Formal administration is the traditional probate process most people picture. It's required when an estate exceeds the summary threshold, when a personal representative needs authority to manage ongoing business affairs, sell real estate, or defend the estate in litigation, or when creditors need to be formally noticed and time-barred. Formal administration realistically takes a minimum of five to six months, largely because Florida law requires creditors be given a window — generally about three months from first publication of notice — to file claims before the estate can be safely distributed.

One quiet but important detail: an estate that looks like it's worth well over $150,000 on the surface may still qualify for summary administration once you exclude exempt homestead property and non-probate assets — things like jointly titled accounts, payable-on-death designations, or assets held in trust. That's a determination worth having reviewed rather than assumed.

What Florida Probate Actually Costs

Cost is usually the first question families ask me, and the honest answer is: it depends heavily on which path the estate takes.

  • Court filing fees run roughly $402 for formal (or ancillary) administration and about $235 for summary administration in 2026, with small variation by county.
  • Attorney and personal representative fees for formal administration often fall in a commonly cited range of 3% to 7% of the gross estate, though this varies by estate size and complexity.
  • Florida has its own statutory fee guideline — F.S. §§ 733.617 and 733.6171 — which presumes reasonable compensation of about 3% to the personal representative and 3% to the attorney on the first $1 million of an estate, sliding down to smaller percentages on larger amounts above $10 million.
Good to know: That statutory percentage schedule is a presumption, not a mandatory fee. Florida law actually requires attorneys to disclose in writing that their fee is negotiable — it doesn't have to track the statute automatically.

Summary administration, by contrast, typically costs meaningfully less simply because there's less required work: no full inventory, no published notice to creditors, no final accounting filed with the court.

The Personal Representative's Job — and Why It's a Real Legal Duty

If you've been named personal representative (Florida's term for executor) in formal administration, you're not just handling paperwork — you're a fiduciary under F.S. § 733.602. That means you're legally obligated to act in the best interest of the estate and its beneficiaries, not your own.

Typical duties include:

  • Filing the will and a petition for administration in the circuit court for the county where the decedent lived
  • Identifying, securing, and valuing estate assets
  • Publishing and serving notice to creditors, then reviewing and paying (or objecting to) claims
  • Filing an inventory and, eventually, a final accounting with the court
  • Distributing remaining assets to beneficiaries under the will or Florida's intestacy laws

Under F.S. § 733.609, a personal representative's duty is held to the same standard as a trustee managing an express trust — and a personal representative who breaches that duty can be personally liable to beneficiaries for resulting losses. This isn't meant to alarm anyone; in the vast majority of estates, a personal representative who keeps good records, communicates with beneficiaries, and works with an attorney has nothing to worry about. But it's a real legal role, not just a title.

⚠ A Note on Deadlines: Creditor claim periods and other probate deadlines are strict and unforgiving of missed dates. If you've been appointed personal representative, don't guess at timing — confirm the applicable deadlines with a Florida probate attorney early in the process.

Avoiding Probate Altogether

Not every asset has to go through probate — formal or summary — at all. Many of the Florida families I work with are surprised to learn how much can pass outside the court process entirely through proper planning:

  • A properly funded revocable living trust (governed by F.S. Chapter 736) can hold assets that pass to beneficiaries without any probate filing.
  • A Lady Bird deed can allow Florida homestead property to pass directly to a named beneficiary while the owner retains full control during life.
  • Accounts titled with payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations, and jointly owned property with rights of survivorship, generally bypass probate by operation of law.

None of these tools are automatic — they require the deed, account title, or trust to actually be set up and funded correctly during life. That's the piece that gets missed most often, and it's exactly the kind of thing worth reviewing with an attorney rather than assuming is already in place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the new $150,000 threshold apply to estates where the person died before July 1, 2026?
No. The higher $150,000 threshold applies to deaths occurring on or after July 1, 2026. Estates involving earlier deaths are generally evaluated under the prior $75,000 threshold, though the two-years-since-death rule for summary administration still applies regardless of estate size.
How long does Florida probate actually take?
Summary administration commonly wraps up in a couple of months once the petition is filed and in order. Formal administration typically takes a minimum of five to six months, mainly because of Florida's required creditor claim period, and can take considerably longer for complex or contested estates.
Is the Florida statutory attorney fee schedule mandatory?
No. Florida's statutory fee guidelines under F.S. §§ 733.617 and 733.6171 are a presumption of reasonableness, not a fixed requirement, and Florida law requires attorneys to disclose in writing that the fee is negotiable.
Can a house be counted toward the $150,000 summary administration limit?
Florida homestead property is generally treated as exempt property and typically isn't counted toward the summary administration dollar threshold, which is one reason some estates that look larger on paper can still qualify. This determination depends on the specific facts and should be confirmed with an attorney.
Do I need a personal representative for summary administration?
No. One of the defining features of summary administration is that no personal representative is appointed; the court issues an Order of Summary Administration directing distribution of assets instead.
What happens if a personal representative doesn't fulfill their duties properly?
A personal representative is a fiduciary under Florida law and can be personally liable to beneficiaries for damage or loss caused by a breach of that duty, so it's important for anyone serving in this role to keep careful records and get guidance on their legal obligations.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida's move to a $150,000 summary administration threshold is genuinely good news for a lot of families — it means more modest estates can avoid the time and expense of formal probate. But whether an estate actually qualifies, and whether formal administration, summary administration, or no probate at all is the right path, depends on the specific mix of assets, titling, and debts involved. If you're serving as a personal representative, or you're simply thinking ahead about your own estate, the sensible next step is the same either way: have your situation — or your existing plan — reviewed by a Florida probate attorney rather than assuming how the new rules apply.
