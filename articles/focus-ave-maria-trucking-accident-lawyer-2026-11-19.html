Ave Maria Trucking Accident Lawyer: What Florida Law Says About Your Claim

Quick Answer

If you were hurt in a trucking accident in or around Ave Maria, Florida law generally gives you two years from the date of the crash to file a lawsuit, requires prompt medical treatment to protect your no-fault insurance benefits, and allows you to recover damages as long as you are not more than 50% at fault. An Ave Maria trucking accident lawyer's job is to preserve evidence quickly, identify every liable party, and protect those deadlines for you.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Personal Injury Attorney November 19, 2026

Why Ave Maria Trucking Accidents Are Different From a Regular Car Crash

In my practice, I tell clients that a collision with a commercial truck is rarely a two-party problem the way a fender-bender between two sedans usually is. Ave Maria sits along major Collier County corridors used heavily by agricultural trucks, delivery fleets, and freight carriers moving through southwest Florida. When one of these vehicles is involved in a crash, you are often dealing with a web of potentially responsible parties: the driver, the trucking company that employed or contracted with the driver, the company that owns the truck or trailer, a maintenance contractor, and sometimes a freight broker that arranged the shipment.

That last category became especially important in 2026. A unanimous U.S. Supreme Court ruling, Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, confirmed that federal transportation law does not automatically shield freight brokers from being sued when they negligently hire an unsafe carrier. This matters a great deal here, because it means a broker who should have known a carrier had a poor safety record can now be named as a defendant in its own right, not just the driver or the trucking company. An experienced Ave Maria trucking accident lawyer evaluates all of these potential defendants, not just the one whose name is on the police report.

How Long Do I Have to File a Truck Accident Claim in Florida?

Florida's statute of limitations for negligence-based personal injury claims, including trucking accidents, is governed by Florida Statute § 95.11. Since the 2023 tort reform legislation (HB 837), the deadline for injuries occurring on or after March 24, 2023 is two years from the date of the crash, not the four years that applied under the older rule. If your accident happened before that date, a longer four-year window may still apply, but that is a fact-specific question worth confirming with an attorney rather than assuming.

⚠ Don't wait to find out your deadline. Two years sounds like a long time, but trucking cases require early evidence preservation, and waiting even a few months to consult a lawyer can mean losing access to data that disappears on its own. If you are unsure which deadline applies to your crash, have a Florida attorney confirm it in writing before you rely on any date you've read online.

What Happens to My Medical Bills Right After the Crash?

Florida is a no-fault state for auto insurance, which means your own Personal Injury Protection (PIP) coverage pays a portion of your medical bills and lost wages regardless of who caused the crash, generally up to policy limits. The catch that trips up many people is timing: Florida law requires that you seek medical treatment within 14 days of the accident to remain eligible for PIP benefits at all. Miss that window, and you can forfeit that coverage entirely, even if your injuries are serious and clearly connected to the crash.

PIP has limits, and trucking accident injuries are frequently severe enough to exceed them quickly. When that happens, Florida law allows an injured person to pursue a claim against the at-fault parties (the driver, the trucking company, and potentially a broker) for damages beyond what PIP covers, including pain and suffering, once the injury meets the threshold set out in Florida's no-fault statute.

What If I Was Partly at Fault for the Accident?

Florida follows a modified comparative negligence rule under Florida Statute § 768.81. In plain terms, this means you can still recover compensation even if you share some blame for the crash, as long as you are found to be 50% or less at fault. If a jury or insurance adjuster determines you were more than 50% responsible, Florida law bars recovery entirely.

Trucking companies and their insurers understand this rule well, and in my experience they often try to shift blame onto the injured driver early in the process, before an independent investigation of logbooks, black box data, and driver qualification files has even happened. This is one of the biggest reasons people search for a dedicated truck accident lawyer rather than handling a claim alone: the comparative fault percentage assigned to you can directly determine whether you recover anything at all.

Why Evidence Disappears Fast in Truck Accident Cases

Commercial trucks carry data that passenger cars simply don't: electronic logging device (ELD) records showing hours of service, GPS tracking, engine control module (black box) data, dashcam footage, and maintenance logs. Trucking companies are not always eager to hand this material over voluntarily, and some of these systems automatically overwrite or delete data on a rolling basis if no one sends a formal preservation request quickly.

What this means practically: The sooner a lawyer sends a spoliation letter demanding preservation of logbooks, black box data, and maintenance records, the better the odds that evidence proving fault still exists by the time your case is investigated. Waiting weeks or months can mean that evidence is gone before anyone asks for it.

This is one of the clearest, most practical reasons that trucking accident cases are handled differently than ordinary car accident claims from the very first phone call.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a lawyer who specifically practices near Ave Maria, or can any Florida attorney handle my case?
Florida law applies statewide, so the statutes governing your claim are the same regardless of where in Florida your firm is based. What matters more is experience with commercial trucking litigation specifically, since these cases involve federal trucking regulations, broker liability, and evidence types that differ from an ordinary car accident case.
How long do I actually have to file a lawsuit after a trucking accident near Ave Maria?
For most crashes occurring on or after March 24, 2023, Florida Statute § 95.11 sets a two-year deadline from the date of the accident. If your crash happened earlier, a different deadline may apply, so you should confirm your specific date with a Florida attorney rather than relying on a general rule.
What if the truck driver worked for a company based outside Florida?
You can generally still bring a claim in Florida if the crash occurred here, and out-of-state trucking companies operating on Florida roads are subject to Florida's negligence laws and, in many cases, federal motor carrier safety regulations as well.
Can I sue the freight broker that hired the trucking company, not just the driver?
Following the 2026 U.S. Supreme Court decision in Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, federal law no longer automatically blocks negligence claims against brokers who negligently hired an unsafe carrier, which means a broker may now be a viable defendant depending on the facts of your case.
What happens if the trucking company says I was partly at fault?
Under Florida's modified comparative negligence rule (Florida Statute § 768.81), you can still recover damages as long as you are found 50% or less at fault, though your recovery will be reduced by your percentage of fault.
How soon should I see a doctor after a truck accident to protect my claim?
Florida law requires treatment within 14 days of the crash to preserve your eligibility for Personal Injury Protection (PIP) benefits, so prompt medical care is important both for your health and for protecting your legal rights.

The Truestead Takeaway

A trucking accident near Ave Maria involves more moving parts than a typical car crash: multiple potential defendants, federal trucking regulations, fast-disappearing evidence, and strict Florida deadlines that have gotten shorter in recent years. The practical next step is the same one I'd give my own family: get medical treatment promptly, avoid giving a recorded statement to an insurer before you understand your rights, and have your specific crash reviewed by a Florida attorney who can confirm which deadlines and comparative fault rules apply to your facts. Nothing in this article is legal advice for your individual situation, but it should give you a clear, accurate starting point for the conversation.
