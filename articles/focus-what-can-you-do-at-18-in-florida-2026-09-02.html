What Can You Do at 18 in Florida?

Quick Answer

At 18, Florida law treats you as a full legal adult: you can sign contracts, make your own will, vote, and manage your own money and medical care—but your parents also lose automatic authority to help you, which is why many families put a healthcare surrogate and power of attorney in place right around this birthday.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 2, 2026

What Can You Do at 18 in Florida? The Short Answer

In my practice, I get some version of this question every summer from parents whose kids just graduated high school. The short answer: turning 18 in Florida means the law removes what's technically called the "disability of nonage" under Florida Statutes § 743.07. In plain English, that means an 18-year-old is treated as a legal adult for essentially all purposes—the same as someone who's 40 or 70.

That includes the right to vote, sign a lease, take out a loan, get married without parental consent, join the military, sue or be sued, and be tried as an adult if charged with a crime. It also includes something people don't think about until it matters: the right to make your own healthcare and estate planning decisions—and the corresponding loss of your parents' automatic authority to make those decisions for you.

You Can Now Make a Valid Florida Will

One of the most overlooked rights that kicks in at 18 is the ability to make a will. Under Florida Statutes § 732.501, any person who is at least 18 years old and of sound mind may execute a will. Florida takes the formalities seriously, so it's not as simple as writing your wishes on a piece of paper:

  • The will must be in writing (Florida does not recognize handwritten, or "holographic," wills, even if fully in your own handwriting)
  • It must be signed by you, the testator
  • It must be signed by two witnesses, who sign in your presence and in each other's presence, per Florida Statutes § 732.502

Most 18-year-olds don't have a taxable estate or complicated assets, so a full will may feel unnecessary. But for young adults with a car, a bank account, digital assets, or strong wishes about who should make decisions if something happens to them, a simple, properly executed will is inexpensive peace of mind—for them and for their parents.

Powers of Attorney: Giving—and Receiving—Legal Authority

Turning 18 also means you can now serve as someone's agent under a power of attorney, and you can appoint your own agent for the first time. Florida's Power of Attorney Act (Florida Statutes Chapter 709) requires that an agent be a natural person who is 18 or older, or a qualified financial institution.

A few Florida-specific quirks matter here:

  • Florida does not allow "springing" powers of attorney that only take effect upon incapacity—a Florida durable power of attorney is effective the moment it's signed
  • Under Florida Statutes § 709.2202, certain powerful authorities—like making gifts, changing beneficiary designations, creating or amending a trust, or disclaiming an inheritance—require the principal to separately sign or initial those specific provisions

For a new 18-year-old, this usually isn't about giving someone control of a fortune—it's about naming a trusted parent or relative who can step in and handle a bank account, a lease, or paperwork if the young adult is traveling, at college out of state, or temporarily unavailable.

Why Parents Lose Automatic Authority—and What Fills the Gap

This is the part that catches families off guard. Once your child turns 18, you no longer have automatic legal authority to access their medical records, speak with their doctors, or make healthcare decisions on their behalf—even though you're still paying for their insurance and driving them to appointments. The same goes for financial matters like bank accounts opened solely in the young adult's name.

⚠ A Real Gap to Plan For If your newly-18 son or daughter is in a car accident or medical emergency and can't communicate, hospital staff and financial institutions are not required to talk to you—even though you're the parent—unless the appropriate documents are in place.

The fix isn't complicated. Two documents commonly close this gap:

  • A healthcare surrogate designation, allowing a parent (or anyone the young adult chooses) to make medical decisions and receive health information if the young adult can't communicate, consistent with the framework in Florida Statutes § 765.202
  • A durable power of attorney under Florida Statutes Chapter 709, allowing a trusted person to handle financial and legal matters

Many families I work with put these in place at the same time as high school graduation or the start of college—not because anything is expected to go wrong, but because it's far easier to sign these documents while everyone is healthy and available than to scramble for court authority later.

Full Legal and Financial Responsibility

Along with new rights come new responsibilities. At 18, a person can enter into binding contracts—cell phone plans, apartment leases, car loans, credit cards—without a parent's signature or guarantee. They can also be sued directly, and if they're accused of a crime, the case proceeds through the adult criminal justice system rather than juvenile court, meaning adult penalties and a record that isn't automatically eligible for the protections available to juveniles.

What I tell Florida families: Turning 18 is a legal milestone, not just a birthday. It's worth a short, calm conversation—and possibly a visit to an attorney's office—rather than assuming everything continues on autopilot.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an 18-year-old in Florida write their own will without a lawyer?
Florida law only requires that the person be 18 or older and of sound mind, and that the will be signed by the testator and two witnesses under Florida Statutes § 732.501 and § 732.502. It's legally possible to do without an attorney, but strict execution formalities mean errors can invalidate the will, so many people still choose to have it prepared and reviewed by a Florida attorney.
Do parents automatically lose access to their child's medical records at 18?
Yes. Once a child turns 18, federal and state privacy protections generally treat them as an adult, and parents no longer have automatic access to medical records or the right to make healthcare decisions unless the young adult has signed a healthcare surrogate designation or similar authorization.
Can an 18-year-old serve as someone else's power of attorney agent in Florida?
Yes. Florida Statutes Chapter 709 requires an agent to be a natural person 18 or older (or a qualified financial institution), so an 18-year-old is legally eligible to serve as an agent.
Is a handwritten will valid in Florida once you turn 18?
No. Florida does not recognize holographic (handwritten, unwitnessed) wills, regardless of the testator's age. A valid Florida will must be in writing and signed by the testator and two witnesses under the requirements in Florida Statutes § 732.502.
What documents should an 18-year-old sign before leaving for college?
Many Florida families have a new 18-year-old sign a healthcare surrogate designation and a durable power of attorney so a parent can step in for medical or financial matters if needed, along with considering a simple will if they have assets or strong wishes about their estate.
Does turning 18 affect how a young adult would be treated in criminal court?
Yes. At 18, a person is prosecuted as an adult rather than through the juvenile system, which generally means exposure to adult penalties and a record that does not receive the confidentiality protections available to minors.

The Truestead Takeaway

Turning 18 in Florida is a genuine legal turning point—your child gains full authority over their own decisions, and you, as a parent, lose the automatic right to step in. None of this is cause for alarm; it's simply the law working as designed. The sensible next step for most families is a short conversation about a will, a healthcare surrogate designation, and a durable power of attorney, followed by a review with a Florida estate planning attorney to make sure the documents fit your family's actual situation.
