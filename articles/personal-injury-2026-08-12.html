Florida Personal Injury Claims in 2026: The Rules Have Changed

Quick Answer

Since March 24, 2023, Florida gives most injured people just two years from the date of an accident to file a negligence lawsuit, and you cannot recover any damages if you're found more than 50% at fault. PIP (no-fault) insurance is still required in Florida in 2026 despite online rumors it was repealed.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Personal Injury Attorney August 12, 2026

The Two-Year Deadline: Florida's Statute of Limitations Has Shrunk

In my practice, the single most damaging mistake I see is someone who waits too long to get advice after a car accident or a fall, assuming they have plenty of time. That assumption used to be safer than it is today. Florida's tort reform law, House Bill 837, changed F.S. § 95.11 so that most negligence-based injury claims — car accidents, slip-and-falls, premises liability, and similar cases — now carry a two-year statute of limitations instead of the old four-year window.

That two-year clock generally starts running on the date of the accident itself — not when you finish treatment, not when you realize an injury is worse than you thought, and not when you finally hear back from an insurance adjuster. If your incident happened before March 24, 2023, the older four-year rule may still apply to your claim, but anything after that date falls under the shorter window.

⚠ Don't wait to find out where you stand. Missing the filing deadline typically means losing the right to sue entirely, no matter how strong your case is. If you're unsure which deadline applies to your situation, that's a conversation to have with a Florida attorney promptly — not something to guess about.

The 51% Rule: Florida's Modified Comparative Negligence Standard

Florida also moved away from its old "pure comparative negligence" rule, which allowed an injured person to recover something even if they were 99% at fault. Under F.S. § 768.81(6), Florida now follows modified comparative negligence: if you are found to be more than 50% at fault for your own injury, you recover nothing at all.

  • If you're found 50% or less at fault, your compensation is simply reduced by your percentage of fault.
  • If you're found 51% or more at fault, Florida law bars recovery completely.
  • This 51% bar does not apply to medical negligence (malpractice) claims, which follow different rules.

What this means practically: insurance companies now have a strong financial incentive to argue that you share significant blame for your own accident, even in cases where that seems like a stretch. How fault gets allocated — and by whom — can make or break a claim, which is exactly why documentation and early legal guidance matter so much.

Florida's No-Fault PIP System Is Still the Law — Don't Believe the Rumors

I've had more than a few clients come in convinced that Florida "got rid of" no-fault insurance. As of this writing, that is not accurate. Florida remains a no-fault state under F.S. § 627.736, and registered vehicles are still required to carry a minimum of $10,000 in Personal Injury Protection (PIP) coverage. PIP is meant to pay a portion of your medical bills and lost wages after a crash regardless of who caused it, before anyone starts pointing fingers.

There has been legislative discussion — including a bill that would have shifted Florida toward a fault-based bodily injury system — but that proposal did not become law. Some of the confusion online traces back to outdated articles and AI-generated summaries repeating claims about a repeal that never happened. Always confirm current requirements with your insurer or attorney rather than relying on search results alone.

The 14-Day Rule: Under Florida's PIP law, you generally must seek medical treatment within 14 days of the accident, or your PIP benefits can be cut off almost entirely. If you were in any kind of crash, seeing a doctor promptly isn't just good health practice — it protects your legal and insurance rights.

Slip-and-Fall and Premises Liability Claims Follow the Same New Deadline

If you were hurt on someone else's property — a grocery store, an apartment complex, a business parking lot — Florida calls this a premises liability claim, and slip-and-fall cases are the most common type. These claims are now subject to the same shortened two-year statute of limitations under HB 837, and the same 51% comparative negligence bar applies.

Property owners in Florida generally owe visitors a duty to maintain reasonably safe conditions and to warn of hazards they knew or should have known about. But proving what the property owner knew, and when, often depends on evidence that disappears quickly — surveillance footage gets overwritten, wet floors get mopped up, incident reports get filed away. That's part of why early documentation matters so much in these cases.

What to Do After an Accident: Practical Steps That Protect Your Claim

Regardless of whether you were in a car accident or a fall, a few habits consistently help protect an injured person's legal position:

  • Seek medical care promptly — both for your health and to satisfy Florida's PIP 14-day rule.
  • Document the scene — photos of the vehicles, road conditions, the hazard that caused a fall, and any visible injuries.
  • Get names and contact information for witnesses before they leave.
  • Report the incident to police (for car accidents) or the property owner/manager (for falls), and get a copy of any report filed.
  • Be cautious with insurance adjusters — recorded statements can be used to argue you share fault, even unintentionally.
  • Keep records of medical bills, missed work, and out-of-pocket expenses.

None of this requires you to have all the answers immediately. It simply preserves your options while you decide how to move forward.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have to file a personal injury lawsuit in Florida?
For most negligence claims — car accidents, slip-and-falls, and similar cases — arising on or after March 24, 2023, Florida law gives you two years from the date of the incident under F.S. § 95.11. Claims from before that date may still follow the older four-year rule; an attorney can confirm which applies to your situation.
What happens if I was partly at fault for my accident?
Under Florida's modified comparative negligence rule (F.S. § 768.81(6)), you can still recover damages reduced by your percentage of fault, as long as you are found 50% or less responsible. If you're found more than 50% at fault, Florida law bars you from recovering anything.
Is Florida still a no-fault insurance state in 2026?
Yes. Despite online claims to the contrary, Florida's PIP no-fault system under F.S. § 627.736 remains in effect, and vehicles must still carry a minimum of $10,000 in PIP coverage. A proposed bill to move Florida to a fault-based system did not pass.
What is the 14-day rule for PIP coverage?
Florida law generally requires you to seek medical treatment within 14 days of a car accident to preserve your PIP benefits. Missing this window can result in PIP coverage being significantly reduced or denied for that claim.
Do the same deadlines apply to slip-and-fall cases?
Yes. Slip-and-fall and other premises liability claims are treated as negligence claims and are subject to the same two-year statute of limitations and 51% comparative negligence rule as car accident cases.
Does the 51% rule apply to medical malpractice cases?
No. Florida's modified comparative negligence standard under F.S. § 768.81(6) specifically does not apply to medical negligence actions, which are governed by separate rules.

The Truestead Takeaway

The practical upshot of Florida's tort reform is that injured Floridians have less room for error than they used to — a shorter two-year filing window and a fault threshold that can eliminate recovery entirely if you're found more than half responsible. None of that means you should panic, but it does mean waiting to seek guidance carries more risk than it once did. If you've been hurt in a car accident, a fall, or another incident in Florida, the sensible next step is to document what happened, get appropriate medical care promptly, and have your specific situation reviewed by a Florida attorney while evidence and deadlines are still fresh.
