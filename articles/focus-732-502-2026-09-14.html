Florida Statute 732.502: What It Requires for a Valid Will

Quick Answer

Florida Statute 732.502 is the law that spells out exactly how a will must be signed and witnessed to be legally valid in Florida: it must be in writing, signed by the testator (or by someone else at the testator's direction and in their presence) at the end of the document, and signed by two witnesses who watched the testator sign, or heard the testator acknowledge the signature, all in each other's presence.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 14, 2026

What Is Florida Statute 732.502?

If you searched "732.502," you're probably trying to figure out whether a will, maybe yours, maybe a family member's, was signed the right way to hold up in a Florida probate court. Florida Statute 732.502 is the section of Florida's Probate Code (Chapter 732, Title XLII) titled "Execution of wills." It lays out the formalities every will must satisfy to be valid in Florida, regardless of where the person who made it (the "testator") lived or where the will was signed.

In my practice, this is one of the statutes I return to most often, because so many will contests and probate delays come down to a simple question: was the will actually executed the way Florida law requires? Get the formalities right, and the will is on solid ground. Get them wrong, even by a small margin, and the whole plan can unravel.

The Core Requirements of Section 732.502

Florida Statute 732.502 requires a will to be:

  • In writing. Florida does not recognize oral ("nuncupative") wills, no matter how clearly the person's wishes were spoken or witnessed.
  • Signed by the testator at the end of the document, or signed by another person on the testator's behalf, in the testator's presence and at the testator's direction, if the testator is physically unable to sign.
  • Signed by two attesting witnesses, who must sign in the presence of the testator and in the presence of each other.

The witnesses don't necessarily need to have watched the testator physically sign the document. Under Florida law, it is enough that the testator signed (or acknowledged a previously affixed signature) in front of the witnesses, and the witnesses then signed in front of the testator and each other. That sequence, testator, then witnesses, all within one continuous, mutually observed transaction, is what Florida courts look for.

Why this matters: A will that is otherwise perfectly drafted, with the right beneficiaries, the right personal representative, and clear instructions, can still fail if the signing ceremony didn't follow section 732.502. That's why I insist on a controlled, attorney-supervised signing for every will I prepare.

Does 732.502 Apply to Wills Signed Outside Florida?

Yes. Section 732.502 also addresses wills executed by people who were not Florida residents at the time of signing. Florida generally recognizes a will as validly executed if it complies with Florida's formalities, or with the formalities of the state (or country) where it was signed, or where the testator was domiciled at signing or at death. This provision, patterned after the Uniform Probate Code, exists precisely because Florida has so many residents, retirees, and snowbirds who moved from other states with a will already in hand.

What I tell Florida clients who relocated from another state: even if your old will is technically valid here, that doesn't mean it's the right document for your current life. A will drafted for a different state may not account for Florida-specific issues like homestead property, Florida's elective share rules, or how Florida probate actually works. A validity review is not the same as an update.

What Happens If a Will Doesn't Meet 732.502's Requirements?

A will that fails to meet the execution requirements of section 732.502 is generally treated as invalid, meaning it cannot be admitted to probate as a will. If that happens, the person's estate may pass under Florida's intestacy laws (as if no will existed at all), or under an earlier, validly executed will, if one exists.

⚠ Common pitfalls I see: Wills signed with only one witness, witnesses who signed at a different time or in a different room than the testator, wills where a page was replaced or re-stapled after signing, and DIY or downloaded will forms that don't get executed with the formality Florida law demands. Any of these can trigger a costly, stressful will contest during probate, exactly when your family can least afford the delay.

How Section 732.503 (Self-Proof) Works With 732.502

Right after the execution statute comes Florida Statute 732.503, which allows a will to be made "self-proved." A self-proving will includes a notarized affidavit, signed by the testator and both witnesses at the time of execution (or later), confirming that the will was signed in compliance with section 732.502.

Why does this matter? Without a self-proving affidavit, the witnesses may need to be tracked down and sworn in during probate to confirm the will was properly executed, which can be difficult if years have passed or a witness has moved, become unreachable, or passed away. A self-proving affidavit lets the will move through probate without that extra step in most cases. This is a small addition at signing that saves real time and expense for your family later, and it's a standard part of how I prepare every will.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Florida require a will to be notarized under 732.502?
No. Section 732.502 itself requires only the testator's signature and two witnesses; notarization is not required to make a will valid. Notarization becomes relevant separately, under section 732.503, if you want to make the will self-proving.
Can a handwritten (holographic) will be valid in Florida?
Florida does not recognize purely handwritten, unwitnessed wills as valid, even if written entirely in the testator's own hand. To be valid in Florida, a will must still be signed and witnessed in accordance with section 732.502.
Do the witnesses need to read the will or know what's in it?
No. Witnesses under section 732.502 are attesting to the signing process, not the contents of the will. They don't need to know what the will says, only that the person signing identified the document as their will and signed it in their presence.
Can a beneficiary of the will also serve as a witness?
Florida law generally allows an interested witness to sign a will, but doing so can create complications and potential challenges regarding that beneficiary's gift. It's best practice, and what I recommend to every client, to use disinterested witnesses whenever possible.
What if a will was signed in another state before moving to Florida?
Section 732.502 generally recognizes wills that were validly executed under the law of the state or country where they were signed, or where the testator was domiciled. That said, a will drafted for another state's laws should still be reviewed by a Florida attorney to make sure it works well with Florida-specific issues like homestead and probate procedure.
Is an electronic will valid under 732.502?
Florida has separate statutory provisions addressing electronic wills, which involve additional requirements beyond traditional paper execution. If you're considering an electronic will, that situation should be reviewed individually with a Florida attorney rather than assumed to fit within the traditional framework of section 732.502.

The Truestead Takeaway

Section 732.502 sounds technical, but the idea behind it is simple: Florida wants to be sure that the document offered as your last will actually reflects your wishes, signed by you (or at your direction) and witnessed properly, so there's no room for doubt later. Small execution mistakes, a missing witness signature, an out-of-order signing, a DIY form filled out without guidance, can cause real problems for your family in probate. If you're unsure whether your existing will, or one you found for a loved one, was executed correctly, that's worth having reviewed by a Florida attorney rather than guessing.
