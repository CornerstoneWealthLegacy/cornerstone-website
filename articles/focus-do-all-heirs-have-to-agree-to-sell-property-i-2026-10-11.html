Do All Heirs Have to Agree to Sell Property in Florida?

Quick Answer

As a general rule, yes: when siblings or other relatives inherit property together, all co-owners must agree to a voluntary sale. But Florida law does not force families to stay deadlocked forever. If one heir wants to sell and the others refuse, that heir can file a partition action in court, and for most inherited family property, a 2020 Florida law gives the other heirs a 45-day window to buy out the heir who wants to sell before any forced sale happens.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Real Estate Attorney October 11, 2026

The Short Answer: Yes, Unless Someone Forces the Issue

In my practice, this is one of the questions I hear most often from families after a parent or grandparent passes away. The honest answer is that Florida law generally requires all heirs to agree before inherited property can be voluntarily sold. When two or more people inherit real estate together, they typically become what Florida law calls "tenants in common." Each heir owns an undivided percentage interest in the whole property, not a specific room or section of it, and no single co-owner can unilaterally sell the entire parcel out from under the others.

That said, "everyone has to agree" does not mean one stubborn or absent heir can hold the property hostage indefinitely. Florida law provides real, court-tested paths forward when heirs cannot reach consensus, both during the probate process and after it closes.

What Happens During Probate (Before the Heirs Even Own It Yet)

Many people assume that once someone dies, the heirs immediately own the house and can do whatever they want with it. In reality, the property usually first passes through probate, and during that phase, the rules are different.

  • If the will specifically directs the personal representative (the executor) to sell the property and distribute the proceeds, that instruction generally controls, and the heirs' individual preferences do not override it.
  • If the estate owes debts, taxes, or administration expenses that cannot be covered by other assets, the personal representative may need to sell real estate to satisfy those obligations, even if the heirs would rather keep the property.
  • Florida probate law also gives the personal representative certain authority to petition the court for permission to sell estate property when it is necessary to properly administer the estate.

In other words, during active probate, the decision is not always purely up to the heirs. The personal representative and the probate court play a significant role, particularly when there are debts, taxes, or a will that spells out a sale.

After Probate Closes: This Is Where Unanimous Consent Really Matters

Once probate concludes and the property is distributed to the heirs as tenants in common, the dynamic shifts. At that point, a voluntary sale genuinely does require everyone's signature. A title company will not close on a sale of the whole property unless every co-owner agrees and signs the deed.

This is exactly when families get stuck. One sibling may want to sell and move on; another may want to keep the family home; a third may be unreachable or uncooperative. Florida law does not force anyone to sell voluntarily, but it also does not force the willing heir to remain co-owner forever against their wishes.

The Partition Remedy: Any co-owner of Florida real property, no matter how small their ownership share, has the right to file a partition action asking the court to either physically divide the property or, more commonly with a single family home, order it sold and the proceeds divided according to each person's ownership interest. This right is set out in Chapter 64 of the Florida Statutes, and specifically Fla. Stat. § 64.031, which confirms that even a co-owner with a modest minority interest, such as 5%, has the same legal standing to seek partition as a co-owner holding the majority share.

The 2026 Reality: Florida's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act

Here is the development every Florida family dealing with inherited property should understand. In 2020, Florida adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (UPHPA), codified at Fla. Stat. §§ 64.201 through 64.214. This law does not replace the old partition process entirely, but it adds meaningful protections specifically for family-inherited property, and by 2026 it has become the standard framework courts apply in these cases.

The UPHPA applies automatically when a property qualifies as "heirs property," generally meaning:

  • The co-owners are related to one another (commonly siblings, cousins, or other family members).
  • At least one owner acquired their interest through inheritance or as a gift, rather than by purchase.
  • There is no written agreement, such as an operating agreement or partnership agreement, governing how the property is to be managed or sold.

When the UPHPA applies, the court process includes safeguards that did not uniformly exist before:

  • All known co-owners must receive proper notice of the partition action.
  • The court orders an independent appraisal to establish the property's fair market value.
  • Under Fla. Stat. § 64.207, the co-owners who did not request the sale get a 45-day window to buy out the interest of the heir who filed for partition, at a price based on that court-ordered appraisal.
  • If no buyout occurs and the property must be sold, the UPHPA generally favors a fair, open-market listing over a courthouse-steps auction, which tends to produce a better sale price for everyone involved.
⚠ Important Nuance: The UPHPA gives family members a real opportunity to keep inherited property in the family, but it does not eliminate the underlying right to partition. If no one buys out the requesting heir within the statutory window, the sale process will move forward.

What This Means for Your Family

If you are one of several heirs and everyone is on the same page about selling, the process is straightforward: all co-owners sign the deed, and the sale proceeds are divided according to each person's inherited share. The friction only arises when even one heir disagrees.

If that happens, Florida law does not leave families in permanent limbo. A willing heir can ask the court to partition the property, but the UPHPA ensures the other heirs are not blindsided. They are notified, given an independent valuation, and given a genuine chance to buy out the departing heir's share before any forced sale occurs. That balance, protecting both the heir who needs to move on and the heirs who want to preserve the family property, is really the point of the 2020 reform.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can one heir force the sale of inherited property in Florida?
Yes. Any co-owner, regardless of how small their ownership percentage is, can file a partition action under Chapter 64 of the Florida Statutes to force a sale or division of the property if the other heirs refuse to agree to a voluntary sale.
What is 'heirs property' under Florida law?
Heirs property generally refers to real estate owned by related co-owners where at least one person acquired their share through inheritance or gift, and there is no written agreement governing the property. If it qualifies, Florida's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (Fla. Stat. §§ 64.201-64.214) applies additional protections before a forced sale can occur.
How does the 45-day buyout right work?
Under Fla. Stat. § 64.207, when a co-owner files for partition of qualifying heirs property, the remaining co-owners who did not request the sale have 45 days to buy out that co-owner's interest at a price based on the court-ordered independent appraisal, potentially avoiding a forced sale of the whole property.
Does the personal representative need every heir's permission to sell during probate?
Not necessarily. If the will directs a sale, or if the estate needs to sell real property to pay debts, taxes, or expenses of administration, the personal representative may proceed with court oversight even if some heirs would prefer to keep the property.
What if one heir refuses to respond or cannot be found?
An unresponsive or missing heir does not permanently block the process. The other co-owners can still pursue a partition action, and the court has procedures for providing proper legal notice even when a co-owner is difficult to locate.
Is a partition action expensive or lengthy?
Partition cases vary significantly depending on whether the UPHPA applies, whether the parties cooperate, and whether the property needs to be appraised and listed on the open market. Because timelines and costs depend heavily on the specific facts, it is worth discussing your situation with a Florida attorney early on.

The Truestead Takeaway

If your family is navigating inherited property in Florida, the good news is that the law is not designed to trap anyone in permanent co-ownership against their will, nor is it designed to force a quick sale that tramples a family's wish to keep the home. The rules differ depending on whether you are still in probate or have already received title as tenants in common, and whether your property qualifies as heirs property under the 2020 reform. Because the right path, voluntary sale, partition action, or a buyout under the 45-day window, depends heavily on your family's specific facts and the property's title history, I'd encourage you to have your situation reviewed by a Florida attorney before anyone files anything in court.
