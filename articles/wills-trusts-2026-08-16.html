Florida Wills, Trusts & the Funding Step Everyone Forgets

Quick Answer

In Florida, a revocable living trust only avoids probate for the assets that are actually retitled into it during your lifetime; a will alone — or an unfunded trust — still has to go through probate. A pour-over will works alongside the trust as a backup, but it doesn't skip probate for whatever it catches.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 16, 2026

Why a Trust Alone Doesn't Avoid Probate — Funding Does

In my practice, this is the single most common misunderstanding I see, even among clients who've done everything else right. They sign a beautifully drafted revocable living trust, feel like the job is finished, and never take the next step: actually moving assets into it. That next step — called funding — is what determines whether your estate skips probate or not.

A Florida revocable trust, created under Florida's Trust Code (F.S. Chapter 736), is simply a legal container. It only controls what's actually titled in its name. That means bank accounts, brokerage accounts, real estate, and business interests generally need to be re-titled — deeded, re-registered, or re-assigned — into the trust before death. An asset left in your individual name, even if your trust document says it should go to your children, will still likely need to pass through Florida probate to get there.

What I tell clients: A trust you haven't funded is like a safe deposit box you never put anything into. The box exists. It just isn't holding what you meant it to hold.

How Trust Funding Actually Works in Florida

Funding looks different depending on the asset type:

  • Real estate is typically transferred by recording a new deed from you, individually, to you as trustee of your trust. Florida law (F.S. § 689.025) sets out formal requirements for these deeds, including how quitclaim deeds must be executed and recorded in the county where the property sits. If you own Florida homestead property, this step deserves special care — homestead has its own constitutional protections and creditor exemptions under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, and how you title homestead can affect those protections and how it passes at death under F.S. § 732.4015.
  • Bank and brokerage accounts usually require paperwork with the institution to retitle the account in the trust's name, or to name the trust as a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death beneficiary.
  • Business interests — an LLC membership, a partnership share, closely held stock — often require review of the operating agreement or bylaws before the trust can legally hold them.
  • Retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s) are generally not retitled into a trust at all; instead, they pass by beneficiary designation, which is a separate document at the custodian and should be coordinated with your overall plan, not left to default to your estate.

None of this happens automatically when you sign the trust. It's a follow-up project, and in my experience it's the step most often left half-done.

The Pour-Over Will: Your Backup, Not Your Plan A

Every revocable trust I draft for a Florida client is paired with a pour-over will. Under Florida's enabling statute, F.S. § 732.513, a pour-over will directs any assets left in your individual name at death into your trust, so they're ultimately distributed under the trust's terms rather than under Florida's intestacy statutes. The statute requires that the trust already exist — or be signed at the same time as the will — and that the will clearly identify it.

But here's the part people miss: a pour-over will is still a will. It must be executed with the same formalities as any other Florida will under F.S. § 732.502 — signed by you at the end, in the presence of two witnesses, all present together, and both witnesses signing in your presence and each other's. And critically, anything the pour-over will "catches" still has to go through the Florida probate process before it lands in the trust. It's a safety net for what falls through the cracks, not a substitute for funding the trust properly in the first place.

So — Will or Trust? For Most Florida Families, It's Both

I rarely tell a client to choose one or the other. A well-built Florida estate plan typically uses:

  • A revocable living trust to hold funded assets, manage them privately during any period of incapacity, and distribute them without a probate proceeding;
  • A pour-over will as backup for anything left outside the trust;
  • Coordinated beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance, since those pass by contract, not by will or trust terms;
  • Supporting documents — a Florida durable power of attorney (F.S. Chapter 709), a healthcare surrogate designation (F.S. § 765.202), and a living will (F.S. § 765.301) — for decisions made while you're alive but unable to act.

For a smaller, simpler estate, a will-only plan with proper beneficiary designations can sometimes be entirely reasonable — probate in Florida isn't the ordeal some marketing materials suggest, and for modest estates it can be a straightforward, court-supervised process. The right structure really depends on the size and complexity of what you own, whether you hold Florida real estate, whether privacy matters to your family, and whether you're trying to plan around a period of incapacity, not just death.

⚠ A Common Gap: Clients often fund the trust when it's first signed, then buy a new account, refinance a property, or open a new brokerage account years later — and forget to title the new asset in the trust's name. I recommend a periodic check-up on trust funding, not just a one-time signing appointment.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I have a revocable living trust, do I still need a will in Florida?
Yes. A pour-over will is the standard companion document — it catches any asset that wasn't properly transferred into the trust before death and directs it there, and it also lets you name a guardian for minor children, which a trust cannot do.
Does putting my Florida homestead into a trust affect its protections?
It can, depending on how the trust is drafted and titled. Florida homestead has unique constitutional protections and specific rules for how it can be devised under F.S. § 732.4015, so homestead property should be reviewed individually before it's deeded into any trust.
Do retirement accounts get transferred into my trust?
Generally no. IRAs and 401(k)s pass by beneficiary designation filed with the account custodian, not by trust titling, so those designations need to be reviewed separately to make sure they match your overall estate plan.
What happens if I never fund my trust?
Assets left titled in your individual name typically still have to go through Florida probate, even though you have a signed trust — the trust only controls what's actually been transferred into it.
Is a handwritten or online will valid in Florida?
Florida wills must be signed by the testator and two witnesses who are all present together, per F.S. § 732.502; documents that don't meet these formalities risk being found invalid, so any will — however it's drafted — should be executed carefully and reviewed by a Florida attorney.
How often should I review my trust and its funding?
I generally recommend a review whenever you buy or sell major property, open new accounts, have a change in family circumstances, or every few years as a matter of routine, since newly acquired assets don't automatically join an existing trust.

The Truestead Takeaway

A revocable living trust can be one of the most useful tools in a Florida estate plan, but only if it's actually funded — and that's the step I see skipped more than any other. If you have a trust you signed some time ago, it's worth pulling your deeds, account statements, and beneficiary forms and checking whether they actually name the trust, or whether they're quietly sitting in your individual name waiting for probate. That's a conversation worth having with a Florida estate planning attorney, not a guess.
