From First Call to Approval: A Florida Medicaid Planning Timeline

Quick Answer

Most Florida families move from initial consultation to a Medicaid decision in roughly two to four months, assuming records are gathered quickly and any necessary asset planning is done before or right after the application is filed. The biggest variable is not the state's processing clock, it's how fast the family can produce complete documentation.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney September 24, 2026

Meet the Alvarez Family

Ramón, 81, moved into a Sanford nursing home in early September after a fall made it unsafe for him to return home. His daughter Marisol and son Diego started making calls within days, worried about the private-pay rate eating through their father's savings. The Alvarez family is a composite I'm using to illustrate a typical timeline, not an actual client, but their situation mirrors what I see constantly in my practice: a sudden nursing home placement, an income and asset picture that needs sorting out, and a family that has no idea how long "getting Dad on Medicaid" is actually going to take.

I've already covered general eligibility rules, the five-year lookback, and homestead protection in other pieces on this site. This one stays narrowly focused on the calendar: what happens week by week, and where families like the Alvarezes typically gain or lose time.

Weeks 1-2: Consultation and Records Gathering

Marisol called our office the week after Ramón was admitted. In the initial consultation, we reviewed his income sources, his bank and investment statements, his homestead status, and whether any gifts or transfers had occurred in the past five years. This is also when we identified that Ramón's countable assets exceeded the $2,000 limit that applies to a single Medicaid applicant, and that his monthly income was near the current gross income cap for Florida's long-term care programs.

The family spent these two weeks pulling together bank statements, life insurance documents, the deed to Ramón's house, and old account records. In my experience, this step is where families either move quickly or stall for a month. Statements that have to be requested from a bank or an old brokerage account can take one to three weeks to arrive, so starting this hunt immediately matters more than almost anything else in the timeline.

Weeks 3-4: Planning Design and Document Signing

Once we had a clear financial picture, we designed a plan to bring Ramón's countable assets down to the eligible limit using strategies allowed under Florida law, such as spending down on exempt expenses, purchasing an irrevocable funeral contract, and structuring any remaining excess assets appropriately. Because Ramón was married years ago but widowed, there was no community spouse to plan around, which simplified things; families with a healthy spouse still living at home often need additional weeks here to address spousal protections.

By the end of week four, Ramón's updated documents (a durable power of attorney allowing Diego to act on his behalf, and any needed asset transfer paperwork) were signed. Anyone whose parent lacks a durable power of attorney in place before a health crisis often loses real time here, because DCF and financial institutions need proof of legal authority before they'll release records or accept instructions.

Weeks 5-6: Executing Transfers and Purchases

With documents signed, the family executed the spend-down: paying down medical bills, prepaying funeral expenses, and repositioning the remaining excess funds. Any transfer made during this window, or in the five years prior, gets scrutinized during the lookback review, so we kept receipts and documentation for every transaction. This is also the point where, if a transfer strategy involving the homestead were part of the plan, a deed would typically be prepared and recorded.

By week six, Ramón's countable assets were at or under the eligible threshold, and we were ready to file.

Weeks 7-8: Filing the Application and the DCF Request for Information

We filed Ramón's application through DCF's online system in week seven. Florida's application process involves more than one agency: DCF determines financial eligibility, CARES (under the Department of Elder Affairs) confirms Ramón needs a nursing facility level of care, and AHCA administers the Medicaid program itself once approved. All three pieces have to come together before a decision issues.

⚠ Watch for the RFI About ten days after filing, DCF sent a Request for Information asking for two additional months of bank statements and clarification on a small transfer from Ramón's account the prior year. This is the single most common reason approvals stall. The application clock effectively pauses while DCF waits on you, so the family's turnaround time on that request mattered as much as anything DCF did on its end. Diego got the documents back within three days, which kept things moving.

Weeks 9-12: CARES Assessment, Interview, and Decision

While DCF reviewed the financial side, the nursing home coordinated with CARES for the level of care assessment confirming Ramón medically qualified for nursing facility Medicaid. DCF also conducted a phone interview with Diego, who was serving as Ramón's representative under the power of attorney, to confirm details in the application.

Florida's target is 45 days from a complete application for standard cases, and 90 days for cases requiring a disability determination or level of care review, which applied here since Ramón needed the nursing facility assessment. These are policy targets, not guarantees, and actual timing varies by county caseload. In Ramón's case, the decision came in about five weeks after the RFI response was submitted, putting the whole process, from that first phone call to the approval letter, at just under twelve weeks.

One critical point I make sure every family understands: Florida eliminated retroactive Medicaid eligibility for nursing home applicants back in 2019. Coverage for a non-pregnant adult begins on the first day of the month of application, not before, and there is no three-month retroactive window like some other states allow. That's why the weeks the Alvarez family spent gathering records mattered so much. Every week that passed before filing was a week of private-pay nursing home costs that Medicaid would never reach back and cover.

Where Families Typically Lose Time

  • No power of attorney in place. Without it, adult children can't get records or sign for a parent, and setting one up during a crisis (especially if the parent's capacity is declining) takes longer and is harder.
  • Incomplete records at filing. An application filed with gaps almost always triggers a Request for Information, and slow responses to that request are the number one cause of delay.
  • Unexplained transfers. Any gift or asset move in the five years before applying needs a clear paper trail. Undocumented transfers can require weeks of additional investigation.
  • Waiting to start the spend-down. Families who wait to see "how things go" before addressing excess assets often lose a month or more they didn't need to lose.
The Alvarez Result By starting immediately, keeping a power of attorney ready, and responding to DCF's request within days, the Alvarez family moved from first call to approval letter in about twelve weeks, close to the faster end of what I typically see for cases requiring a level of care assessment.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does Florida Medicaid approval usually take?
Florida's policy targets are 45 days for standard applications and 90 days for cases needing a disability or nursing facility level of care determination, measured from a complete application. Real-world timing varies by county and how quickly the family responds to any requests for information.
Does Florida Medicaid cover nursing home bills from before the application date?
No. Florida eliminated retroactive Medicaid eligibility for most adults, including nursing home applicants, in 2019. Coverage generally begins the first day of the month in which the application is filed, which is why filing promptly matters.
What is a Request for Information and how does it affect the timeline?
DCF issues a Request for Information (RFI) when submitted documents are missing or unclear. The processing clock effectively pauses until the family responds, so a fast, complete response is one of the best ways to keep an application on schedule.
Can the planning and document signing happen after someone is already in a nursing home?
Yes, and it often does, as it did for Ramón. The key is having a valid power of attorney in place so a family member can act on the applicant's behalf, since capacity can decline quickly after a health crisis.
What agencies are involved in a Florida Medicaid nursing home application?
DCF determines financial eligibility, CARES (part of the Department of Elder Affairs) determines whether the applicant needs a nursing facility level of care, and AHCA administers the Medicaid program once eligibility is established. All three pieces generally need to align before a final decision issues.
What is the biggest thing families can do to speed up approval?
Submit a complete application with all required documentation upfront, and respond to any DCF request for information within days, not weeks. Insufficient or delayed records are the most common cause of extended processing times.

The Truestead Takeaway

The Alvarez family's twelve-week path from first call to approval letter wasn't luck, it was the result of moving fast on records, having a power of attorney ready, completing the spend-down before filing, and turning around DCF's follow-up request within days. Every Florida family's numbers and timeline will look a little different depending on income, assets, marital status, and whether transfers need explaining, so the sensible next step is the same one Marisol took: sit down with a Florida elder law attorney as soon as a nursing home placement looks likely, not after. Reviewing your specific situation early is what turns a stressful guessing game into a plan with a realistic calendar attached to it.
