Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator: What Your Estate Will Actually Pay

Quick Answer

Florida law (F.S. § 733.6171) sets a presumed-reasonable fee schedule for probate attorneys based on the estate's value — generally starting around $1,500 for small estates and moving to roughly 3% of the first $1 million for larger ones — but this schedule is a ceiling, not a mandatory charge, and many straightforward Florida probate matters are handled for a flat fee well below it.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 1, 2026

How a Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator Actually Works

When people search for a "florida probate attorney fee calculator," what they're really looking for is a straight answer to a reasonable question: how much is this going to cost my family? In my practice, I tell clients that any calculator worth using is really just applying one statute — Florida Statute § 733.6171 — which sets out a schedule of fees that Florida law presumes to be reasonable for probate attorney services.

That schedule is tied to the value of the probate estate. It generally starts at a flat amount for smaller estates and then shifts to a percentage-based structure as the estate grows — commonly described as around 3% on the first $1 million, with lower percentages applying to larger portions of bigger estates. A calculator simply plugs your estate's value into that formula and spits out the presumed-reasonable number.

Here's the part most online calculators leave out: that number is a starting point for negotiation, not a bill you're required to pay. I'll explain why below.

The Statutory Fee Schedule Under F.S. § 733.6171

Florida is unusual among states in that it actually writes a fee schedule into the probate statutes. Under § 733.6171, the presumed-reasonable fee is generally structured in tiers based on the estate's value, with a flat starting amount for the smallest estates and a percentage-based approach — often cited around 3% of the first $1 million — once the estate grows larger. Larger estates see the percentage step down for value above certain thresholds, down to roughly 1% for very large estates.

  • Smaller estates typically see a flat presumed fee.
  • Mid-size estates (a few hundred thousand dollars) commonly fall in a percentage range that, for illustration, could work out to somewhere in the five figures for a $500,000 estate under the statutory formula.
  • Larger estates see the marginal percentage decrease as value increases past $1 million, $5 million, and $10 million tiers.

Separately, Florida law also sets presumed-reasonable compensation for the personal representative (Florida's term for what other states call the executor) under a related statute, generally following a similar tiered-percentage structure. Many online calculators combine both the attorney fee and the personal representative fee into one total estimate — which is useful, but can make the number look larger than what a family will actually pay in attorney fees alone.

Why the Statutory Fee Is Presumed, Not Required

This is the single most important thing I want every Florida family to understand: the § 733.6171 schedule is a presumption of reasonableness, not a mandatory price tag. Florida law requires attorneys to disclose in writing that this fee is negotiable, and in my experience, most routine Florida probate matters — where there's no litigation, no contested will, and no complicated tax issue — are handled on a flat-fee basis that is often meaningfully lower than what the statutory percentage would produce.

What this means for you: A calculator that only applies the statutory percentage may overstate what a straightforward, uncontested Florida probate will actually cost. Ask any attorney you're considering whether they charge the statutory fee or a flat fee for administration of an estate like yours.

The statutory schedule exists to protect families from being overcharged and to give courts a benchmark when reviewing fee petitions — not to guarantee attorneys a percentage of every estate. Fee agreements are still a matter of contract between the personal representative and the attorney, subject to the requirement that the fee ultimately be reasonable and, in most cases, subject to court review or approval.

What Drives Cost Beyond the Basic Schedule: Extraordinary Services

The statutory fee schedule is meant to cover ordinary, routine probate administration — collecting assets, paying valid claims, and distributing property to beneficiaries. It does not automatically cover what the statute calls extraordinary services, which can include:

  • Will contests or other litigation among beneficiaries
  • Contested creditor claims
  • Elective share proceedings for a surviving spouse
  • Tax planning and preparation of estate tax returns
  • Sale or transfer of real estate held by the estate
  • Continuing a decedent's business
  • Proceedings to enforce a personal representative's statutory authority

When any of these arise, the attorney may seek additional compensation beyond the base schedule, subject to court approval. This is often where a simple calculator falls short — it can estimate the baseline, but it can't predict whether a family member will contest the will or whether a creditor dispute will require litigation.

⚠ A Word of Caution: Don't assume the calculator's number is your final cost, and don't assume it's guaranteed either. Every estate is different, and the only way to know your real cost is to have a Florida probate attorney review the actual assets, debts, and family situation involved.

Small Estates and Summary Administration

Not every Florida estate needs full, formal administration. Florida offers a streamlined process called summary administration for smaller estates or estates where the decedent has been deceased for a certain number of years. Recent changes to Florida's probate thresholds have expanded which estates qualify for this simplified process, meaning more Florida families may be able to avoid the cost and complexity of formal administration altogether.

Summary administration is typically handled for a flat fee that is substantially lower than what the statutory percentage schedule would produce for a formal administration — often in the low thousands of dollars rather than a percentage of the estate. If your loved one's estate is modest in value, or if enough time has passed since their death, it's worth asking specifically whether summary administration is available, since that alone can be the biggest cost-saving factor of all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there an official Florida court probate fee calculator?
No. Florida courts don't publish an official fee calculator. What's commonly called a "Florida probate attorney fee calculator" is simply a tool or worksheet that applies the presumed-reasonable percentages from F.S. § 733.6171 to your estate's value.
Can I negotiate probate attorney fees in Florida?
Yes. Florida law treats the statutory schedule as a presumption, not a requirement, and attorneys must disclose in writing that the fee is negotiable. Many attorneys offer flat fees for routine, uncontested estates.
How much does probate cost in Florida for an average estate?
It varies widely by estate value, complexity, and whether the matter is contested. Simple, uncontested estates using summary administration often cost far less than a full formal administration, where fees can approach several percent of the estate's value once attorney fees, personal representative compensation, and court costs are added together.
What's the difference between attorney fees and personal representative fees?
The probate attorney is paid for legal services in administering the estate; the personal representative (executor) is separately entitled to compensation for serving in that role. Florida law sets a similar tiered-percentage framework for both, and some online calculators combine them into one total estimate.
Does a large estate always mean a huge attorney fee?
Not necessarily. The statutory percentage decreases at higher estate values, and many attorneys negotiate flat or reduced fees for larger but straightforward estates. The percentage schedule is a ceiling based on presumed reasonableness, not an automatic charge.
What makes probate more expensive than the calculator shows?
Extraordinary services — such as will contests, contested claims, real estate sales, tax return preparation, or litigation to enforce the personal representative's authority — can add fees beyond the base statutory schedule, and these are hard for any generic calculator to predict.

The Truestead Takeaway

A Florida probate attorney fee calculator can give you a useful ballpark using the § 733.6171 schedule, but it can't tell you whether your family's estate qualifies for a lower-cost path like summary administration, whether a flat fee makes more sense than the statutory percentage, or whether extraordinary circumstances will add cost. In my practice, the smart next step is always the same: bring the actual facts — the estate's assets, debts, and family dynamics — to a Florida probate attorney for a real, honest estimate before assuming any calculator's number is your final cost.
