Real Estate Law in Islamorada, FL: What Buyers and Sellers Need to Know

Quick Answer

Real estate law in Islamorada, FL follows the same statewide Florida statutes that govern deeds, closings, and disclosures everywhere else in the state, but Keys transactions also involve flood zone rules, Monroe County land use regulations, and insurance considerations that make local guidance especially important.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 10, 2026

Is Real Estate Law in Islamorada, FL Different From the Rest of Florida?

In my practice, I hear a version of this question often: is buying or selling a house in the Florida Keys somehow governed by different rules than the mainland? The honest answer is mostly no. Real estate law in Islamorada, FL runs on the same statutory backbone as every other Florida transaction: written deeds under Chapter 689, Florida Statutes, notarization and acknowledgment requirements under F.S. § 695.03, and licensing standards for real estate professionals under Chapter 475, Florida Statutes.

What makes Islamorada distinct isn't the statute book, it's the terrain. Properties here sit within FEMA-mapped flood zones, are subject to Monroe County's building and land-use regulations, and often carry unique considerations around seawalls, docks, canal access, and elevation certificates. A contract that would be routine in Orlando can require extra attention in the Keys simply because of what's built on (or near) the water.

How Does a Real Estate Closing Work in Islamorada?

Every Florida closing, whether in Islamorada or Jacksonville, follows the same basic legal skeleton. The deed must be a written instrument signed by the seller (the grantor) in the presence of two subscribing witnesses, as required by F.S. § 689.01. Florida also provides a recognized statutory short form for warranty deeds under F.S. § 689.02, which many closings still use today.

Before that deed can be recorded in the Monroe County public records, it must be properly acknowledged and authenticated by a notary public or other authorized officer under F.S. § 695.03. A title search is a critical step in any closing, verifying that the seller actually holds clear, marketable title and identifying any liens, easements, or encumbrances that need to be resolved before the sale. Title insurers typically issue a commitment letter listing exceptions to coverage, which both buyer and seller should review carefully before closing.

  • Written deed signed by the seller with two witnesses
  • Notarized acknowledgment before recording
  • Title search and title insurance commitment
  • Review of any liens, easements, or HOA obligations
  • Recording in the Monroe County Official Records

What Disclosures Are Required When Selling Property in Islamorada?

Florida law imposes meaningful disclosure obligations on sellers, and I encourage every client to take them seriously rather than treat them as boilerplate. Sellers are generally expected to disclose known material defects, including structural issues, roofing or foundation problems, and known environmental hazards such as mold or proximity to flood zones as reflected on current FEMA maps. In the Keys specifically, flood zone status and elevation are not minor details, they can significantly affect insurability and financing.

If the property is part of a homeowners' or condominium association, additional disclosure rules apply regarding the association's financial condition, including pending special assessments and insurance coverage. Failing to provide required association disclosures can expose a seller to real financial consequences, potentially including damages tied to the contract price plus attorney's fees under F.S. § 720.306(2)(a).

⚠ Don't Skip the Fine Print Waterfront and canal-front properties common in Islamorada often carry additional obligations tied to seawalls, docks, and submerged land leases. These items are easy to overlook in a standard residential contract but can create real headaches after closing if not addressed up front.

What's Changing in Florida Real Estate Law in 2026?

A few developments are worth knowing about if you're buying or selling in Islamorada this year. First, the FAR/BAR residential contract forms most commonly used across Florida have been updated for 2026, incorporating revised disclosure language that has become the baseline for standard residential transactions statewide, including in the Keys.

Second, following the National Association of Realtors settlement, Florida practice now generally requires a written buyer representation agreement, with clear compensation terms disclosed upfront before a buyer tours most properties with an agent. If you're working with a real estate agent in Islamorada, expect to sign this agreement earlier in the process than you may have in years past.

Third, on the insurance side, state regulators approved a statewide average rate decrease for Citizens Property Insurance policies, roughly 8.8 percent for multiperil policies and 5.5 percent for wind-only coverage, effective for new policies starting July 1, 2026, and applying to existing policies as they come up for renewal. Given how central insurance cost is to any Keys purchase decision, this is genuinely good news for buyers and sellers alike.

On the Ballot This November Florida voters will decide in November 2026 whether to approve a constitutional amendment that would expand the homestead exemption and adjust the cap on non-homestead property tax increases. Nothing has changed yet for today's transactions; this is a proposed change that, if approved, would take effect January 1, 2027.

Why Local Knowledge Matters for Islamorada Real Estate

What I tell clients considering property in the Upper Keys is this: the statutes are statewide, but the practical risks are local. Monroe County's land development regulations, building permit requirements, and environmental restrictions (including protections related to the Florida Keys' unique ecosystem) can affect everything from a simple renovation to a full teardown-and-rebuild. Title issues involving old submerged land grants, dock permits, or shared canal easements show up more often here than in most of the state.

None of this means buying or selling in Islamorada is riskier than elsewhere in Florida, it simply means the details deserve careful review by someone familiar with both Florida real estate law and the specific quirks of Keys properties.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need an attorney to close on a home in Islamorada, FL?
Florida does not require a buyer or seller to use an attorney for a residential closing, but many Keys transactions involve title, flood zone, or association issues that benefit from legal review before signing a contract.
What makes real estate transactions in the Florida Keys different from the mainland?
The underlying statutes are the same statewide, but Keys properties often involve additional considerations like flood zone designations, elevation certificates, canal or dock easements, and Monroe County land use rules that don't come up as often elsewhere in Florida.
What disclosures does a seller have to make in Florida?
Sellers generally must disclose known material defects such as structural problems and environmental hazards, and if the property is part of an association, financial disclosures about the association's condition are also required.
Is the 2026 Florida property tax amendment already in effect?
No. It is a proposed constitutional amendment on the November 2026 ballot that would need 60 percent voter approval, and if passed, it would not take effect until January 1, 2027.
Do I need a written agreement with my real estate agent now?
Following changes stemming from the national NAR settlement, Florida buyers generally sign a written buyer representation agreement with clear compensation terms before touring most properties with an agent.
Where are deeds recorded for Islamorada property?
Deeds and other real estate instruments for Islamorada properties are recorded in the Monroe County Official Records after being properly signed, witnessed, and notarized under Florida law.

The Truestead Takeaway

Real estate law in Islamorada, FL is built on the same Florida statutes that govern every closing in the state, but the practical details of Keys property, from flood zones to canal easements to association disclosures, deserve a closer look than a routine mainland sale. Whether you're buying your first home on the water or selling a longtime family property, it's worth having your contract and title work reviewed by a Florida attorney familiar with both the statewide rules and the local landscape before you sign anything.
