Florida Probate, Explained: What Families and Personal Representatives Need to Know

Quick Answer

Florida probate is the court-supervised process for settling a person's estate. Most estates use either summary administration (a faster track for smaller or older estates) or formal administration (the full process with a personal representative).

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney June 23, 2026

What is probate, and when is it required in Florida?

When someone passes away in Florida, the assets they owned in their own name alone — a bank account with no beneficiary, a car, a home titled solely to them — usually can't just be handed to the family. Someone with legal authority has to step in, settle the debts, and pass what's left to the right people. That court-supervised process is probate, and it's governed by Florida's Probate Code and the Florida Probate Rules.

Here's the part that calms most clients down: not everything goes through probate. Probate generally only touches assets that were owned individually by the person who died, with no built-in way to pass on automatically. Accounts with a named beneficiary, jointly-held property with survivorship rights, and assets held in a properly funded trust typically pass outside probate. So the real question is rarely "do we have to do probate?" — it's "how much of the estate has to go through it, and which track applies?"

Summary vs. formal administration: which one applies?

Florida offers two main probate tracks, and the difference matters a great deal for time and cost.

  • Summary administration — the streamlined option. In general terms, it's available when the value of the estate that's actually subject to probate is below a statutory threshold, or when enough time has passed since the death (Florida law provides a separate path once a decedent has been gone for more than two years). Summary administration usually does not require appointing a personal representative, which is why it's faster and less expensive.
  • Formal administration — the full process. This is the standard route for larger estates and any situation that needs a person with ongoing legal authority to act. The court appoints a personal representative (PR), issues "letters of administration" proving their authority, and supervises the gathering of assets, notice to creditors, payment of valid claims, and final distribution.

Florida also has a very limited "disposition without administration" path for tiny estates made up of exempt property and final-expense reimbursement. Because the eligibility thresholds and dollar figures are set by statute and are exactly the kind of detail that can be updated, I'd want any specific estate's numbers confirmed against the current Florida Statutes before you rely on them.

What does a Florida personal representative actually have to do?

The PR — what other states sometimes call an "executor" — is a fiduciary. That's the most important word in this whole article. It means the PR must act in the best interests of the estate and its beneficiaries, not their own, and Florida holds them to that standard.

In a formal administration, the PR's core duties generally include:

  • Collecting and inventorying the estate's assets and getting them valued;
  • Giving proper notice to beneficiaries and to creditors;
  • Reviewing creditor claims and paying the valid ones (and properly objecting to the ones that aren't);
  • Paying any taxes and administration expenses; and
  • Distributing what remains to the people entitled to it, then closing the estate.

Florida generally requires the personal representative to be represented by an attorney in a formal administration, except in narrow situations such as where the PR is the only interested person. Florida also has rules about who can serve — there are residency and family-relationship limits on out-of-state PRs that surprise a lot of snowbird families. In my practice, this is the single issue that most often trips up the adult child living up north who expected to handle everything.

Good to know Serving as a personal representative is a real legal job with real liability. You don't have to figure it out alone — Florida law actually contemplates the estate (not your own pocket) paying reasonable attorney's fees for the administration.

How long does Florida probate take, and what does it cost?

A straightforward summary administration can sometimes wrap up in a couple of months. A typical formal administration more often runs from roughly six months to a year or more, and a big reason is the creditor process: the PR publishes a notice to creditors, and creditors then have a defined window to file claims before the estate can safely close. Estates with disputes, hard-to-value assets, or litigation take longer.

On cost, Florida law addresses both personal representative compensation and attorney's fees for estate administration, with statutory guidance that ties presumptively reasonable fees to the value of the estate. On top of that you'll see court filing fees, the cost of publishing the creditor notice, and any appraisal or accounting expenses. Because those fee structures are statutory and can be adjusted, I describe them in concept here rather than quoting a figure — the current rates should be confirmed for your estate.

Can my family avoid probate altogether?

Often, yes — at least for most of the estate, and with planning done before death. The well-established Florida tools include:

  • A properly drafted and fully funded revocable living trust;
  • Payable-on-death (POD) and transfer-on-death (TOD) designations on bank and brokerage accounts;
  • Up-to-date beneficiary designations on life insurance and retirement accounts;
  • Joint ownership with right of survivorship where appropriate; and
  • Florida's enhanced life estate ("Lady Bird") deed for real estate, used carefully.

Florida's strong constitutional homestead protections also affect how a primary residence passes, and homestead can transfer outside the usual probate process to certain heirs — but the homestead rules are technical and interact with who's in the family, so this is exactly where a plan should be reviewed rather than assumed.

⚠ A common mistake A trust only avoids probate for the assets actually retitled into it. I regularly see beautifully drafted trusts that were never "funded" — the home and accounts were never moved in — so the family ends up in probate anyway. Funding the plan is just as important as signing it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does having a will avoid probate in Florida?
No. A will is your instruction manual for probate — it tells the court who should serve and who inherits — but the will itself still goes through the probate process to be effective.
Can an out-of-state relative serve as my personal representative?
Sometimes. Florida limits who may serve as a non-resident personal representative, generally requiring a qualifying family relationship. A Florida attorney should confirm whether your chosen person is eligible.
What happens if someone dies without a will in Florida?
The estate still goes through probate, but Florida's intestacy laws decide who inherits and in what shares. That outcome may not match what the person would have chosen, which is one reason planning matters.
Do I need a lawyer for Florida probate?
For a formal administration, Florida generally requires the personal representative to be represented by an attorney, with limited exceptions. Some summary administrations are simpler, but most families still benefit from counsel.
How are creditors handled in Florida probate?
The personal representative publishes a notice to creditors and serves known creditors. Creditors then have a limited time to file claims, and valid claims are paid from the estate before beneficiaries receive their shares.
Is the homestead house part of the probate estate?
Florida's homestead has special constitutional protections and often passes to certain heirs outside the ordinary probate distribution. Because the rules are technical, the specific home should be reviewed by a Florida attorney.

The Truestead Takeaway

Probate in Florida is more navigable than most families fear, but the right path — summary or formal — and the personal representative's duties depend heavily on the specific assets, the family, and current statutory thresholds I'd want confirmed rather than assumed. If you're administering an estate, get the will (if any) and account titles reviewed early so you choose the correct track. And if you're planning ahead, the most reliable way to spare your family the process is a properly drafted plan that's actually funded.
