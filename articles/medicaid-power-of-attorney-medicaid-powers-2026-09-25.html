Florida Medicaid Planning

Why the Elder Law Attorney Said Dad's Power of Attorney Isn't Enough for Medicaid

Quick Answer

Florida law requires certain powers, gifting, trust creation, and beneficiary changes among them, to be separately signed or initialed in a power of attorney. A generic bank form that just names an agent, without those specific grants, usually can't be used to do Medicaid planning without a costly guardianship.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney September 24, 2026

Vince's Situation: A Common Story in Titusville

Vince is 77 and lives in Titusville. He's a composite example, not an actual Truestead client, but his situation is one I see often. Years ago, at his bank, Vince signed a durable power of attorney naming his daughter as agent. It's a perfectly fine, simple document. It lets her walk into the branch, deposit a check, and manage his checking account.

The trouble started when Vince needed nursing home care and the family met with an elder law attorney to talk about Medicaid planning. The attorney read the form, looked up, and said: this isn't enough. Vince's daughter had legal authority to manage his money, but she had no authority to do the things Florida Medicaid planning often requires: make gifts beyond ordinary limits, create or fund a trust, or change how his retirement accounts pay out. The form was silent on all of it.

What Florida Law Actually Requires: The 'Super Powers'

Florida's Power of Attorney Act, found in F.S. Chapter 709, treats certain powers differently from everyday banking authority. Under F.S. § 709.2202, an agent cannot exercise these powers unless the principal separately signed or initialed next to each one, specifically enumerated, not just implied by broad language elsewhere in the document. Elder law attorneys often call these the "super powers" because of how consequential they are.

The powers that must be individually initialed include the authority to:

  • Create, amend, revoke, or terminate a trust
  • Make gifts of the principal's property, beyond what the law allows automatically
  • Change beneficiary designations on life insurance, retirement accounts, or payable-on-death accounts
  • Waive the principal's right to be a beneficiary of a joint and survivor annuity, including a survivor benefit under a retirement plan
  • Create or change survivorship rights in jointly held property
  • Delegate the agent's own authority under the power of attorney

A form that only says "my agent may handle my banking and financial affairs" does not grant any of these. Florida requires the specific line item, initialed, before an agent can touch them.

Why This Matters So Much for Medicaid Planning

Medicaid planning strategies almost always depend on one or more of these super powers. Funding a trust as part of asset restructuring, for example, requires trust-creation authority. Setting up a Qualified Income Trust, sometimes needed when a person's income exceeds Medicaid's income limit, requires the agent to create and fund a trust on the principal's behalf. Purchasing a Medicaid-compliant annuity, transferring assets between spouses, or making gifts as part of a planning strategy all depend on gifting or trust powers being expressly written into the document.

Without those powers spelled out, the agent legally cannot do these things, no matter how urgently the family needs to act or how clearly everyone agrees it's the right move. This is precisely what Vince's attorney meant. His daughter had authority to pay his bills, but not to help implement a Medicaid plan.

⚠ When the Form Falls Short If a power of attorney lacks these specific grants and the principal has since lost capacity to sign a new one, the family's only path forward may be petitioning the court for guardianship, a process that takes time, costs money, and puts a judge in charge of decisions the family could have made privately with a properly drafted document.

Durable vs. Springing, and Why Banks Reject Old Forms

A durable power of attorney remains effective even after the principal becomes incapacitated, which is exactly what's needed for long-term care and Medicaid planning. A "springing" power, one that only takes effect upon a doctor's certification of incapacity, can create delays at the worst possible moment, since the agent has to first prove incapacity before anyone will honor the document. Florida law generally favors durable powers that are effective immediately upon signing, precisely to avoid that gap.

Families are also increasingly running into practical friction with banks and financial institutions refusing to honor older or generic power of attorney forms, especially ones that predate the 2011 overhaul of Chapter 709 or that use vague, boilerplate language. Institutions want to see the specific, initialed grants of authority the statute requires. A form that looks fine on paper can still get rejected at the teller window if it doesn't meet current statutory form.

The Time to Fix This Is Now, While Capacity Exists

A power of attorney is only as good as the mind that signs it. Once a person loses the capacity to understand and execute legal documents, a power of attorney can no longer be updated, no matter how badly the family needs it. This is why elder law attorneys push families to review these documents early, well before a health crisis, rather than waiting until a nursing home is calling.

For Vince, this meant a straightforward next step: sit down with his elder law attorney while he still had full capacity and sign a new, properly drafted durable power of attorney that separately initials the gifting and trust powers his family's Medicaid strategy would need. It didn't mean giving away control today. It meant making sure his daughter would have the legal tools to act on his behalf later, without a guardianship proceeding standing in the way.

A Simple Way to Think About It A bank-form power of attorney is built for everyday bill-paying. A Medicaid-ready power of attorney is built for a future crisis. They are not the same document, even if they look similar at first glance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my parent's old power of attorney be fixed, or do we need a brand new one?
It depends on the document and whether your parent still has the legal capacity to sign. An elder law attorney can review the existing form to see if it can be amended or whether a new document is the cleaner path.
What happens if my parent has already lost capacity and the POA doesn't have the super powers?
In that situation the family generally cannot simply update the document. The typical remedy is a guardianship proceeding, which involves court oversight, additional cost, and time that a properly drafted power of attorney would have avoided.
Does every power of attorney need gifting and trust powers?
No. Someone who never expects to need Medicaid planning or complex asset transfers may not need these provisions. But for families anticipating long-term care costs, these powers are often essential to have in place.
Is a durable power of attorney the same as a healthcare surrogate designation?
No. A durable power of attorney under Chapter 709 covers financial and legal matters. Healthcare decisions are handled separately through a healthcare surrogate designation under F.S. § 765.202 and, for end-of-life wishes, a living will under F.S. § 765.301.
Why would a bank refuse to honor a power of attorney that seems valid?
Banks are cautious about liability and often insist on documents that clearly meet current statutory requirements, including the specifically initialed powers. A generic or outdated form can trigger a refusal even if it was validly signed at the time.

The Truestead Takeaway

Vince's family learned an important lesson many Florida families face too late: a power of attorney that works fine for everyday banking may not work at all for Medicaid planning. Florida law requires gifting, trust creation, beneficiary changes, and similar powers to be spelled out and separately initialed, not assumed from general language. If your family is holding an older or bank-issued power of attorney and long-term care may be on the horizon, it's worth having a Florida elder law attorney review the document now, while your parent still has the capacity to sign an updated one if needed. Waiting for a crisis is the one thing this planning cannot fix.
