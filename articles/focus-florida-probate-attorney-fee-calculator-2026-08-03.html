Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator: What You'll Really Pay in 2026

Quick Answer

Florida law sets a presumed-reasonable attorney fee schedule tied to estate value (F.S. § 733.6171) — roughly 3% of the first $1 million for a typical formal administration — but that schedule is a ceiling, not a mandate, and your attorney must tell you in writing that the fee is negotiable. Most straightforward Florida estates today are handled for a flat fee well below the statutory percentage, especially small estates using summary administration.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 3, 2026

How a Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator Actually Works

When Floridians search for a "florida probate attorney fee calculator," what they're really trying to answer is simple: how much of my loved one's estate is going to go to legal fees? In my practice, I tell clients that any true Florida probate attorney fee calculator has to start with one statute: F.S. § 733.6171. This law gives Florida a rare feature most states don't have — a published fee schedule that courts presume to be reasonable, based on the value of the estate being administered.

The statutory schedule is tiered. As a general framework, it starts at a flat amount for very small estates and then moves to percentages of estate value — roughly 3% on the first $1 million for a typical formal administration, stepping down to lower percentages as the estate grows into the millions, and down to around 1% for the portion of an estate above $10 million. A $500,000 estate run through the statutory schedule might work out to something in the neighborhood of $15,000 in attorney fees — but as I explain below, that number is a starting point for negotiation, not a bill you're stuck with.

Why the Statutory Fee Is a Ceiling, Not a Requirement

This is the single most misunderstood point in Florida probate, and it's the reason I want every reader to slow down before assuming they owe a percentage-based fee. Under § 733.6171, the schedule is presumed reasonable — meaning a court won't second-guess a fee that falls within it. But the statute does not require an attorney to charge that amount, and Florida law requires the attorney to disclose, in writing, that the fee is negotiable.

In real-world practice, most Florida probate matters — especially estates without litigation, contested wills, or complicated real estate and tax issues — are handled on a flat fee. For many straightforward estates I see flat fees quoted somewhere in the range of $3,500 to $5,000, not a percentage of the estate, because the actual attorney work (filing the petition, handling notice to creditors, preparing the inventory, and closing the estate) is largely administrative rather than hourly-intensive litigation.

What this means for you: Before you sign an engagement letter, ask directly whether the fee is a flat fee or a statutory percentage fee, and ask for the written disclosure Florida law requires. A reputable Florida probate attorney will walk you through both options.

Summary Administration: Florida's Fast, Lower-Cost Track

Not every Florida estate needs full, formal administration. Florida offers a streamlined process called summary administration for smaller or older estates, and this is where the biggest cost savings usually happen. Recent 2026 changes to Florida's probate framework raised the monetary threshold for summary administration, so more estates now qualify for this faster, less expensive path — generally available where the estate's value falls under the current statutory threshold, or where the death occurred more than two years before the petition is filed, regardless of value.

  • Attorney fees for summary administration typically run as a flat fee, often in the range of $1,500 to $4,500, depending on the complexity of assets and whether real estate is involved.
  • Court filing fees are separate from attorney fees and are paid to the clerk of court.
  • Publication costs (the legal notice required in many counties) add a modest additional cost on top of filing fees.

If your loved one's estate is modest in value, or if enough time has passed since the death, it's worth asking a Florida probate attorney specifically whether summary administration is available — because it changes the entire fee calculation.

Formal Administration and 'Extraordinary Services' That Cost Extra

Larger or more complicated estates go through formal administration, which involves appointing a personal representative (Florida's term for an executor), giving formal notice to creditors, and court supervision of the process. This is where the § 733.6171 percentage schedule most often comes into play as a benchmark, even when the actual fee charged is a negotiated flat fee or hourly rate below that benchmark.

Certain legal work falls outside the base statutory fee and is billed as an extraordinary service. This can include will contests, contested creditor claims, elective share disputes, estate tax planning and return preparation, sale of real estate held by the estate, continuation of a family business, and any adversarial litigation. Effective July 1, 2026, Florida law also expressly recognizes legal work spent enforcing a personal representative's statutory authority as a potential extraordinary service. These services are billed separately from the base fee and typically require court approval.

⚠ A Word of Caution: No online calculator — including the statutory percentages discussed here — can tell you what your family's specific estate will actually cost. Real estate holdings, out-of-state property, disputes among heirs, and tax issues all change the picture. Treat any number you calculate online as a rough starting estimate only.

Personal Representative Compensation vs. Attorney Fees

One source of confusion I see often: the personal representative (executor) is entitled to their own compensation, separate from the attorney's fee. Under Florida law, the personal representative's fee generally follows a similar percentage framework — commonly cited as around 3% of the first $1 million of the estate, stepping down at higher value tiers. Family members serving as personal representative sometimes waive this fee, but they are not required to, and it's a separate line item from whatever the estate pays its probate attorney.

All fees in a supervised Florida probate matter — attorney fees, personal representative fees, and extraordinary service fees — are subject to review and approval by the probate court, either as the matter proceeds or when the estate is finally closed. This court oversight is part of why Florida's system, while it can feel bureaucratic, also builds in a real check against unreasonable charges.

The Full Cost Picture: What Estates Actually Pay

Putting it all together, most Florida estates fall somewhere along this range:

  • Simple summary administration: Often a few thousand dollars total, combining a flat attorney fee, court filing fees, and publication costs.
  • Typical formal administration: Commonly falls somewhere in the range of roughly 3% to 7% of the estate's value once attorney fees, personal representative compensation, filing fees, and administrative costs are combined — though many estates pay meaningfully less than the statutory percentage would suggest, because of flat-fee arrangements.
  • Court filing fees: Generally a few hundred dollars, with formal administration costing somewhat more than summary administration, plus small county-level variations.

The single biggest variable is always attorney's fees, and the single biggest lever you have to control that cost is asking upfront about a flat fee versus the statutory percentage — and asking whether your estate even qualifies for summary administration in the first place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there an official Florida probate attorney fee calculator online?
There's no single official government calculator, because so much depends on estate-specific facts. The best approach is to use the F.S. § 733.6171 percentage tiers as a rough benchmark, then get an actual flat-fee quote from a Florida probate attorney based on your specific estate.
Can I negotiate probate attorney fees in Florida?
Yes. Florida law treats the statutory fee schedule as presumed reasonable, not mandatory, and requires attorneys to disclose in writing that the fee is negotiable. Many Florida probate attorneys handle straightforward estates on a flat fee that is lower than the statutory percentage.
What estates qualify for summary administration in Florida?
Summary administration is generally available for estates at or under the current statutory value threshold, or for any estate where the death occurred more than two years before the petition is filed. The specific dollar threshold has been updated by recent Florida legislation, so it's worth confirming the current figure with an attorney.
Who pays the probate attorney's fee — the estate or the family?
The attorney's fee is typically paid out of the estate's assets, not out of pocket by the personal representative or heirs personally, though the specific arrangement should be spelled out in the engagement agreement.
Are attorney fees the only cost of probate in Florida?
No. Court filing fees, publication costs for legal notices, personal representative compensation, and costs for extraordinary services like real estate sales or tax preparation are all separate from the attorney's base fee.
Does a bigger estate always mean a bigger percentage fee?
Not necessarily. The statutory schedule actually decreases in percentage terms as estate value climbs into higher tiers, and larger estates often still negotiate flat or hourly arrangements rather than paying the full statutory percentage.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida's statutory fee schedule under F.S. § 733.6171 gives families a useful benchmark, but it's a ceiling the law presumes reasonable — not a bill you're required to accept, and not a substitute for an honest conversation with a Florida probate attorney about your specific estate. Before assuming your family will pay a percentage of the estate, ask whether the matter qualifies for summary administration, ask for a flat-fee quote, and ask for the written fee disclosure Florida law requires. If you're facing a probate matter, the sensible next step is a review with a Florida probate attorney who can tell you, plainly, what your family's situation will actually cost.
