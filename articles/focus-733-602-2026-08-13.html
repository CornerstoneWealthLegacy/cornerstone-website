Florida Statute 733.602: A Personal Representative's General Duties

Quick Answer

Florida Statute 733.602 makes a personal representative (executor) a fiduciary who must act in the best interests of the estate and its beneficiaries, using the same standard of care Florida law applies to trustees, and administer the estate as efficiently as circumstances reasonably allow.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 13, 2026

What Florida Statute 733.602 Actually Says

If you landed here after Googling "733.602," you're probably serving as a personal representative of a Florida estate — or you're a beneficiary trying to understand what the executor is legally supposed to be doing. Here's the plain-English version.

Florida Statute § 733.602 is part of Chapter 733, the section of Florida's Probate Code that governs the duties and powers of personal representatives (what many states call "executors"). At its core, the statute establishes two things:

  • A personal representative is a fiduciary — someone legally required to act in the best interests of another party rather than themselves.
  • That fiduciary must observe the same standard of care that Florida law applies to trustees — a high, well-established bar of loyalty, prudence, and impartiality.

In my practice, I tell new personal representatives that this statute is really the "job description" for the role. It doesn't list every specific task — other sections of Chapter 733 do that — but it sets the tone and the legal standard that governs everything else you do as PR.

The Core Duty: Settle and Distribute the Estate Efficiently

Section 733.602 directs a personal representative to settle and distribute the estate according to the terms of the decedent's will (or Florida's intestacy law if there's no valid will) and to do so "as expeditiously and efficiently as is consistent with the best interests of the estate."

That phrase matters. Florida law doesn't expect a personal representative to rush through probate recklessly, but it also doesn't tolerate needless delay. The statute strikes a balance: move the estate toward closing, but don't sacrifice sound decision-making — paying valid debts, resolving disputes, handling real estate, or dealing with creditor claims properly — just to finish faster.

The personal representative is also required to use whatever authority they have — granted by the Probate Code itself, by the terms of the will, or by court order — for the benefit of interested persons, including creditors. That last part often surprises families: a personal representative doesn't just answer to the beneficiaries. Legitimate creditors of the estate have rights too, and the PR has to balance both.

Why the "Trustee Standard of Care" Is a Big Deal

Florida trust law imposes some of the most demanding fiduciary obligations in American law, including duties of loyalty, prudence, impartiality among beneficiaries, and careful recordkeeping. By tying personal representatives to that same standard, § 733.602 effectively imports all of that rigor into probate administration.

In practical terms, this means a personal representative generally must:

  • Act solely in the best interests of the estate and its beneficiaries — not for personal gain.
  • Remain impartial between beneficiaries, treating them fairly unless the will itself directs unequal treatment.
  • Exercise reasonable care, skill, and caution in managing estate assets.
  • Keep the estate's property separate from personal assets and avoid self-dealing.
Why this matters for families: If you're a beneficiary who feels the personal representative is favoring one heir, dragging things out for no good reason, or mixing estate funds with their own, § 733.602 is often the statute an attorney will point to first when evaluating whether that conduct crosses a legal line.

Liability Protection: When a Personal Representative Is NOT on the Hook

Section 733.602 isn't only about obligations — it also offers some protection. A personal representative generally is not liable for an act of administration or distribution that was properly authorized at the time it was taken, even if circumstances later change or someone later disagrees with the decision.

This protects personal representatives who act in good faith, follow the terms of the will, comply with court orders, and stay within the powers granted under the Probate Code. It's a reasonable rule: without it, no one would be willing to serve as an executor, knowing that hindsight could always be used against them.

That said, this protection has real limits. It does not shield a personal representative who breaches their fiduciary duty, acts outside their authority, self-deals, or otherwise mismanages the estate. Related sections of Chapter 733 — including the provisions on the general powers of a personal representative and on improper exercise of power — work together with § 733.602 to define exactly where that line sits.

⚠ A Note on Related Statutes § 733.602 rarely stands alone in a real probate dispute. If you're researching a personal representative's conduct, you'll often also need to look at the sections addressing when a PR's duties begin, the specific powers a PR holds, and what happens when those powers are misused or a fiduciary duty is breached. An attorney reviewing your specific estate should look at these provisions together, not in isolation.

How This Applies If You're Currently Serving as Personal Representative

What I tell Florida clients who've just been appointed personal representative is this: § 733.602 is the umbrella standard, but your day-to-day job involves dozens of concrete steps — inventorying assets, providing notice to creditors, filing tax returns, paying valid claims, and eventually distributing what's left according to the will or Florida intestacy law. Every one of those steps should be measured against the fiduciary standard this statute sets: is this decision in the best interest of the estate and its beneficiaries, made with the care a prudent trustee would use?

Keeping clear records, communicating with beneficiaries, and getting professional guidance on anything unusual (a disputed claim, an odd asset, a family disagreement) is generally the safest way to stay well within the protection the statute offers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Florida Statute 733.602 in simple terms?
It's the Florida law establishing that a personal representative (executor) of an estate is a fiduciary who must act in the best interests of the estate and beneficiaries, held to the same standard of care Florida applies to trustees.
Does 733.602 apply to both executors and trustees?
The statute itself applies to personal representatives of probate estates, but it borrows the standard of care Florida law already applies to trustees, so the practical duties are very similar across both roles.
Can a personal representative be sued under 733.602?
The statute isn't primarily a basis for a lawsuit by itself, but it's often cited alongside other Chapter 733 provisions when a beneficiary or creditor alleges a personal representative breached their fiduciary duty.
Is a personal representative protected if they make a mistake?
Generally, a personal representative is not liable for an act of administration or distribution that was properly authorized at the time it was taken, but this protection does not cover breaches of fiduciary duty or actions outside their authority.
Do creditors have rights under this statute?
Yes. Section 733.602 directs the personal representative to use their authority for the best interests of interested persons, which explicitly includes creditors of the estate, not just beneficiaries.
Should I talk to an attorney if I have concerns about a personal representative's conduct?
Because fiduciary duty questions depend heavily on the specific facts of an estate, anyone with concerns about a personal representative's actions should have their situation reviewed by a Florida probate attorney.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida Statute 733.602 is short, but it does a lot of work: it defines a personal representative as a fiduciary held to a trustee's standard of care, requires efficient and fair administration of the estate, and gives good-faith, properly authorized actions some protection from liability. If you're serving as a personal representative, or you're a beneficiary or creditor with questions about how an estate is being handled, this statute is usually just the starting point — the right next step is having your specific situation reviewed by a Florida probate attorney who can look at it alongside the related duties, powers, and liability provisions in Chapter 733.
