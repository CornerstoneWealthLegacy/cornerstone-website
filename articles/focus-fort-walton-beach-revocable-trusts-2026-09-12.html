Fort Walton Beach Revocable Trusts: What Okaloosa County Families Need to Know

Quick Answer

A Florida revocable living trust is a legal arrangement you create and can change or cancel during your lifetime, designed to hold your assets and pass them to your beneficiaries without probate court. For Fort Walton Beach residents, it's a popular tool, but it only works if it's properly funded, meaning your home, accounts, and other property are actually retitled into the trust's name.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 12, 2026

What Is a Fort Walton Beach Revocable Trust, Exactly?

In my practice, I hear the same question from Okaloosa County clients every week: "Do I need a trust, or is a will enough?" A Fort Walton Beach revocable trust is simply a legal container you create during your lifetime. You transfer your assets into it, you (usually) serve as trustee and control everything exactly as before, and you name who takes over as trustee and who inherits the assets when you pass away.

Because it's revocable, you can amend it, restate it, or cancel it entirely at any time while you're competent to do so. This is a key Florida rule worth knowing: under F.S. § 736.0602, a Florida trust is presumed revocable by default unless the document specifically says otherwise. That's a favorable, flexible starting point for most families.

The main appeal for Fort Walton Beach residents, whether you're a long-time homeowner, a retiree, or a snowbird splitting time between Florida and another state, is that assets properly titled in the trust's name typically pass to your beneficiaries without going through probate court in Okaloosa County.

How Florida's Trust Code (Chapter 736) Governs Your Trust

Florida trusts aren't governed by guesswork or whatever form you find online. They're governed by the Florida Trust Code, found in F.S. Chapter 736, along with related provisions in Chapters 737 through 739. This statutory framework covers everything from how a trust is validly created, to how it can be modified, to the duties your successor trustee owes your beneficiaries.

  • Duty of prudence (F.S. § 736.0804): your trustee must manage trust assets as a careful, responsible person would.
  • Duty of impartiality (F.S. § 736.0803): if you have multiple beneficiaries, your trustee must treat them fairly, not favor one over another.
  • Trust modification (F.S. § 736.04117): under certain circumstances, an irrevocable trust can still be modified with court approval or beneficiary consent, which matters if you're converting a revocable trust to irrevocable status later, such as for long-term care planning.

For Fort Walton Beach families dealing with a homestead, Florida also has specific statutory tools addressing how homestead property can be held in trust, since Florida's homestead protections under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution interact with trust planning in ways that require careful drafting.

Does a Revocable Trust Protect My Assets From Creditors?

This is one of the most common misunderstandings I encounter, so let me be direct: a Florida revocable living trust provides no asset protection during your lifetime. Because you retain control over the trust and can revoke it whenever you like, the law treats trust assets as though they're still yours for creditor purposes. If you're sued or owe a debt, assets in your revocable trust are generally reachable by your creditors, just as they would be if you owned them outright.

⚠ Common Misconception A revocable trust is a probate-avoidance and incapacity-planning tool, not a lawsuit shield. If asset protection from creditors is your primary goal, that requires a different conversation about irrevocable trusts, homestead protections, or other Florida-specific strategies, and it should be reviewed with an attorney based on your full financial picture.

After your death, the trustee generally must still pay valid debts and administration expenses from trust assets to the extent your probate estate (if any) isn't sufficient to cover them. The trust doesn't erase your obligations; it simply changes the mechanism for transferring what's left.

What Does a Fort Walton Beach Trust-Based Plan Cost, and Is It Worth It?

A complete Florida trust-based estate plan, meaning the revocable trust itself plus a pour-over will, durable power of attorney, healthcare surrogate designation, and living will, typically runs in the range of $1,500 to $3,500 at Florida firms, depending on complexity. That may sound like a real expense, and it is, but compare it to the alternative.

Under F.S. § 733.6171, Florida's statutory probate attorney fee schedule commonly results in fees running roughly 3 to 5 percent of the gross probate estate, before you even factor in court costs, personal representative fees, and the time delay (often many months) that formal probate administration requires. For a family with a Fort Walton Beach home and modest savings, that percentage adds up quickly, and it's paid by your estate, not by you in advance.

Why This Matters The upfront cost of a trust is often far less than the eventual cost of probate, and a properly funded trust also keeps your family's financial affairs private, since trust administration, unlike probate, is not a matter of public court record.

The Mistake That Undermines Most Trusts: Failing to Fund It

Here's what I tell every client who signs a new trust: signing the document is not the finish line. A revocable trust only controls assets that have actually been retitled into its name. I've seen families create beautiful, well-drafted trusts that still ended up in probate because the home deed, the bank accounts, or the investment portfolio were never formally transferred.

  • Real estate, including your Okaloosa County homestead, needs a new deed recorded in the trust's name.
  • Bank and brokerage accounts need to be retitled or given transfer-on-death instructions coordinated with the trust.
  • Beneficiary designations on life insurance and retirement accounts need to be reviewed, since these typically pass outside the trust unless intentionally coordinated with it.
  • Business interests, such as an LLC operating agreement, sometimes need to be reviewed or amended before the trust can legally hold that interest.

This funding step is tedious, but it's the entire point of the exercise. An unfunded trust is, functionally, an expensive piece of paper that didn't accomplish what you paid for.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I still need a will if I have a revocable trust in Florida?
Yes. A properly designed Florida trust plan includes a 'pour-over will,' which catches any assets accidentally left outside the trust at your death and directs them into it, and a will is also how you name a guardian for minor children if applicable.
How is a revocable trust different from an irrevocable trust in Florida?
A revocable trust can be changed or canceled by you at any time while you're competent, and it offers no creditor protection during your life. An irrevocable trust generally cannot be easily changed and, when properly structured, can offer stronger asset protection, but it comes with real tradeoffs in flexibility and control.
Will a revocable trust avoid probate for my Fort Walton Beach home?
Only if the deed to your home has been properly transferred into the trust's name during your lifetime. Simply mentioning the home in the trust document without recording a new deed generally will not avoid probate.
Can I be my own trustee of my Florida revocable trust?
Yes, most Floridians serve as their own trustee while they're alive and able, maintaining full control over the assets, and name a successor trustee to step in if they become incapacitated or pass away.
Does a revocable trust protect my homestead's tax benefits?
Homestead property can often be held in a properly drafted revocable trust while still qualifying for Florida's homestead exemption and Save Our Homes assessment cap, but the trust language matters a great deal, so this should be reviewed with an attorney familiar with Florida homestead rules under Article VII, Section 6 of the Florida Constitution and F.S. § 196.031.
How much does it cost to set up a revocable trust near Fort Walton Beach?
A complete trust-based estate plan, including the trust, pour-over will, durable power of attorney, healthcare surrogate, and living will, commonly costs between $1,500 and $3,500 at Florida firms, though your specific quote depends on the complexity of your assets and goals.

The Truestead Takeaway

A revocable trust can be one of the most useful tools available to Fort Walton Beach families for avoiding probate, planning for incapacity, and keeping family affairs private, but it is not a magic asset-protection device, and it only works if every asset is properly funded into it. If you already have a trust, it's worth having it reviewed to confirm your home and accounts were actually retitled. If you don't have one yet, the right next step is a conversation with a Florida attorney who can look at your specific assets, your homestead, and your family situation before recommending a plan.
