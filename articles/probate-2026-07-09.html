Florida Probate in 2026: The New $150,000 Summary Administration Threshold and What Every Family Should Understand

Quick Answer

Florida probate splits into two tracks — summary and formal administration — and as of July 1, 2026, the summary administration threshold doubled to $150,000, giving many middle-income Florida families access to a faster, less expensive process.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney July 9, 2026

A Real Change for Florida Families: The $150,000 Summary Administration Threshold

In my practice, the most common frustration I hear from families is this: "Our loved one didn't have a complicated estate — why does this have to take so long?" For years, Florida law gave a faster, simpler probate track — called summary administration — only to estates with $75,000 or less in non-exempt assets. That left a large group of perfectly ordinary middle-income families stuck in full-blown court proceedings that their situations simply didn't warrant.

That changed on July 1, 2026. Legislation identified in multiple Florida law firm sources as CS/HB 1337 doubled the threshold under Fla. Stat. § 735.201 from $75,000 to $150,000. If the decedent passed away on or after July 1, 2026, and the value of the estate subject to administration in Florida — after subtracting exempt assets like a protected homestead — is $150,000 or less, the estate may now qualify for summary administration. This is the most significant change to Florida's probate thresholds in recent memory, and it matters to a lot of families.

Note on the new threshold: The $150,000 figure applies only to non-exempt probate assets. Florida's homestead, protected under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, is generally excluded from this calculation when it passes to a surviving spouse or heirs. A Florida estate planning attorney can help you determine exactly which assets count toward the threshold for your family's situation.

Summary vs. Formal Administration: Which Track Applies?

Understanding Florida's two probate tracks is the starting point for any family navigating this process.

Summary administration (Fla. Stat. Chapter 735) is available when:

  • The non-exempt probate estate is $150,000 or less (for decedents who passed away on or after July 1, 2026); or
  • The decedent has been dead for more than two years — in which case there is no estate value limit, because the creditor claim period has already run.

Summary administration does not require appointing a personal representative. A petition is filed, the court reviews it, and an order of distribution typically issues in four to eight weeks — sometimes as little as two to three months from filing. No personal representative bond, no lengthy creditor publication window running in parallel. It is meaningfully simpler.

Formal administration (Fla. Stat. Chapter 733) is required for all other estates: those with more than $150,000 in non-exempt assets and a decedent who passed away less than two years ago. Formal administration involves appointing a personal representative (what most states call an executor), marshaling assets, notifying creditors, paying claims, filing an inventory, and petitioning the court for a final discharge. It typically takes 6 to 12 months, with the three-month creditor claim period — running from the date of first publication, or 30 days after service on a known creditor, whichever is later — setting the practical floor on how quickly the estate can close.

What Does Probate Actually Cost in Florida?

Florida has a statutory fee schedule that sets a presumed-reasonable fee for both the estate's attorney and the personal representative. Under Fla. Stat. §§ 733.617 and 733.6171, each is entitled to compensation that starts at a percentage of the estate's value — roughly 3% on the first $1 million, scaling down from there. That means a $500,000 estate could generate $15,000 in attorney fees and another $15,000 in personal representative compensation, plus court and publication costs.

Here's what I always make sure clients understand: the statutory fee is not mandatory. Under § 733.6171(2), the schedule is presumed reasonable, but the attorney is required by Florida law to disclose in writing that the fee is negotiable. Some estates — particularly those with unique complexity, litigation, or out-of-state assets — may justify more; straightforward estates may cost less if you negotiate or use flat-fee services.

All told, total probate costs — attorney fees, personal representative compensation, court filing fees, and publication — typically run somewhere between 3% and 7% of the gross estate value. Court filing fees generally range from roughly $232 to $500 depending on the county. A bond may also be required unless waived by the will or by all interested persons under Fla. Stat. § 733.402.

One piece of good news for Florida families: Florida has no state estate or inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax applies.

The Personal Representative's Duties: What Florida Law Actually Requires

If an estate goes through formal administration, a personal representative (PR) must be appointed by the court. This person — named in the will or, if none, selected under Florida's priority rules — carries significant legal responsibilities under Chapter 733, Part VI of the Florida Statutes (§§ 733.601–733.620), with the general duty of administration set at § 733.602.

The PR's core obligations include:

  • Marshaling and protecting estate assets — locating, securing, and valuing everything the decedent owned that passes through probate.
  • Notifying creditors — publishing a notice to creditors in a local newspaper and serving known creditors directly, triggering the statutory claim period.
  • Filing an inventory with the court within the time required by the court's order.
  • Paying valid claims in the priority order established by Fla. Stat. § 733.707 — which means certain claims (like family allowance, funeral costs, and estate administration expenses) are paid before unsecured creditors.
  • Petitioning for discharge once all debts are paid and assets are distributed.

Florida law also requires the custodian of a will to deposit it with the clerk of court within 10 days of learning of the testator's death under Fla. Stat. § 732.901. Failing to do so can expose that person to liability for damages and attorney fees. If you are holding a loved one's original will right now, that 10-day obligation is real.

⚠ Personal Representatives Can Be Held Personally Liable A PR who distributes assets prematurely, pays the wrong creditors first, or fails to follow Florida's statutory priority order can be held personally responsible for resulting losses. If you have been named PR of a Florida estate, please consult a Florida probate attorney before taking distribution actions.

Avoiding Probate Altogether: The Tools Florida Residents Use

Probate is not inevitable — and for many Florida families, the right planning documents mean the probate courthouse never enters the picture at all. Here are the most commonly used strategies in my practice:

  • Revocable living trust — Assets held in a properly funded trust pass directly to beneficiaries without probate, under Fla. Stat. Chapter 736. A trust also provides privacy (no public court filing), continuity for incapacity, and flexibility for complex family situations. Attorney fees typically run higher than simpler tools but the long-term savings on probate costs are often significant.
  • Lady Bird deed (enhanced life estate deed) — A Florida-specific deed form that lets a homeowner transfer real property at death simply by recording a death certificate — no probate, no court order, no new deed needed. The owner retains complete control during life, including the right to sell or mortgage without the beneficiary's consent. Attorney fees for a Lady Bird deed typically run $400–$1,000. This tool also protects the home from Medicaid estate recovery and beneficiaries generally receive a stepped-up tax basis, potentially eliminating capital gains tax on a prompt sale after death.
  • Beneficiary designations and payable-on-death accounts — Life insurance, retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s), and bank or investment accounts with POD/TOD designations pass directly to named beneficiaries outside of probate. These are often the simplest and most overlooked planning moves.
  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship — Property held this way passes automatically to the surviving co-owner at death.

Each of these tools has trade-offs, and the right combination depends on your family's circumstances, your assets, and your goals. What I tell Florida clients is this: the cost of planning is almost always far less than the cost of not planning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the new $150,000 summary administration threshold apply if my loved one died before July 1, 2026?
No. The $150,000 threshold applies only to decedents who passed away on or after July 1, 2026. If your loved one died before that date, the prior $75,000 threshold applies to their estate.
Does the Florida homestead count toward the summary administration threshold?
Generally no. Florida's homestead is protected from most creditor claims under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, so its value is typically excluded from the threshold calculation. A Florida attorney can confirm how this applies to your specific property and family situation.
How long does formal probate take in Florida?
Formal administration typically takes 6 to 12 months, with the three-month creditor claim period setting the practical minimum. Estates involving tax filings, real estate issues, or disputes can take longer.
Can a personal representative be paid for their work in Florida?
Yes. Florida law provides a statutory fee schedule for personal representative compensation under Fla. Stat. § 733.617, generally mirroring what the estate's attorney may charge. The PR and the beneficiaries may also agree on a different amount.
What happens if someone doesn't file a will with the court after a death?
Under Fla. Stat. § 732.901, anyone holding an original will must deposit it with the clerk of the circuit court within 10 days of learning of the testator's death. Failure to do so can result in personal liability for damages and attorney fees.
Is a Lady Bird deed right for every Florida homeowner?
A Lady Bird deed is a powerful and affordable tool for many Floridians, but it works best for straightforward situations with clear beneficiaries. For blended families, larger estates, or situations where a trust's ongoing management benefits outweigh the cost, a revocable trust may be the better fit. A Florida estate planning attorney can help you compare.

The Truestead Takeaway

The July 1, 2026 increase in Florida's summary administration threshold — from $75,000 to $150,000 — is the most meaningful change to the state's probate landscape in years, and it opens a faster, less expensive path for a significant number of Florida families. But understanding which track applies to your family, what the personal representative is legally required to do, and how to structure your estate to avoid probate altogether are questions that deserve a real conversation with a Florida-licensed attorney. Every family's asset mix, beneficiary situation, and planning goals are different. If you've recently lost a loved one and are trying to understand next steps, or if you want to make sure your own estate never puts your family through a lengthy court process, I'd encourage you to schedule a review with a qualified Florida estate planning or probate attorney — including, of course, the team here at Truestead Law.
