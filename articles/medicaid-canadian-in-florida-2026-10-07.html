Our Canadian Father Had a Stroke in Fort Myers. Does Florida Medicaid Help?

Quick Answer

Florida Medicaid is generally not available to Canadian visitors because it requires U.S. citizenship or qualifying immigration status, and the narrow emergency Medicaid exception for noncitizens typically excludes tourists. The real answers for a Canadian snowbird are travel insurance, provincial coverage (which has shrunk considerably), private pay, and careful planning around the Florida condo.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney October 6, 2026

Gordon's Situation: A Familiar Florida Story

Gordon is 79, from Ontario, and has wintered in a condo near Fort Myers for years. He is a composite, not a real client, but his story is one I have heard in some form many times. He had a stroke this fall. He is now in a Florida hospital, his adult children are flying down from Canada, and someone at the hospital or a well-meaning neighbor has mentioned "Medicaid" as a way to cover the bills.

I understand why that word surfaces. It is the word American families use when a parent needs long-term nursing care and cannot pay privately. But Medicaid is a needs-based government health program built around U.S. residency and immigration status, and it was never designed with a Canadian provincial health card in mind. Before Gordon's family spends energy chasing a Medicaid application, they need to understand why that door is generally closed, and what doors are actually open.

Why Florida Medicaid Generally Does Not Cover a Canadian Visitor

Florida Medicaid, like Medicaid programs nationwide, requires an applicant to be a U.S. citizen or a qualified alien, which generally means someone with lawful permanent residency status held for a number of years, or certain other defined immigration categories. A Canadian citizen visiting Florida as a tourist, even one who owns a condo and spends several months a year here, does not fall into either category. Owning Florida property, paying Florida property taxes, or even registering to vote does not change immigration status for Medicaid purposes.

This is a different question from the one we address in our general Medicaid eligibility guide, which deals with income and asset limits for people who already qualify on immigration and residency grounds. Gordon's family is facing a threshold problem that comes before any of those calculations: he is not the kind of applicant the program is built to serve.

What About Emergency Medicaid for Noncitizens?

Florida, like other states, has a narrow program sometimes called emergency Medicaid for aliens. It exists for noncitizens who would otherwise meet Medicaid's financial and medical criteria except for their citizenship status, and it covers treatment of a genuine medical emergency, not ongoing care.

The trouble for a family like Gordon's is that this program is generally aimed at noncitizens who live and work in the United States without qualifying immigration status, not at tourists who are here temporarily and maintain a home and health coverage in another country. A tourist's visit does not typically establish the kind of ongoing presence or need this program contemplates. Hospitals and the Department of Children and Families, which runs Medicaid applications through its ACCESS system, evaluate this on a case-by-case basis, but families should not count on this narrow exception as a financial plan.

⚠ Do Not Delay Care Waiting on an Application A hospital must stabilize a patient regardless of ability to pay or immigration status under federal emergency treatment law. That obligation is separate from Medicaid eligibility. Do not let uncertainty about payment delay getting Gordon the care he needs; sort out payment questions afterward.

The OHIP Problem: Provincial Coverage Has Shrunk

Many Canadian families assume their parent's provincial health plan will pick up the slack. For Ontario residents, that assumption is now largely outdated. As of January 1, 2020, the Ontario Health Insurance Plan eliminated its out-of-country emergency coverage entirely. Before that change, OHIP paid a modest per-day amount toward U.S. hospital costs, and even then it typically covered only a small fraction of the real bill. Today, for most emergency care received outside Canada, OHIP pays nothing at all.

There is a narrow exception for certain dialysis patients traveling outside Canada, but a stroke admission like Gordon's would not fall under that carve-out. If Gordon's family assumed his Ontario health card would soften the financial blow of a Fort Myers hospital stay, that assumption needs to be corrected quickly, and it is worth checking whether other provinces' plans have made similar changes.

Travel Medical Insurance, Air Ambulance, and Repatriation

This is where the real financial protection for a Canadian snowbird usually lives, if it exists at all. Most snowbirds who are properly advised purchase private travel medical insurance before heading south, often supplemental to OHIP or whatever provincial plan applies. That policy, not Medicaid and not OHIP, is typically what pays the Florida hospital directly or reimburses the family.

  • Travel medical insurance policies vary enormously in what they cover, especially regarding pre-existing conditions like prior strokes or heart disease, so the policy language matters enormously after the fact.
  • Air ambulance and medical evacuation coverage is often bundled into travel insurance or sold separately. Repatriating a patient from Florida back to Canada by air ambulance is genuinely expensive, commonly tens of thousands of dollars depending on distance, the patient's condition, and whether a medical team must accompany them.
  • If Gordon has no travel insurance, or if his policy excludes his stroke because of a prior medical history, his family is likely looking at private payment for his Florida hospital stay and, if repatriation to Ontario is medically advisable, a separately negotiated and very real cost for that transport.

Families in this position should ask the hospital's case management or social work department about repatriation logistics early. Florida hospitals that regularly treat snowbirds, especially in areas like Fort Myers, Naples, and Sarasota, have often coordinated this before.

Power of Attorney, the Condo, and Planning Ahead for a Non-Resident

Two practical legal issues tend to surface alongside the medical crisis. First, if Gordon signed a Canadian power of attorney document before his stroke, Florida hospitals and financial institutions do not automatically have to honor a foreign power of attorney the way they would a document executed under Florida law. Families are often better served, while a parent is healthy, by having both a Canadian power of attorney and a Florida durable power of attorney prepared, so that whichever document is needed is ready and recognized.

Second, Gordon's Florida condo raises its own questions, separate from anything Medicaid related. Florida homestead protections generally apply to permanent Florida residents, not part-time visitors, so a snowbird's condo typically does not receive the same property tax or creditor protections a full-time Florida homeowner enjoys. And because Gordon is a Florida property owner, his estate may need to go through a Florida probate proceeding for that asset when he passes, in addition to whatever estate process applies in Ontario. This is a separate planning conversation from his medical crisis, but families often discover both issues at the same painful moment. A properly drafted trust or deed arrangement, reviewed by a Florida attorney with the Canadian side of the picture in mind, can often simplify this considerably.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Gordon apply for Florida Medicaid once he is admitted to the hospital?
Generally no. Florida Medicaid requires U.S. citizenship or qualifying immigration status, and simply being physically present or hospitalized in Florida does not establish that status.
Does emergency Medicaid cover a Canadian tourist's hospital stay?
Emergency Medicaid for noncitizens is a narrow program generally aimed at people without qualifying immigration status who live in the U.S., and it typically does not extend to short-term visitors. It should not be relied upon as a financial plan.
Will Gordon's Ontario health card (OHIP) pay for his Florida hospital bill?
As of January 1, 2020, OHIP eliminated coverage for out-of-country emergency medical care, so in most cases it will pay nothing toward a U.S. hospital stay. Families should check the specifics of their own provincial plan, since rules vary across Canada.
What actually pays for a Canadian snowbird's emergency care in Florida?
Usually private travel medical insurance purchased before the trip, supplemented by personal funds if the policy has limits or exclusions. Without travel insurance, the patient or family is generally responsible for the cost directly.
Is his Canadian power of attorney valid at a Florida hospital?
Not necessarily. Florida institutions are not required to honor a power of attorney executed under Canadian law the same way they honor one executed under Florida law, which is why many snowbirds have both documents prepared in advance.
Does Gordon's Florida condo need special estate planning because he is Canadian?
Yes, in the sense that a non-resident property owner's Florida real estate is typically still subject to Florida probate on death, and homestead protections generally do not apply to part-time residents. A Florida attorney can review whether a trust or other arrangement fits his situation.

The Truestead Takeaway

Gordon's story, like many I see in Southwest Florida each winter, is really two stories at once: an urgent medical one and a financial and legal one running quietly alongside it. Medicaid is not the answer for a Canadian visitor, and provincial coverage has become far thinner than most snowbirds realize. The honest answers are travel insurance purchased before the trip, a clear-eyed look at private pay and repatriation costs after an emergency happens, and a Florida power of attorney and property plan put in place while a parent is still healthy. If your family splits time between Canada and Florida, the right moment to review this is before the next flight south, not from a hospital waiting room. Our firm regularly helps Florida families, including those with loved ones who winter here from Canada, review these documents and property questions before a crisis forces the issue.
