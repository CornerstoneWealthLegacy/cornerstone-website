Blended Families, Snowbirds, and Business Owners: Where Florida Estate Plans Often Fall Short

Quick Answer

A generic will or trust often fails blended families, part-time Florida residents, and business owners because Florida law has specific default rules — spousal elective share, homestead inheritance rules, and residency/domicile tests — that override generic planning unless your documents are drafted to address them directly.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 21, 2026

Why a "standard" estate plan isn't enough for these situations

In my practice, I see the same pattern over and over: a family did some planning years ago — maybe a simple will, maybe a trust from another state — and assumes it will work the way they intend. For most people, it will. But for four groups of Floridians, generic planning creates real gaps: blended families, snowbirds and new residents, business owners, and families with a special-needs beneficiary. Each of these situations runs into a specific rule of Florida law that a standard plan doesn't anticipate.

None of this is cause for alarm. Florida's rules are well established and predictable once you know them. The point of this article is to walk through what the law actually provides in each situation, so you know which questions to bring to a review of your own plan.

Blended families: Florida law protects your spouse — sometimes more than you intended

If you've remarried and have children from a prior relationship, Florida law has two features you need to understand before you assume your will controls everything.

  • Elective share. Under F.S. § 732.2065, a surviving spouse generally has the right to claim a percentage of the elective estate, regardless of what your will says. This exists to prevent one spouse from being disinherited entirely, but in a blended family it can mean assets you intended for your children from a first marriage end up passing to a surviving spouse instead.
  • Homestead rights. Florida's homestead protections (Art. X, § 4, Fla. Const., and F.S. § 732.4015) place restrictions on how a homestead residence can be devised if you're survived by a spouse or minor child — restrictions that apply even if your will says something different.
  • Stepchildren inherit nothing automatically. Under Florida law, stepchildren are not treated as your legal children for inheritance purposes unless they were legally adopted. If you want a stepchild to inherit, they must be specifically and clearly named in your will or trust — general language like "my children" will not include them.

What I tell blended-family clients: the plans that actually hold up use tools designed for this exact situation — a properly drafted marital trust that provides for a surviving spouse during their lifetime while preserving the remainder for your children, and, where appropriate, a written prenuptial or postnuptial waiver of elective-share rights. These aren't do-it-yourself documents; small drafting errors can undo the whole structure.

Snowbirds and new residents: where you're domiciled matters more than where you spend the most time

Many of my clients split their year between Florida and a northern state. That's completely workable from a legal standpoint, but it raises a question that surprises people: which state actually has legal authority over your estate when you pass away?

Probate jurisdiction generally follows your legal domicile — not simply the state where you own a home, or the state where you spend the most months. If your domicile isn't clearly established, both Florida and your former home state can each assert a claim, which can mean disputes, duplicate proceedings, and unnecessary cost and delay for your family.

What clarifies Florida domicile: Steps like filing a Florida declaration of domicile, claiming the Florida homestead exemption under F.S. § 196.031, registering to vote in Florida, and titling your primary residence and estate documents consistently with Florida as your home base all help establish where you legally belong.

For real estate you still own in another state, a properly funded revocable living trust (Florida trusts are governed by F.S. Chapter 736) can hold that out-of-state property and avoid the need for a separate ancillary probate proceeding in that state after you pass. Without that step, your family may need to open probate in two states — one in Florida for your primary estate, and a second, ancillary proceeding wherever the other property sits.

Business owners: your operating agreement matters as much as your will

If you own a business — an LLC, a professional practice, a family company — your business interest is personal property under Florida law. That means without a properly drafted buy-sell agreement or clear succession terms in your operating agreement, your ownership interest passes through your estate under your will, or under Florida's intestacy statute if you have no will. Your surviving business partners may suddenly find themselves in business with your spouse, your children, or whoever inherits under default rules — not necessarily a workable outcome for anyone.

  • A buy-sell agreement sets the terms for what happens to your ownership share on death, disability, or retirement, and how it will be valued and funded.
  • Coordinating your operating agreement with your estate plan (rather than treating them as separate documents) closes the most common gap I see.
  • For owners doing lifetime gifting of business interests to the next generation, staying current on the federal gift and estate tax exemption amounts for the year in question is important — these figures adjust periodically, so confirm the current numbers with your attorney or CPA rather than relying on a past year's figures.
⚠ A word of caution: A revocable living trust is an excellent tool for avoiding probate on business interests, but by itself it does not reduce estate tax exposure. For owners with larger estates, irrevocable trust strategies are a separate conversation your attorney and tax advisor should have together.

Special-needs beneficiaries: protecting benefits while still providing an inheritance

If someone you love receives means-tested government benefits — Supplemental Security Income, Medicaid — an outright inheritance can unintentionally disqualify them. A properly structured special needs trust allows you to leave assets for that person's benefit — covering things public benefits don't, like therapies, equipment, travel, or quality-of-life expenses — without those trust assets counting against benefit eligibility limits.

This is a highly individualized area, and the details (whether the trust is first-party or third-party funded, how it interacts with a pooled trust, and how it's administered after you're gone) genuinely need attorney review specific to your family member's benefits and circumstances. I'd rather tell you that plainly than oversimplify it here.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my Florida will automatically leave everything to my spouse and cut out my kids from a prior marriage?
Not automatically, but Florida's elective share and homestead rules give a surviving spouse certain guaranteed rights regardless of what your will says, which is why blended-family plans need specific drafting rather than a standard will.
I split time between Florida and another state — which state handles my estate?
Generally, whichever state is your legal domicile, not simply where you spend the most time or own property. Establishing Florida domicile clearly (declaration of domicile, homestead exemption, voter registration) helps avoid a dispute between states after your death.
Do stepchildren inherit under Florida law if I don't have a will?
No. Under Florida's intestacy rules, stepchildren who were not legally adopted do not inherit automatically. If you want a stepchild to receive anything, they need to be specifically named in a valid will or trust.
What happens to my LLC or business interest if I die without a buy-sell agreement?
Your ownership interest is treated as personal property and passes through your estate under your will, or under Florida intestacy law if you have no will — which can leave your business partners unexpectedly co-owning with your heirs.
Will an inheritance affect my disabled family member's SSI or Medicaid?
It can, if left outright. A properly drafted special needs trust is generally the tool used to leave assets for their benefit without disqualifying them from means-tested government benefits — this requires individualized legal review.
Does a revocable living trust reduce estate taxes?
No. A revocable living trust helps your estate avoid probate, but it does not by itself reduce estate tax exposure. Tax-reduction strategies generally involve irrevocable trusts and should be discussed with both your attorney and tax advisor.

The Truestead Takeaway

If you fall into one of these categories — blended family, part-time Florida resident, business owner, or family with a special-needs beneficiary — the right question isn't whether you have an estate plan, but whether your plan actually accounts for the specific Florida rules that apply to your situation. These gaps are common, fixable, and best caught in a review rather than discovered by your family later. If any of this sounds like your circumstances, it's worth having a Florida attorney take a look at what you currently have in place.
