Broward County Probate Court: What Floridians Need to Know

Quick Answer

Broward County Probate Court is the Probate and Guardianship Division of the 17th Judicial Circuit Court in Fort Lauderdale, which handles the estates of anyone who lived in Broward County at death. As of July 1, 2026, more estates qualify for the faster summary administration process because the asset ceiling rose from $75,000 to $150,000.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney November 22, 2026

What Is Broward County Probate Court?

When someone asks me about Broward County probate court, what they usually mean is the Probate and Guardianship Division of Florida's Seventeenth Judicial Circuit Court. It's housed at the main courthouse at 201 S.E. 6th Street in Fort Lauderdale (West Building, 3rd Floor, Room 03160), and it's where probate is opened for anyone who was legally domiciled in Broward County at the time of death.

What makes Broward a bit unusual compared to some other Florida circuits is that one division handles both probate and guardianship matters, along with mental health, substance abuse, and related adult protective proceedings. If you're the personal representative of an estate, the family member of someone who's passed, or you're trying to figure out where to even start, this is the court whose procedures will shape your experience from day one.

All of this is governed by Florida's Probate Code, specifically Chapters 731 through 735 of the Florida Statutes. The Clerk of Court for Broward is Brenda D. Forman, and the probate division can be reached at (954) 831-7154.

Big Change in 2026: More Estates Now Qualify for Summary Administration

Here's the development every Broward family should know about. Under CS/HB 1337 (Chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida), which amended Florida Statute 735.201, the threshold for summary administration increased significantly effective July 1, 2026.

  • For deaths occurring on or after July 1, 2026, an estate can qualify for summary administration if the total value of non-exempt assets is $150,000 or less.
  • For deaths that occurred before July 1, 2026, the older $75,000 ceiling still applies.

Summary administration is the simplified, faster alternative to full (formal) probate. It skips the appointment of a personal representative in the traditional sense and generally resolves much faster than a formal estate. Doubling the threshold means a meaningful number of Broward estates that used to require the longer formal process may now qualify for the streamlined one, which can save both time and money.

Why this matters: If a loved one passed away with a modest estate, this change could mean the difference between a probate process measured in weeks rather than the better part of a year. Whether your situation qualifies still depends on the date of death, the nature of the assets, and whether debts of the estate are an issue, so it's worth having the numbers reviewed.

Formal vs. Summary Administration in Broward: Timelines and Costs

In my practice, clients want two things above all: how long will this take, and what will it cost. Here's the general picture for Broward:

  • Formal administration (used for larger or more complicated estates) typically runs 8 to 12 months from start to finish in a clean case with no major disputes.
  • Summary administration (now available to more estates under the new $150,000 threshold) typically resolves in 4 to 8 weeks when the estate qualifies and the paperwork is in order.

As for filing fees charged by the Broward Clerk of Court as of 2026, opening a formal administration costs $401, and summary administration for an estate valued at $1,000 or more costs $346. These are clerk filing fees only. They don't include attorney's fees, publication costs, or other administrative expenses that may apply depending on the estate.

How Do You File Probate in Broward County?

Broward's probate division has some procedural quirks that trip up people who try to file on their own or who are used to how another Florida county handles things.

  • Electronic filing is mandatory for attorneys. All probate documents must be submitted through the Florida Courts E-Filing Portal.
  • Broward requires its own 'Smart Forms.' The 17th Judicial Circuit maintains a specific set of Probate and Guardianship Smart Forms for most petitions. These are interactive PDF forms with built-in fields and logic, and a plain, non-interactive PDF submitted in their place will typically be rejected by the clerk outright.
  • The original will must reach the courthouse quickly. Florida law requires the custodian of a decedent's original will to deposit it with the clerk of the court in the county where the decedent resided, and Broward's local practice calls for this within 10 days of death.
  • Many routine hearings happen fast, by Zoom. Simple matters, like a petition to sell estate real property, are typically handled on the Uniform Motion Calendar (UMC), which consists of short, roughly 5-minute hearings conducted remotely.
⚠ A Common Mistake: Filing the wrong form, or a standard PDF instead of Broward's required Smart Form, is one of the most frequent reasons self-filed probate petitions get bounced back by the clerk, adding weeks to a case that should have moved quickly.

Which County's Probate Court Has Jurisdiction?

This trips up a lot of families, especially when a parent lived in Broward but passed away in a hospital in Miami-Dade, or when the heirs live out of state. Florida law is clear on this point: probate is opened in the county where the decedent was domiciled at the time of death, not where they happened to die, and not where their children or heirs currently live.

So if your loved one lived in Broward County, whether in Fort Lauderdale, Hollywood, Pembroke Pines, Coral Springs, or anywhere else in the county, Broward is almost always the correct venue, even if other circumstances point elsewhere. If the decedent owned real estate in Broward but was domiciled in another county (or another state entirely), a separate "ancillary" probate proceeding may be needed in Broward just to deal with that property, while the main estate is probated where the decedent actually lived.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where exactly is Broward County probate court located?
The Probate Division is at 201 S.E. 6th Street, Fort Lauderdale, FL 33301, in the West Building, 3rd Floor, Room 03160. You can reach the probate division by phone at (954) 831-7154.
Do I need a lawyer for Broward County probate?
Florida law generally requires formal administration estates with a personal representative to be represented by a Florida-licensed attorney, with limited exceptions. Summary administration has more flexibility, but given Broward's Smart Form filing requirements and e-filing rules, many families still find it faster and less stressful to have an attorney handle the process.
How long does probate take in Broward County?
A straightforward formal administration generally takes 8 to 12 months, while a qualifying summary administration can often be completed in 4 to 8 weeks. Timelines vary based on estate complexity, creditor claims, and court scheduling.
What changed with Florida's summary administration limit in 2026?
Effective July 1, 2026, under CS/HB 1337 (Chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida), the non-exempt asset threshold for summary administration rose from $75,000 to $150,000. Estates of decedents who died before July 1, 2026 are still evaluated under the old $75,000 ceiling.
Which county handles probate if my parent lived in Broward but died in a Miami hospital?
Probate is filed in the county of the decedent's domicile at death, not the county where death occurred. If your parent was domiciled in Broward County, Broward is almost always the correct venue.
What happens if I use the wrong probate form in Broward?
Broward requires specific interactive 'Smart Forms' for most probate petitions. Submitting a standard, non-interactive PDF in place of the required Smart Form typically results in the clerk rejecting the filing, which can delay the case.

The Truestead Takeaway

Broward County probate court follows the same Florida Probate Code as every other circuit, but it has its own local filing requirements, hearing procedures, and now a significantly more generous summary administration threshold as of mid-2026. Whether an estate you're handling qualifies for the faster, cheaper summary process, or needs full formal administration, depends on the date of death, the value and type of assets, and whether debts are involved. If you're facing a Broward probate matter, the sensible next step is to have your specific situation reviewed by a Florida probate attorney who can tell you which path applies and keep the filing on track from the start.
