How Much Will a Will Cost in Florida?

Quick Answer

A basic Florida will typically costs between $300 and $800, often bundled with a power of attorney and healthcare directives for under $1,000. A full estate plan with a revocable living trust usually runs $2,000 to $7,000 or more, depending on complexity.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney November 25, 2026

How Much Will a Basic Will Cost in Florida?

If you searched "how much will" hoping for a straight dollar figure, here it is: in my practice and across Florida generally, a basic individual will runs somewhere in the neighborhood of $300 to $700. For a will with straightforward wishes, such as leaving everything to a spouse, splitting an estate among children, or naming a guardian for minor children, many Florida attorneys price a complete package (will, financial power of attorney, healthcare surrogate designation, and living will) at roughly $400 to $800, sometimes bundled for under $1,000 total.

That price range isn't arbitrary. It reflects the attorney's time to meet with you, understand your family situation, draft documents that meet Florida's execution formalities, and supervise proper signing. A will that isn't signed correctly under Florida law can be thrown out in probate, so the value isn't just the document, it's getting it right the first time.

How Much Will a Full Estate Plan or Trust Cost?

A will alone often isn't the whole answer for Florida homeowners, retirees, and families with real estate or more than one beneficiary situation to manage. A more complete plan built around a revocable living trust under Florida Statutes Chapter 736 is common for people who want to avoid probate, manage incapacity smoothly, or coordinate a Florida homestead with out-of-state property.

  • Simple revocable trust (one or two properties, standard beneficiaries, no special conditions): often $1,500 to $2,500.
  • Full bundled estate plan (trust, pour-over will, durable power of attorney under Chapter 709, healthcare surrogate under section 765.202, Florida Statutes, and living will under section 765.301, Florida Statutes): commonly $2,000 to $7,000 or more, depending on the number of properties, blended family considerations, special needs planning, or business interests involved.

What drives the price up isn't paperwork volume, it's complexity: multiple marriages, minor or special-needs beneficiaries, out-of-state property, closely held businesses, or family dynamics that need careful drafting to avoid future disputes.

What Makes a Florida Will Legally Valid (Regardless of Cost)?

Whatever you pay, a Florida will is only worth something if it's executed correctly. Under section 732.502, Florida Statutes, a valid will must be:

  • In writing,
  • Signed by the testator (the person making the will), or by someone else in the testator's presence and at their direction, and
  • Signed by two witnesses, who must sign in the testator's presence and in each other's presence.
⚠ A cheap will isn't a bargain if it's invalid. I've seen families discover, only after a loved one's death, that a will wasn't witnessed properly and is therefore unenforceable. Florida's signing formalities are strict, and a DIY or out-of-state form that doesn't meet section 732.502 can leave an estate treated as if no will existed at all.

This is one reason the cost of attorney involvement buys more than drafting time: it buys supervised execution, which protects the document from later challenge.

What Happens (and What It Costs) If You Die Without a Will in Florida?

Some Floridians ask "how much will" really meaning: what does it cost me if I skip the will entirely? The answer is that dying without a will, called dying intestate, doesn't save money, it shifts the cost and control elsewhere. Florida Statutes Chapter 732 sets the state's intestate succession rules, meaning the Legislature, not you, decides who inherits. Generally, assets pass to a surviving spouse and children in shares set by statute, and if there's no spouse or children, the law works through a fixed order of other relatives.

Without a will, your estate typically still goes through probate, often formal administration, which can involve more court supervision, more time, and more attorney and court costs than if you'd had a will directing a simpler process. Florida also offers a streamlined summary administration process for smaller estates, but eligibility depends on the value of the estate and how much time has passed since death, under thresholds set in Chapter 732. If you're unsure whether your estate would qualify, that's a detail worth confirming with a Florida probate attorney rather than assuming.

Why Do Will and Estate Plan Prices Vary So Much in Florida?

Clients often ask why one Florida firm quotes $400 for a will and another quotes $4,000 for a plan. The honest answer is that these aren't the same product. A flat-fee basic will serves someone with a simple family structure and modest assets. A comprehensive plan built around a trust, powers of attorney, and healthcare documents serves someone trying to avoid probate, protect a homestead, manage incapacity, or address a blended family or special needs beneficiary.

What actually drives Florida pricing: number of beneficiaries, whether you own Florida homestead property (protected under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution and devised under section 732.4015, Florida Statutes), whether a trust is involved, blended family or guardianship considerations, and whether the attorney bills flat-fee or hourly.

There is no Florida estate tax, which is good news, but federal estate tax exposure is a separate question for larger estates and depends on the current federal exclusion amount, which changes periodically at the federal level.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much will a simple will cost in Florida?
A basic individual will in Florida commonly costs between $300 and $700, with many attorneys bundling it with a power of attorney and healthcare directives for under $1,000 total.
How much will a living trust cost compared to a will?
A simple revocable living trust typically costs more upfront, often $1,500 to $2,500, while a bundled estate plan including a trust and related documents can run $2,000 to $7,000 or more depending on complexity.
Is it cheaper to skip a will entirely in Florida?
Not usually. Dying without a will means Florida's intestate succession rules under Chapter 732 decide who inherits, and the estate typically still goes through probate, sometimes a more involved process than if a will had directed a simpler path.
Does a Florida will have to be notarized?
Florida law under section 732.502 requires a will to be signed by the testator and witnessed by two people, but many Florida wills also include a self-proving affidavit, which does require notarization, to simplify probate later.
Are online will templates valid in Florida?
A document can only be a valid Florida will if it meets the signing and witnessing requirements of section 732.502, Florida Statutes. Online templates vary widely in whether they guide users to execute the document correctly, so errors in signing are a real risk worth having reviewed.
Will Florida estate planning costs change in 2026?
Some marketing sources reference 2026 changes to probate thresholds, but no such statutory amendment to Chapter 732 could be confirmed in official Florida Legislature records at the time of writing. Readers should confirm current thresholds with a Florida attorney rather than rely on unverified claims.

The Truestead Takeaway

The honest answer to "how much will" a Florida will cost is that it depends on what you actually need: a simple will for a straightforward family situation is a modest, worthwhile expense, while a full plan with a trust and incapacity documents costs more because it does more. What matters most isn't the price tag, it's whether the document is valid under Florida law and actually fits your family and your property, including any Florida homestead. The sensible next step is a review of your specific situation with a Florida estate planning attorney, so you pay for the plan you need, not more and not less.
