How Much Does a Medicaid Planner Cost in Florida?

Quick Answer

Most Florida families pay somewhere between $2,500 and $10,000 or more for a full Medicaid planning engagement, depending on how complex the case is, and that fee is almost always paid as a flat rate rather than by the hour. In Florida, this work can only be legally performed by a licensed attorney, which is a key difference from many other states.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney October 14, 2026

So, How Much Does a Medicaid Planner Cost?

If you're asking this question, you or a parent is probably facing a nursing home or assisted living bill, and you want to know what it costs to get help protecting savings while qualifying for long-term care Medicaid. Here's the honest range: in my practice and across Florida generally, a full Medicaid planning engagement typically runs from about $2,500 to $10,000 or more, with many average cases falling in the middle of that range. Simple cases (a single applicant with modest, straightforward assets) tend to cost less. Complex cases (a married couple, a family business, life insurance with cash value, long-term care policies, or a desire to protect a home for heirs) cost more.

Nationally, some Medicaid planning services charge $300 to $600 per hour, or a flat fee anywhere from roughly $3,000 to $15,000 depending on the state and complexity. Initial consultations are frequently free, or in the $150 to $300 range if there is a charge. Most Florida elder law attorneys prefer flat fees over hourly billing for this work, because clients understandably don't want an open-ended bill while they're already worried about a parent's care.

One benchmark I've seen used in the field: for an average case, the planning fee is often comparable to roughly one month's nursing home bill. With Florida's average cost of care running well over $8,000 a month for assisted living (and skilled nursing often higher), that gives you a realistic sense of scale, and it also explains why the fee is usually worth it: the planning can protect many months or years of savings that would otherwise be spent down.

Why Florida Is Different: Only Attorneys Can Legally Do This Work

This is the single most important thing for a Florida family to understand, and it's a big reason costs here look the way they do. In many states, non-lawyer "Medicaid planners" or "Certified Medicaid Planners" can legally sell planning services. Florida does not allow that. The Florida Supreme Court has made clear that key aspects of Medicaid planning, including drafting a personal service contract or determining the need for and preparing a Qualified Income Trust, constitute the practice of law. Only a licensed Florida attorney may perform that work.

⚠ Unauthorized Practice of Law Warning A non-lawyer who provides this kind of Medicaid planning advice or document preparation in Florida is committing a third-degree felony under Florida law. If someone offers you Medicaid planning services and isn't a licensed Florida attorney, that should stop you cold. Beyond the legal risk, you could end up with documents that don't actually protect you, filed by someone with no accountability to The Florida Bar.

Because only attorneys may do this work in Florida, you won't find the cheaper non-attorney "planner" tier that exists in some other states. What you're paying for is a licensed elder law attorney's judgment, document preparation, and the protection of the attorney-client relationship and malpractice coverage that comes with it.

What Actually Drives the Cost Up or Down

In my practice, I tell clients the fee isn't arbitrary, it tracks directly to how much work and risk is involved in the case. Factors that typically affect cost include:

  • Marital status: planning for a married couple, where one spouse needs care and the other (the "community spouse") stays in the home, is more involved than planning for a single applicant.
  • Total countable assets: more assets generally means more planning tools are needed (trusts, annuities, asset conversions) to get below Florida's asset limits.
  • Home ownership: Florida's homestead is generally an exempt asset for Medicaid purposes, but protecting it for heirs while qualifying the applicant often requires additional planning.
  • Income above the cap: if monthly income exceeds Florida's income limit, a Qualified Income Trust (sometimes called a "Miller Trust") is required, which only an attorney may prepare in Florida.
  • Veterans' benefits: coordinating Medicaid planning with VA Aid and Attendance benefits adds complexity and often value.
  • Life insurance and annuities: cash-value policies and certain annuities count as assets and may need to be restructured.

As of 2026, Florida's Medicaid income limit for long-term care is $2,982 per month for an individual, and the asset limit is $2,000 for a single applicant and $3,000 for a couple applying together, with the healthy spouse allowed to keep up to $162,660 in countable assets. These numbers explain why so much planning is needed: most middle-class retirees have assets well above these limits, and the legal strategies to bridge that gap (without triggering penalties) are exactly what you're paying the attorney to design and execute correctly.

The 5-Year Look-Back Period, and Why It Makes Planning Worth the Fee

Florida Medicaid applies a 60-month (5-year) look-back period on asset transfers under Florida Statute § 409.9101. That means the state reviews financial records going back five years from the application date, and any gifts or below-market transfers made during that window can trigger a penalty period during which Medicaid won't pay for care. This is precisely why rushed, do-it-yourself asset transfers (giving the house to a child the month before applying, for example) so often backfire.

Why the fee pays for itself A properly structured plan, done early and correctly by a licensed attorney, can legally protect assets that would otherwise be entirely spent down on care, sometimes tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars, while still qualifying the applicant for Medicaid. Measured against that, a one-time flat fee in the thousands of dollars is often a small fraction of what it preserves.

Fees are commonly structured so they're paid from what elder law practitioners call "spend-down," meaning the money used to pay the attorney comes from funds that would otherwise have gone toward the cost of care anyway. This is one reason flat fees, agreed to upfront, tend to feel more manageable to families than open-ended hourly billing.

Free and Low-Cost Help Before You Pay for Full Planning

Not every family needs a full paid engagement right away. In Florida, there are free resources worth checking first:

  • Public benefits counselors and caseworkers can help with basic Medicaid application questions at no charge.
  • Nonprofits and advocacy organizations, including groups affiliated with the American Council on Aging, offer general education and application guidance for free.
  • Many Florida elder law attorneys offer a free or low-cost initial consultation specifically to assess whether your situation needs formal planning at all, or whether you can apply directly.

If your assets are already below Florida's limits, or your situation is simple, you may not need paid planning at all. Paid Medicaid planning tends to make the most financial sense when there are meaningful countable assets to protect, a home to preserve for a spouse or heirs, or income above the cap requiring a Qualified Income Trust.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it legal to use a non-attorney Medicaid planner in Florida?
No. The Florida Supreme Court has held that key Medicaid planning tasks, such as drafting personal service contracts and Qualified Income Trusts, constitute the practice of law. A non-lawyer performing this work in Florida commits a third-degree felony, so only a licensed Florida attorney should be engaged for this service.
Do I pay the Medicaid planning attorney hourly or a flat fee?
Most Florida elder law attorneys and clients prefer a flat fee agreed upon upfront, rather than hourly billing, because it gives the family a defined, predictable cost during an already stressful time.
Will the Medicaid planning fee come out of my loved one's care budget?
Often, yes. Fees are frequently paid from what's called spend-down, meaning funds that would have gone toward paying for care are instead used to pay for the planning that ultimately protects other assets.
What is Florida's Medicaid look-back period?
Florida Medicaid reviews financial transactions going back 60 months (5 years) from the application date under Florida Statute 409.9101. Gifts or transfers made during that window can create a penalty period that delays Medicaid eligibility, which is why early, properly structured planning matters.
Is my home safe if I apply for Florida Medicaid?
A primary residence is generally treated as an exempt asset for Florida Medicaid eligibility purposes, though the specific facts of ownership, equity, and intent to return home can matter, and this should be reviewed with an attorney given your situation.
What are Florida's current Medicaid income and asset limits?
As of 2026, the income limit is $2,982 per month for an individual applicant, and the asset limit is $2,000 for a single applicant, $3,000 for a couple applying together, with the non-applicant spouse generally permitted to retain up to $162,660 in countable assets.

The Truestead Takeaway

The fee for Medicaid planning in Florida isn't arbitrary, it reflects the complexity of your situation and the fact that only a licensed attorney may lawfully perform this work in our state. Before you pay anyone for Medicaid planning, confirm you're speaking with a Florida-licensed attorney, and consider starting with a consultation to find out whether your situation truly needs full paid planning or something simpler. In my practice, that conversation costs nothing and often saves families far more than the planning fee itself. If you or a family member is facing a nursing home or assisted living admission, it's worth having your specific facts reviewed by a Florida elder law attorney sooner rather than later, given the five-year look-back period.
