Do You Actually Need a Probate Attorney in Florida?

The honest answer is: it depends on the size and type of the estate, but for most formal probate cases in Florida, yes. Under Florida law, a personal representative handling a formal administration generally must be represented by a Florida-licensed attorney, unless the personal representative is the sole beneficiary of the estate. This isn't a sales pitch, it's a procedural requirement built into how Florida courts handle estates under Florida Statutes Chapter 733.

  • Formal administration: Used for most larger or more complex estates. This is the process most people picture when they think of "probate," and it almost always requires an attorney.
  • Summary administration: A shorter, simpler process under Florida Statutes Chapter 735, available when the estate's non-exempt assets fall under a statutory threshold, or when the person has been deceased for more than two years. Summary administration can sometimes be handled with less attorney involvement, though many families still choose to have counsel review the paperwork.
  • Disposition without administration: A very limited option for estates with minimal assets that mainly cover final expenses.

Because Indian River County's probate division, like every Florida county, has specific local filing requirements, an attorney familiar with Sebastian-area probate practice can help make sure nothing gets kicked back for missing paperwork.

How Florida Probate Actually Works

Florida probate is governed primarily by Florida Statutes Chapters 731 through 735. Here's the general flow for a formal administration:

  • Filing the petition: The personal representative (sometimes called an executor) files a petition with the circuit court, along with the original will if one exists.
  • Appointment of the personal representative: The court issues "Letters of Administration," giving the personal representative legal authority to act on behalf of the estate.
  • Notice to creditors: The personal representative must publish and often directly notify known creditors. Creditors generally have a short window, typically within three months of proper notice, to file claims against the estate.
  • Inventory and administration: The personal representative identifies, values, and manages estate assets, pays valid debts and taxes, and prepares for distribution.
  • Distribution and closing: Once debts and claims are resolved, remaining assets are distributed to beneficiaries or heirs, and the estate is formally closed with the court.

If the deceased person did not leave a valid will, Florida's intestacy laws under Chapter 732 determine who inherits, based on the family relationships involved (spouse, children, parents, and so on).

What Happens to the Family Home in Probate?

In Sebastian, as in much of Florida, the family home is often the single largest asset in an estate, and it comes with special protections. Under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, homestead property receives significant creditor protection, meaning most creditors of the estate cannot force a sale of a validly protected homestead to satisfy their claims.

Florida Statute 732.4015 also restricts how homestead property can be devised (left to someone in a will) when the deceased is survived by a spouse or minor children. These restrictions exist to protect surviving family members, but they can create real complications if a will doesn't account for them properly. This is one of the most common areas where I see families need experienced guidance, because the rules around homestead in probate are more nuanced than most people expect.

Good to know: Not every asset goes through probate. Assets held jointly with rights of survivorship, accounts with valid beneficiary designations, and property held in a properly funded trust typically pass outside the probate process entirely.

Deadlines and Timing You Should Know

Florida law sets real deadlines in probate matters, and missing them can complicate an estate. Generally speaking:

  • A will must be admitted to probate within two years of the date of death, unless the law provides otherwise for a specific circumstance.
  • Creditors who receive proper notice typically have a short window, often around three months, to file claims against the estate.
  • Summary administration may be available if more than two years have passed since the date of death, even without meeting the smaller asset threshold.
⚠ A note on personal injury and wrongful death claims: If the estate involves a potential injury or wrongful death claim connected to the decedent, the deadlines and rules are different from routine probate matters, and Florida's personal injury laws have changed in recent years. Anyone in that situation should confirm current deadlines directly with a Florida attorney rather than relying on older information.

The length of a typical probate case varies widely depending on the size of the estate, whether there are disputes among heirs, and how quickly creditors and tax matters are resolved. Simple summary administrations can move relatively quickly, while formal administrations with real estate, business interests, or family disagreements can take considerably longer.

What a Sebastian Probate Consultation Typically Covers

When a Sebastian-area family comes to me after a loved one has passed, the first conversation usually covers the same core ground: whether a will exists and where the original is located, what assets the estate includes and roughly what they're worth, whether real estate (especially a homestead) is involved, whether there are known creditors or disputes among family members, and what the client's goals are for timing and involvement.

From there, I can generally tell a family whether their situation looks like a candidate for summary administration, formal administration, or something else entirely, and what documents we'll need to get started. Many Florida probate matters today can be handled with significant flexibility, including remote consultations and electronic document execution, which can be especially helpful for out-of-town family members or snowbirds who split time between Florida and elsewhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a probate lawyer cost in Sebastian, Florida?
Probate attorney fees in Florida vary based on the size and complexity of the estate, and some fee arrangements are tied to the value of the estate while others are hourly or flat fee. It's best to ask any attorney you're considering for a clear explanation of how their fees work before you hire them.
Can I handle probate in Florida without a lawyer?
It depends on the type of administration. Formal administration generally requires a Florida-licensed attorney unless the personal representative is the sole beneficiary, while summary administration and disposition without administration sometimes involve less mandatory attorney involvement, though many families still choose to have a lawyer review the process.
How long does probate take in Florida?
There's no single answer. Summary administration for smaller, simpler estates can move relatively quickly, while formal administration, especially with real estate, multiple heirs, or creditor claims, typically takes longer. An attorney can give you a more specific estimate once they understand your family's situation.
What happens if my Sebastian family member died without a will?
If someone dies without a valid will in Florida, their estate is distributed according to the state's intestacy laws under Florida Statutes Chapter 732, which determine inheritance based on surviving family relationships such as a spouse, children, or parents.
Does the family home have to go through probate in Sebastian?
It depends on how the property was titled and whether homestead protections apply. Homestead property often receives special constitutional protection from creditors and is subject to specific distribution rules under Florida law, so it's worth having an attorney review the title and any will language carefully.
What's the deadline to file a will for probate in Florida?
Generally, a will must be admitted to probate within two years of the date of death unless the law provides an exception for the circumstances involved.
