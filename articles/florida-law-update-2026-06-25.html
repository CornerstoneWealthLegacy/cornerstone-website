Tracking Florida Estate, Elder & Property Law: The Durable Framework Behind Every Change

Quick Answer

Florida's core estate, elder law, and property rules live in the probate code (Chapters 731–735), the Florida Trust Code (Chapter 736), and the homestead provisions of the Florida Constitution. Knowing this framework lets you spot real legislative changes—and ignore the noise.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney June 25, 2026

Why I'm giving you the framework instead of a 'breaking news' headline

Every legislative season, Florida headlines promise that estate or property law has been turned upside down. In my practice, most of those stories describe small amendments to a legal structure that has stayed remarkably stable for decades. So before chasing the latest bill number, I want Florida readers to understand the durable framework that governs their plan. When you know where the rules actually live, you can quickly judge whether a reported change is a genuine shift or just noise.

A quick but honest note: I was not able to confirm a specific brand-new 2026 statute or court decision from verified primary sources for this article, so I'm not going to assert one. Instead, here is the definitive explainer—the legal foundations that almost every Florida estate, elder law, probate, and real estate question turns on, and how to check for real updates yourself.

Where Florida estate and trust law actually lives

If you want to track changes intelligently, it helps to know which chapters of Florida law matter. Here are the ones I reach for most:

  • Wills and probate — Florida's probate code, Chapter 732 (and the surrounding 731–735 chapters), governs how wills work, who inherits when there's no will, and how an estate is administered.
  • Will execution — F.S. § 732.502 sets the formalities: a Florida will generally must be signed by the testator and witnessed by two witnesses who sign in each other's and the testator's presence.
  • Revocable living trusts — the Florida Trust Code, Chapter 736, governs how trusts are created, administered, and challenged.
  • Durable power of attorney — Chapter 709 controls how you appoint someone to handle financial matters if you can't.
  • Healthcare surrogate and living will — F.S. § 765.202 (surrogate) and F.S. § 765.301 (living will) cover medical decision-making and end-of-life wishes.

When a news story says "Florida changed the rules on trusts," the real question is: which section of Chapter 736 was amended, and what is the effective date? That's how a lawyer separates a meaningful change from a headline.

Homestead: the Florida feature that surprises newcomers

Nothing defines Florida property and estate law quite like homestead. It does three different jobs, and people often confuse them:

  • Creditor protection — Article X, § 4 of the Florida Constitution shields your homestead from most creditors, within acreage limits.
  • Property tax benefits — the homestead exemption (Art. VII, § 6, Fla. Const., and F.S. § 196.031) and the Save Our Homes assessment cap (F.S. § 193.155) limit how fast your taxable value rises, with portability allowing you to carry savings to a new Florida home.
  • Restrictions on devise — F.S. § 732.4015 limits how you can leave a homestead in your will if you have a surviving spouse or minor child. This catches many people off guard: you may not be able to leave the house to whomever you choose.
Why this matters for snowbirds: Homestead protections generally attach to your permanent Florida residence. If Florida isn't your primary home, you may not qualify—so where you establish residency has real legal consequences.

Personal injury deadlines changed—verify before you rely on old advice

If your interest in Florida law extends to personal injury, here's a caution I give everyone: Florida's 2023 tort reform (HB 837) materially changed the landscape, including the negligence statute of limitations and a shift to a modified comparative-negligence standard where a plaintiff found more than 50% at fault generally cannot recover.

Because deadlines and bars are unforgiving, I won't restate a specific limitations period here from memory. The honest, lawyerly answer is to confirm the current deadline that applies to your particular type of claim with a Florida attorney before relying on anything—including older articles still floating online.

⚠ Don't trust outdated PI deadlines: Much of the personal injury content online predates the 2023 reforms. If a source doesn't reflect the post-HB 837 rules, treat its deadlines as unreliable and verify with current Florida law.

How to track real Florida legal changes (and skip the hype)

You don't need a law license to follow Florida legislation responsibly. Here's the approach I'd recommend:

  • Watch the Florida Senate (flsenate.gov) and Florida House (myfloridahouse.gov) bill pages during and after each legislative session.
  • Check the official Florida Statutes at the Florida Legislature's site to confirm the actual current text and effective date.
  • Follow The Florida Bar's Real Property, Probate & Trust Law (RPPTL) Section, which tracks proposals affecting estate and property practice.
  • For elder law, watch AHCA / Florida Medicaid materials on long-term care eligibility and estate recovery.

The golden rule: a change isn't real until you can point to the amended statute and its effective date. A bill that's merely filed is not law.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did Florida pass a major new estate planning law in 2026?
I couldn't confirm a specific new 2026 statute from verified primary sources for this article, so I won't assert one. Check flsenate.gov, myfloridahouse.gov, and the official Florida Statutes for the current text and effective dates, or ask a Florida attorney.
How many witnesses does a Florida will need?
Under F.S. § 732.502, a Florida will generally must be signed by the testator and by two witnesses, each signing in the presence of the testator and of one another. Formalities matter, so the document should be reviewed by an attorney.
Can I leave my Florida homestead to anyone I want in my will?
Not always. F.S. § 732.4015 restricts how homestead can be devised when there's a surviving spouse or a minor child, which can override what your will says. This is a common surprise and worth reviewing.
Do snowbirds get Florida's homestead tax benefits?
Generally, homestead tax benefits and Save Our Homes apply to your permanent Florida residence, not a seasonal one. Where you establish residency affects whether you qualify.
Did the deadline to file a Florida personal injury lawsuit change?
Florida's 2023 tort reform (HB 837) made material changes to negligence deadlines and adopted a modified comparative-negligence bar. Verify the current limitations period for your specific claim with a Florida attorney before relying on older sources.
How do I tell if a reported legal 'change' is real?
Confirm the amended statute number and its effective date in the official Florida Statutes. A bill that's only been filed or proposed is not yet law.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida estate, elder law, probate, and property rules rest on a stable framework—the probate code (Chapters 731–735), the Trust Code (Chapter 736), the powers and healthcare statutes (Chapters 709 and 765), and the constitutional homestead provisions. Legislative tweaks happen, but the smart move is to know that framework, verify any reported change against the official statute and its effective date, and have your own plan reviewed periodically by a Florida attorney rather than reacting to headlines.
