What Estate Planning Looks Like In Florida (2026 Update)

Quick Answer

In Florida, a valid estate plan generally means a properly signed will or trust, a durable power of attorney, and healthcare directives, and in 2026 the state made probate noticeably easier by raising the summary administration limit to $150,000 and giving personal representatives new tools to deal with uncooperative banks.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney October 15, 2026

What "Estate Planning In Florida" Actually Means

When people search "in Florida" alongside estate planning questions, they're usually trying to figure out one simple thing: does Florida do this differently than other states? The honest answer is yes, often significantly. Florida has its own execution rules for wills, its own probate court system, strong constitutional protections for homestead property, and (as of mid-2026) a freshly updated probate code that makes settling smaller estates faster and cheaper than it used to be.

In my practice, I tell clients that a complete Florida estate plan usually includes four pieces: a last will and testament (or a revocable trust used in place of, or alongside, a will), a durable power of attorney for financial decisions, a healthcare surrogate designation, and a living will. Each one answers a different "what if" question, and together they cover incapacity planning as well as what happens after death.

How You Sign a Valid Will In Florida

Florida is strict about the mechanics of signing a will, and getting this wrong is one of the most common reasons a will gets challenged. Under F.S. § 732.502, a valid Florida will must be signed by you (or signed on your behalf, in your presence and at your direction) and then signed by two witnesses, who must sign in your presence and in each other's presence.

Most Florida wills are also made "self-proving." This means you and your witnesses sign a short affidavit in front of a notary at the same time you sign the will. A self-proving will doesn't change what the will says, but it allows the probate court to accept the will without tracking down your witnesses later to testify that the signing was valid, which saves real time and expense for your family.

Florida has also authorized qualified electronic wills since January 1, 2020, under a specific set of statutes governing remote online notarization and electronic signing. Electronic wills have narrower, more technical requirements than paper wills, so if you're considering one, that's a conversation worth having with a Florida attorney rather than a DIY project.

Florida's 2026 Probate Changes: What's New

Florida significantly updated its probate code in 2026, and if you searched "in Florida" hoping for the most current answer, this is likely the most important development for you.

  • Summary administration threshold doubled. Florida offers a simplified probate process called summary administration for smaller estates. That threshold was raised from $75,000 to $150,000 in assets subject to administration (not counting property exempt from creditors), meaning many more estates now qualify for the faster, cheaper process instead of full formal administration.
  • Small-estate collection without probate increased. Certain small intestate estates that can pass with no formal administration at all saw their threshold rise from $10,000 to $20,000.
  • Easier access to tax refunds. A surviving spouse or child can now collect up to $5,000 of a decedent's income tax refund without opening a probate estate, up from the prior $2,500 limit.
  • New power to enforce letters of administration. A new statute gives personal representatives a direct court remedy when a bank or financial institution refuses to honor valid letters of administration or demands extra, legally unnecessary paperwork.
Why this matters: If you handled a Florida probate years ago and found it slow or frustrating, the process for smaller and mid-sized estates is meaningfully more efficient now. It's worth having your estate plan reviewed to see whether these new thresholds change how your assets will pass to your family.

Does Florida Have an Estate Tax?

No. Florida does not impose a state estate tax, inheritance tax, or state income tax, and this has been true for years, not just a 2026 development. This is one of the genuine advantages of Florida residency for estate planning purposes.

That said, federal estate tax law still applies to very large estates, and the federal exemption amount is set by Congress, not Florida. If your estate (or your combined marital estate) is large enough that federal estate tax could ever be a factor, that threshold has changed recently at the federal level, and you should confirm the current federal exemption amount with your attorney or CPA rather than relying on an older figure.

Spousal and Homestead Protections Unique to Florida

Florida law gives surviving spouses protections that many other states don't offer in the same form. Two stand out:

  • Elective share. Under F.S. § 732.2065, a surviving spouse is generally entitled to claim a share of the deceased spouse's "elective estate" (a defined set of assets, broader than just the probate estate) regardless of what the will says. This exists so one spouse can't simply write the other out entirely.
  • Homestead protection. Florida's homestead protections, rooted in Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, restrict how a homestead can be devised when the owner is survived by a spouse or minor child, under F.S. § 732.4015. Homestead property is also generally protected from most creditor claims, which is a major reason Florida is attractive to retirees.
⚠ A common mistake: Leaving your homestead to someone other than your spouse or minor children, assuming your will controls, can run into these restrictions. If you're survived by a spouse, Florida law may override what your will says about the homestead. This is exactly the kind of issue a plan review catches before it becomes a problem for your family.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a will if I live in Florida?
You're not legally required to have one, but without a valid will, Florida's intestacy laws (not your personal wishes) decide who inherits your property, which often doesn't match what most families actually want.
How long does probate take in Florida now?
It depends on the size and complexity of the estate, but the 2026 increase in the summary administration threshold to $150,000 means many more Florida estates now qualify for the faster, simplified process instead of full formal administration.
Who has to file the will with the court after someone dies in Florida?
Under Florida law, the person who has custody of the original will (usually the named executor or a close family member) must deposit it with the clerk of court within 10 days of learning of the death, even if no probate case is opened right away.
Can I write my own will in Florida?
You can, but Florida's execution requirements are strict (proper signing by you and two witnesses in each other's presence), and a technical error can make the will invalid, so many Florida residents have an attorney prepare or at least review it.
Does Florida recognize electronic wills?
Yes, Florida has authorized qualified electronic wills since 2020, but they involve specific technical and notarization requirements that differ from traditional paper wills.
Is there still an estate tax in Florida?
No, Florida does not have a state estate tax or state income tax; only very large estates may ever face federal estate tax, and that federal exemption threshold is set separately by Congress.

The Truestead Takeaway

Florida's 2026 probate reforms are genuinely good news for most families: smaller and mid-sized estates can now move through a faster process, and personal representatives have more leverage when banks drag their feet. But the fundamentals haven't changed: your will still has to be signed correctly, your homestead still has its own set of rules if you're survived by a spouse or minor child, and incapacity planning (power of attorney, healthcare surrogate, living will) matters just as much as what happens after death. If your documents are more than a few years old, or you've never put a plan in place, it's worth having a Florida attorney review where things stand against the current law rather than assuming an older plan, or no plan, still fits.
