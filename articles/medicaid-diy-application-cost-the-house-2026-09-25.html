The DIY Medicaid Application That Cost a Florida Family the House

Quick Answer

A composite Florida family, the Baxters of Deltona, filled out their mother's Medicaid application without guidance, answered the transfer question incorrectly, and left off the intent-to-return statement. The result was a denial, a penalty period once corrected, and later an estate recovery claim on the home that a properly drafted lady bird deed would have avoided.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney September 24, 2026

Scene One: The Application

In my practice I meet a lot of families like the Baxters. I want to say up front that the Baxters are a composite I use to illustrate patterns I've seen play out again and again in Central Florida, not an actual client, but every mistake in this story is one I have seen a real family make.

Pam Baxter sat down at her mother Joan's kitchen table in Deltona with a stack of Department of Children and Families paperwork and her mother's checkbook register going back two years. Joan needed nursing home care and the family knew Medicaid would eventually have to help pay for it. Pam was smart, organized, and determined to save the family the cost of an elder law attorney. She had read online that Florida's Medicaid application was mostly a matter of listing assets and income.

What Pam did not fully understand was the application's question about any transfer of assets in the look-back period. A few years earlier, Joan had added Pam's name to a certificate of deposit for convenience, so Pam could help pay bills if Joan became unable to. Pam answered the transfer question as though nothing had happened, because in her mind nothing really had. But adding a child's name to an account is treated by Medicaid as a transfer of that asset, and it should have been disclosed and explained.

Pam also left blank the section asking about the family home and Joan's intent regarding it. She assumed that because Joan was in the hospital and unlikely to ever go home, there was no honest answer to give. In fact, Florida allows an applicant to state an intent to return home even when a full recovery is unlikely, and that single statement is often what keeps the homestead an exempt, non-countable asset during the applicant's lifetime.

Scene Two: The Denial and Reapplication

Six weeks later the denial notice arrived. The caseworker flagged the undisclosed CD transfer and requested additional documentation the Baxters did not have readily organized: three years of bank statements, an explanation of the joint account, and proof of how the funds had been used. Because the intent-to-return statement was missing, the caseworker also treated the home's status as unresolved, which slowed everything further.

Pam reapplied, this time disclosing the joint account transfer. But by then she had lost real time, and Joan's nursing home bill kept accruing at a rate many Florida families find genuinely alarming, often well over ten thousand dollars a month. The Baxters were paying privately out of Joan's savings while they sorted out the paperwork, which meant fewer resources were left by the time benefits were finally approved.

What a Fix Would Have Looked Like: A short consultation before filing could have flagged the joint CD as a reportable transfer, prepared a clear explanation and paper trail showing the funds were never a gift, and ensured the intent-to-return form was signed and filed with the original application rather than added later under pressure.

Scene Three: The Approval With a Penalty

When Medicaid ultimately approved Joan's benefits, it imposed a penalty period tied to the value of the joint CD transfer, treating a portion of it as an uncompensated transfer even though Pam had never actually taken the money for herself. During the penalty period, Medicaid would not pay for Joan's care, and the family had to cover the gap privately.

This is one of the costliest and most common outcomes of a self-filed application. The five-year look-back period exists precisely to catch transfers like this, and Medicaid does not care whether the family's intentions were innocent. A transfer is a transfer unless it is properly explained, documented, or unwound. An elder law attorney reviewing Joan's finances in advance likely would have caught the joint account issue early and either restructured it or prepared supporting documentation showing it was for convenience only, potentially avoiding the penalty altogether or minimizing it substantially.

The Baxters also never explored whether any of Joan's spend-down could have gone toward exempt purchases, things like prepaying funeral expenses, home repairs, or a reliable vehicle, all of which can reduce countable assets without triggering a transfer penalty. That opportunity passed once the crisis was already underway.

Scene Four: The Letter After Death

Joan passed away roughly two years after entering care. Several months later, the Baxter family received a letter from Florida's Medicaid estate recovery program. Because Joan had received Medicaid benefits after age fifty five, the state had a legal right under Florida law (Section 409.9101, Florida Statutes) to seek recovery from her estate, and the house in Deltona was still titled in Joan's name alone.

Florida law does exempt a homestead from estate recovery when it passes to a qualifying heir at law, such as a child. But because the home went through Joan's probate estate rather than passing automatically outside of probate, the family had to open a probate case, prove the homestead exemption applied, and delay closing out the estate while the claim was resolved. It worked out in the end, but it cost time, legal fees, and months of uncertainty during an already difficult period.

⚠ What a Lady Bird Deed Would Have Changed: Had Joan signed an enhanced life estate deed, commonly called a lady bird deed, years earlier, the home would have passed directly to Pam and her siblings outside of probate at Joan's death. That kind of deed lets the owner keep full control and the right to sell or change beneficiaries during life, while removing the home from the probate estate afterward, which generally keeps it outside the reach of a Medicaid estate recovery claim entirely.

When DIY Is Fine, and When It Isn't

Not every Medicaid application needs an attorney. A single applicant with very simple finances, no recent transfers, no jointly titled accounts, and a clear, uncomplicated home situation may be able to file successfully without help. DIY becomes risky the moment any of the following are present:

  • Any account with a child's or relative's name added within the past five years
  • Gifts, loans, or transfers of property or money to family members
  • Income above the monthly cap, which requires a qualified income trust set up and funded correctly from the start
  • Uncertainty about whether the home will need an intent-to-return statement
  • A desire to protect the home from future estate recovery through proper deed planning

The Baxters' story shows how a handful of small, understandable errors compounded into a denial, a penalty, and a probate delay. None of it stemmed from dishonesty. It came from not knowing which questions actually mattered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does adding my name to my parent's bank account count as a Medicaid transfer?
It can. Florida Medicaid generally looks at how the account was actually used and titled, and adding a child as a joint owner is often treated as a transfer of that portion of the asset unless properly explained and documented.
What is the intent-to-return statement and why does it matter?
It's a form an applicant signs stating they intend to return to their home if their health improves, and signing it is generally sufficient to keep the home an exempt asset during the applicant's lifetime, even if return is medically unlikely.
Can Florida Medicaid take my parent's house after they pass away?
Florida's Medicaid estate recovery program can file a claim against a deceased recipient's probate estate, but Florida law exempts the homestead from recovery when it passes to a qualifying heir at law, such as a child, though how the home is titled affects how smoothly that exemption is applied.
How does a lady bird deed help avoid estate recovery?
A lady bird deed lets a homeowner keep full lifetime control of the property while it passes automatically to named beneficiaries at death, outside of probate, which generally removes the home from the estate that Medicaid recovery can reach.
Is it ever safe to file a Medicaid application without an attorney?
Yes, in genuinely simple cases with no recent transfers, no joint accounts added within five years, and straightforward income and assets, but any complexity in the past five years of finances significantly raises the risk of denial or penalty.

The Truestead Takeaway

The Baxter family's experience, though a composite drawn from patterns I see often in my practice, reflects exactly the kind of trouble that arises when well-meaning families handle a Medicaid application without guidance on the transfer question, the intent-to-return statement, and the deed work that keeps a home out of the probate estate. None of Pam's mistakes were careless. They were simply the kind of details that Florida's Medicaid rules treat as decisive. If your family is approaching this process, or has already filed and run into questions, it is worth having a Florida elder law attorney review the finances and the home's title before, not after, a denial or an estate recovery letter arrives.
