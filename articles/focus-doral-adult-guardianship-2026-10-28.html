Doral Adult Guardianship: What Florida Families Need to Know

Quick Answer

Doral adult guardianship isn't a separate legal category, it's the general Florida guardianship process under Chapter 744 of the Florida Statutes, handled through the Eleventh Judicial Circuit Court and, for indigent wards in Miami-Dade County, supported locally by the Guardianship Program of Dade County headquartered in Doral.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney October 28, 2026

What Does "Doral Adult Guardianship" Actually Mean?

In my practice, I get a lot of calls from families searching exactly this phrase, and I understand why: it sounds like there might be a special Doral rule or a shortcut process just for this part of Miami-Dade County. There isn't. What's actually happening is that Doral is home to the Guardianship Program of Dade County (GPDC), a private nonprofit organization that serves as the designated public guardian for indigent adults adjudicated incapacitated by the Eleventh Judicial Circuit Court. GPDC has operated for more than four decades and currently serves well over a thousand Miami-Dade residents who have been found incapacitated and have limited financial means.

So if you're a Doral resident, or you have a family member who lives there, the law that governs your situation is the same Florida guardianship law that applies statewide: Chapter 744 of the Florida Statutes. What's local is the court (the Eleventh Judicial Circuit, based in Miami-Dade County) and, for families who can't afford or don't have a private guardian, the nonprofit infrastructure headquartered in Doral that steps in to serve as guardian of last resort.

How Florida's Adult Guardianship Process Works

Guardianship is a court proceeding in which a judge determines that an adult's capacity to make decisions is impaired to the point that someone else, called a guardian, needs legal authority to make decisions on their behalf. This is a serious step because it removes rights from the individual, called the ward, so Florida law builds in significant procedural protections.

  • Petition filed with the circuit court. Anyone seeking a determination of incapacity files a petition with the circuit court under F.S. § 744.3201.
  • Attorney appointed for the alleged incapacitated person. The court appoints counsel to represent the person whose capacity is being questioned, and anyone petitioning to become guardian must also be represented by an attorney under the Florida Rules of Probate Procedure.
  • Examining committee. Within five days, the court appoints a three-member examining committee, typically including physicians and other professionals, to evaluate the individual.
  • Reports and hearing. Committee members file reports within fifteen days, and the adjudicatory hearing is generally held ten to thirty days after the last report comes in.
  • Clear and convincing evidence required. Incapacity must be proven by clear and convincing evidence under F.S. § 744.331(5)(c), a meaningfully higher standard than ordinary civil cases.

This process exists to protect vulnerable adults from having their rights taken away too easily, while still giving families and courts a path to step in when someone truly can't manage their own affairs.

Limited vs. Plenary Guardianship: Not All Guardianships Are the Same

One of the most important things I explain to Florida families is that guardianship doesn't have to mean someone loses every single right they have. Florida recognizes two main types:

  • Limited guardianship: appropriate when the individual can still handle some decisions or tasks but needs help with others, such as managing finances but not medical decisions, or vice versa.
  • Plenary guardianship: reserved for individuals found to be incapacitated in essentially all respects, where the guardian assumes the full range of legal rights and duties the person would otherwise exercise.

Florida law directs courts to use the least restrictive appropriate alternative, meaning a judge should not impose a plenary guardianship if a limited guardianship, or another arrangement entirely, would adequately protect the person.

Alternatives That May Avoid Guardianship Altogether

Guardianship is sometimes necessary, but it's rarely the first tool I reach for. Because it involves a court stripping away legal rights, Florida law and good practice both favor less restrictive options whenever they're genuinely workable:

  • Durable power of attorney (Chapter 709, Florida Statutes), executed while a person still has capacity, letting a trusted agent handle financial matters.
  • Healthcare surrogate designation (F.S. § 765.202) and a living will (F.S. § 765.301), which let someone make medical decisions without court involvement.
  • Guardian advocacy, a more streamlined process available in certain circumstances (such as for individuals with developmental disabilities) that can establish authority over specific areas, like medical care or benefits, without a full incapacity trial, and while often preserving rights like the right to vote.

These documents are far easier, faster, and less costly to put in place before a crisis than to try to arrange after someone has already lost capacity, which is exactly when a court-supervised guardianship becomes necessary in the first place.

Planning Ahead: If your family member in Doral or anywhere in Miami-Dade still has capacity today, executing a durable power of attorney and healthcare surrogate designation now is often the single best way to avoid a guardianship proceeding later.

The Guardianship Program of Dade County's Role in Doral

For families who cannot serve as guardian themselves, and who don't have the financial means to hire a professional guardian, the Guardianship Program of Dade County, based in Doral, serves as the court-designated public guardian for indigent incapacitated adults throughout Miami-Dade County. GPDC has served thousands of individuals over its history and continues to manage care for well over a thousand wards today, working under the oversight of the Eleventh Judicial Circuit Court.

GPDC is also a recognized provider of the training courses Florida requires for guardians, including the shorter courses aimed at family members serving as guardian and the forty-hour course required to become a professional guardian in Florida. If you've been asked, or are considering asking a court, to serve as guardian for a loved one, understanding these training requirements early can help you plan realistically for what the role involves.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a special legal process for guardianship just for Doral, Florida?
No. Doral residents go through the same Florida guardianship process under Chapter 744 of the Florida Statutes as anyone else in Miami-Dade County, filed in the Eleventh Judicial Circuit Court. What's local to Doral is the Guardianship Program of Dade County, a nonprofit that serves as public guardian for qualifying indigent adults.
What's the difference between a limited and a plenary guardianship in Florida?
A limited guardianship gives the guardian authority only over the specific decisions the ward can't safely make themselves, while a plenary guardianship gives the guardian authority over essentially all legal and personal decisions because the court has found the person incapacitated in all relevant respects.
Do I need a lawyer to petition for guardianship of a family member in Florida?
Yes. Under the Florida Rules of Probate Procedure, anyone petitioning to become a guardian must be represented by an attorney, and the court separately appoints an attorney to represent the alleged incapacitated person.
Can guardianship be avoided if my aging parent still has capacity?
Often, yes. A durable power of attorney under Chapter 709 and a healthcare surrogate designation under Section 765.202 of the Florida Statutes, executed while your parent still has capacity, can give trusted family members legal authority without ever needing a court-supervised guardianship.
What is the Guardianship Program of Dade County and does it serve Doral residents?
It's a private nonprofit headquartered in Doral that serves as the designated public guardian for indigent adults adjudicated incapacitated by the Eleventh Judicial Circuit Court throughout Miami-Dade County, including Doral residents who qualify based on limited financial means.
How long does a Florida guardianship proceeding typically take once a petition is filed?
After a petition is filed, the court generally appoints an examining committee within about five days, committee reports are due within fifteen days, and the adjudicatory hearing usually follows ten to thirty days after the last report, though individual case timelines can vary.

The Truestead Takeaway

Guardianship exists to protect people who genuinely can't make safe decisions anymore, but Florida law is built to use it only when less restrictive options won't work, and to remove only the rights that truly need to be removed. If you're facing a possible guardianship for a loved one in Doral or anywhere in Miami-Dade County, or you want to put documents in place now so your family never has to go through this process, the sensible next step is to have your situation reviewed by a Florida elder law attorney who can walk through your family's specific circumstances.
