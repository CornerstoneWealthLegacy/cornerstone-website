Windermere Real Estate Attorney: What Florida Buyers, Sellers, and Owners Need to Know

Quick Answer

A Windermere real estate attorney is a Florida-licensed lawyer who handles property closings, title issues, contract disputes, and related legal matters for owners and buyers in the Windermere area, and many of these same attorneys also help clients make sure that real estate is properly titled and passed on through a will or trust.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 7, 2026

What Does a Windermere Real Estate Attorney Actually Do?

In my practice, I find that people search for a "windermere real estate attorney" for one of a handful of reasons: they're buying or selling a home in Windermere or the surrounding Orange County communities, they've hit a snag in a contract, or they've inherited property and aren't sure how title should be handled. A Florida real estate attorney can generally assist with:

  • Reviewing and negotiating purchase and sale contracts
  • Conducting or reviewing title searches and resolving title defects
  • Handling the closing itself, including preparation of the deed and closing disclosures
  • Advising on specific performance when a buyer or seller refuses to close
  • Working through landlord-tenant disputes, habitability complaints, and lease issues
  • Addressing condominium association matters, zoning questions, and code enforcement issues
  • Litigating construction defect or boundary disputes when they arise

What I always tell clients is this: a real estate agent, no matter how experienced, is not permitted to give you legal advice or represent your legal interests in a transaction. Agents are wonderful at finding property and negotiating price, but the contract you sign has real legal consequences, and an attorney is the one who can tell you what you're actually agreeing to.

Do I Need an Attorney to Buy or Sell a Home in Windermere, Florida?

Florida does not require a buyer or seller to hire an attorney to close on a residential purchase. Many closings here are handled through a title company alone. But "not required" is different from "not a good idea." I recommend involving an attorney when:

  • The property involves a trust, an estate, or multiple heirs
  • There's a boundary, easement, or title defect that shows up in the search
  • You're buying property subject to an existing mortgage assumption or unusual financing
  • A dispute arises over the contract, inspection findings, or the closing date
  • The transaction involves commercial property, new construction, or a homeowners' or condo association with complex rules

Straightforward, cash-clean transactions between cooperative parties often go smoothly with just a title company. But Windermere has a mix of long-held family homes, newer luxury construction, and lakefront property, and any of those can raise title or survey issues that are worth having a lawyer's eyes on before you're contractually committed.

Choosing an Attorney: What "Board Certified" and Bar Standing Actually Mean

When you're evaluating a Windermere real estate attorney, two credentials are worth understanding. First, Board Certification in real estate law is a designation from The Florida Bar that an attorney only earns after demonstrating substantial experience in the field, passing a specialized exam, and meeting peer review requirements. Not every competent real estate attorney is board certified, but it is a meaningful signal of focused expertise if you find one who is.

Second, every attorney you hire in Florida should be in good standing with The Florida Bar. You can verify any attorney's license status and disciplinary history directly through The Florida Bar's website before you hire them. I'd encourage anyone to do this, not because most attorneys have problems, but because it takes two minutes and gives you real peace of mind.

A Practical Note: Many Windermere-area firms, including mine, handle both real estate transactions and estate planning. If you're buying property that will eventually pass to children or other heirs, it's worth asking your attorney how the title should be held now to make that transition smoother later.

How Real Estate and Estate Planning Overlap in Florida

Here's something I see often that surprises clients: how you take title to your Windermere home has real consequences for your estate plan, and vice versa. Florida's homestead protections are among the strongest in the country. Under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, a homestead is generally protected from forced sale by most creditors, and it also carries specific rules about how it can be devised at death under F.S. § 732.4015. If you're married or have minor children, Florida law restricts your ability to leave your homestead to just anyone you choose, so this is an area where a mismatch between your deed and your will can create real problems for your family.

Similarly, if you hold Florida real estate in a properly funded revocable trust (governed by F.S. Chapter 736), that property can generally pass to your beneficiaries outside of probate, which means a faster, more private transfer than going through the court process. But the trust has to actually hold title to the property, meaning the deed has to be properly recorded in the trust's name, not just mentioned in the trust document. I've seen families assume their home was "in the trust" simply because an attorney drafted the trust years ago, only to discover during probate that the deed was never transferred.

⚠ A Common Mistake: Adding a child's name directly to your deed to "avoid probate" can trigger gift tax reporting requirements, expose the property to that child's creditors or divorce, and complicate your Save Our Homes tax benefit under F.S. § 193.155. This shortcut often creates more problems than it solves, and it's worth discussing alternatives with an attorney first.

What Happens to Windermere Real Estate If Someone Dies Without a Will?

If a Windermere property owner dies without a valid will, Florida's intestacy statutes (F.S. §§ 732.101 through 732.111) determine who inherits, based on a fixed formula involving the surviving spouse, children, and other relatives. This formula doesn't ask what the deceased person would have wanted; it simply applies the same rule to everyone. For homestead property specifically, there are additional constitutional and statutory rules that can restrict or dictate how the home passes, particularly when there's a surviving spouse and children from a prior relationship.

This is one of the clearest reasons I encourage every Florida homeowner to have at least a basic will, even a simple one. It replaces a generic formula with your own decisions about who gets your property and who's in charge of settling your affairs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a Windermere real estate attorney the same as an estate planning attorney?
Not necessarily. Real estate attorneys focus on property transactions and disputes, while estate planning attorneys focus on wills, trusts, and incapacity planning. Many Florida firms, including mine, handle both because the two areas frequently intersect, especially with homestead property.
Does Florida require an attorney to be present at a real estate closing?
No. Florida allows closings to be handled by title companies without an attorney present, though hiring one is often wise for transactions involving trusts, estates, disputes, or title complications.
How do I confirm a Windermere attorney is actually licensed in Florida?
You can look up any attorney's license status, standing, and disciplinary history directly through The Florida Bar's official website using their name or bar number.
Can I add my child's name to my Windermere home's deed instead of using a will or trust?
You can, but this approach carries real risks, including exposing the property to your child's creditors or divorce and potentially affecting your Save Our Homes tax assessment. It's worth discussing safer alternatives with a Florida attorney before making changes to your deed.
What happens to my Florida homestead if I die without a will?
Florida's intestacy statutes and constitutional homestead rules will determine who inherits, based on a fixed formula involving your spouse and children, which may not match what you would have chosen yourself.
Does putting my house in a trust automatically avoid probate?
Only if the deed is actually transferred into the trust's name. A trust document alone doesn't move title. The property must be properly retitled and recorded for it to pass outside of probate.
