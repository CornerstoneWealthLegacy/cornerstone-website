Lady Bird Deeds and the Mortgage: What Florida Homeowners Should Know

Quick Answer

A properly drafted Florida lady bird deed does not trigger a due-on-sale clause because the owner keeps full control during life, but refinancing and reverse mortgages each involve their own lender-specific rules worth understanding before you sign.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 25, 2026

Nora's situation: a mortgage, a deed, and a plan to refinance

Nora is 70, lives in Palm Coast, and still has eleven years left on her mortgage. She wants her house to pass to her son without probate, and she's heard a lady bird deed can do that. But she also plans to refinance next year, and she's worried: will signing a deed now cause trouble with her lender later? Nora is a composite example, not an actual Truestead client, but her question is one I hear often from homeowners who are still paying on their homes.

A Florida lady bird deed lets an owner keep full control of the property for life, including the right to sell, mortgage, or revoke the deed at any time, while naming a beneficiary who receives the home automatically at death, without probate. That basic structure is exactly what protects Nora here.

Why the due-on-sale clause generally isn't a problem

Most mortgages contain a due-on-sale clause, which lets the lender demand full repayment if the property is transferred without its consent. On paper, that sounds alarming for anyone recording a new deed. In practice, a lady bird deed is designed so that no present transfer happens at all. Nora keeps complete ownership, the right to sell, the right to mortgage, and the right to revoke the deed outright. Her son's interest doesn't become real until she passes away.

Federal law backs this up. The Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 prohibits lenders from enforcing due-on-sale clauses against certain estate-planning transfers, including transfers to a relative on the borrower's death and transfers where the borrower remains a beneficiary and continues to occupy the property. A lady bird deed fits squarely within that protection because Nora isn't giving up anything during her lifetime.

  • No present transfer means no trigger for acceleration while Nora is alive.
  • Garn-St. Germain separately protects the transfer that happens at her death, since her son is a relative inheriting under a deed that named him as beneficiary.
  • Individual loan language still matters, so it's worth having a Florida attorney glance at the mortgage documents before recording, particularly with less conventional loans.
What this means for Nora: Recording her lady bird deed this year should not give her current mortgage lender any legal basis to call the loan due, because she retains full ownership and control until she dies.

Should Nora tell her lender about the deed?

There's no Florida requirement to notify a mortgage lender before recording a lady bird deed, and most homeowners don't. The deed is recorded in the county where the land sits, becoming part of the public record, but it doesn't require the lender's signature or consent because the owner isn't giving up any present rights.

That said, silence now doesn't mean the lender will never notice. Deeds are public record, and many loan servicers do periodic title reviews. If Nora's lender does raise a question, the answer is straightforward: she still owns the home outright, still owes the debt, and still has full authority to sell or refinance without her son's involvement. Florida's title standards confirm this directly, and they exist in part so that title companies, lenders, and closing attorneys have a shared, predictable way to treat these deeds.

What happens when Nora refinances next year

This is where things can get a little more hands-on, even though the underlying law is on Nora's side. Florida's title standards make clear that an owner who signed a lady bird deed can still sell or mortgage the property without getting the beneficiary's signature. Legally, her son has no present interest to sign away.

In practice, though, some lenders are cautious. It isn't uncommon for a refinance lender to flag the recorded lady bird deed during title review and ask questions before closing. Depending on the lender's internal policies, Nora might encounter one of a few responses:

  • The lender proceeds normally once its title team confirms the deed reserves full control to Nora during her lifetime.
  • The lender asks Nora's son to sign a waiver or acknowledgment, even though it isn't strictly required under Florida law, simply to satisfy internal underwriting comfort.
  • In less common cases, the lender asks that the deed be temporarily revoked before closing, with a new lady bird deed recorded again afterward.

None of these outcomes should alarm Nora. They're closing logistics, not legal obstacles. The key is to expect the conversation and not be surprised if her refinance lender wants extra paperwork or a short delay to sort out the title question.

Reverse mortgages and lady bird deeds

Some Florida homeowners consider a reverse mortgage instead of, or alongside, a lady bird deed. The two can work together, but reverse mortgage lenders tend to look at title more closely than a standard refinance lender would. Current guidance in the reverse mortgage industry indicates that a homeowner who holds a life estate with named remaindermen (the lady bird beneficiaries) generally does not need to remove them from title to close a reverse mortgage, but the remaindermen are typically required to attend the mandatory reverse mortgage counseling session alongside the borrower.

⚠ Where reverse mortgages can get complicated

Reverse mortgage underwriting can be stricter than standard refinancing. Some reverse mortgage loans include due-on-transfer language that reads more broadly than a typical mortgage, and existing life estates, pending liens, or unusual title histories can slow down or derail a reverse mortgage application. Anyone considering a reverse mortgage after (or before) recording a lady bird deed should have both the deed and the loan documents reviewed together by a Florida attorney familiar with both.

What Nora's son inherits, mortgage and all

When Nora eventually passes the home to her son through the lady bird deed, he inherits the property subject to whatever mortgage balance remains at that time, whether that's her original loan, her refinanced loan, or a reverse mortgage. Florida's lady bird deed transfers the home outside of probate, but it does not erase the debt attached to it.

At that point, her son generally has three paths available: continue making payments and keep the loan as is, refinance the loan into his own name if he wants to keep the house long term, or sell the property and use the proceeds to pay off the balance, keeping whatever equity remains. Garn-St. Germain protections extend to him too. As a relative inheriting the home, the lender cannot force immediate repayment of the full loan balance just because the ownership changed at Nora's death. That gives him breathing room to decide, rather than facing pressure to act immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Nora need her mortgage lender's permission to sign a lady bird deed?
No. Because she retains full ownership and control during her lifetime, Florida law and federal Garn-St. Germain protections mean the lender has no basis to require permission or call the loan due.
Will a lady bird deed cause problems if Nora is married?
If the home is Nora's Florida homestead and she is married, her spouse would generally need to join in signing the deed, since Florida homestead property owned by a married person cannot be conveyed or devised without spousal joinder.
Can Nora revoke or change the lady bird deed if her plans change before she refinances?
Yes. One of the defining features of a lady bird deed is that the owner can revoke or amend it at any time during their life, which is also what allows a temporary revoke-and-rerecord approach if a lender ever requests it.
Does the deed itself need to be recorded, and where?
Yes. Like any Florida deed, it must be signed with two witnesses, notarized, and recorded in the official records of the county where the property is located to be effective against later purchasers or claims.

If Nora's son can't afford the mortgage after she passes, does he lose the house immediately?
Not immediately. Garn-St. Germain protections generally prevent the lender from accelerating the loan simply because of the inherited transfer, giving him time to decide whether to sell, refinance, or continue payments.

The Truestead Takeaway

Nora's plan works the way she hopes: recording a lady bird deed now should not disturb her existing mortgage, and her planned refinance next year is a matter of paperwork and title review, not a legal barrier. The deed does its job quietly in the background, preserving her full control while setting up a probate-free transfer to her son, who will inherit the house along with whatever loan balance remains. Every mortgage, every lender, and every family situation carries its own wrinkles, so before recording a deed or starting a refinance, it's worth having a Florida attorney look at both the deed and the loan documents together.
