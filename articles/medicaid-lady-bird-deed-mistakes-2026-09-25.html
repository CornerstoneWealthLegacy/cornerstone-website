Lady Bird Deed Mistakes That Break a Florida Medicaid Plan

Quick Answer

A lady bird deed only works as intended when it is the right kind of deed, signed with two witnesses in the owner's presence, naming the correct grantees, filed before any other ownership change, and coordinated with title, mortgage, and homestead rules. Get any one of those wrong, and the plan can unravel even though the family thought the job was done.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney September 24, 2026

Hector's Situation: A Form, But Not a Plan

Hector is a composite, not an actual Truestead client, but his situation reflects what I see often enough that it deserves its own article. Hector is 80 and lives in Kissimmee. Facing the possibility of nursing home care, his family wanted to keep his house out of Medicaid's reach after he passed. A nephew found a lady bird deed template online, filled in the blanks, and had Hector sign it. Nobody in the family knew that Hector had already added his daughter to the deed as a joint owner a year earlier, or that Florida requires two witnesses on every real estate deed, or that the form named the wrong people as the future owners.

When Hector's family later sat down with an elder law attorney to prepare his Medicaid application, the deed became the biggest obstacle in the file. Nothing about their intentions was wrong. The execution was.

What a Lady Bird Deed Is Supposed to Do

Florida does not have a statute called a "lady bird deed." The name describes a specific way of drafting an enhanced life estate deed, using ordinary Florida property law. The owner keeps full control of the home during life: the right to live in it, sell it, mortgage it, or change who receives it later. Because the owner has not given up anything of real value while alive, the Department of Children and Families generally does not treat recording this deed as a disqualifying gift under the five-year Medicaid look-back.

At death, the home passes automatically to the named remainder beneficiaries outside of probate. Florida has chosen, as a matter of policy, not to expand Medicaid estate recovery to reach non-probate assets like a home that passed this way. That is the current practice, not a guarantee written permanently into law, and it is why getting the deed itself right matters so much: the protection depends entirely on the deed doing its job correctly.

Six Ways the Deed Goes Wrong

Here are the failure modes I see most often, using Hector's file as the throughline.

  • 1. Wrong grantees. The downloaded form named beneficiaries who were not who the family intended, mixing up a niece's married name with an old address record. In Hector's case, this meant the deed did not clearly transfer the remainder interest to his daughter at all. A lady bird deed is only as good as the names in it, and ambiguity here can force a court proceeding to sort out who actually inherits.
  • 2. Only one witness. Florida law requires two subscribing witnesses on any deed conveying real property, and they generally need to watch the owner sign. Hector's deed had one. Florida does have a curative statute that can rescue an improperly witnessed deed after it has been on record for a period of years without challenge, but a family filing a Medicaid application now cannot wait that out. A defectively witnessed deed recorded shortly before an application invites exactly the scrutiny a family is trying to avoid.
  • 3. Recorded after a joint deed was already in place. A year before the lady bird deed, Hector's daughter had been added as a joint owner with rights of survivorship. That earlier transfer was a completed gift of a real ownership share, the kind of transfer that can trigger a Medicaid penalty period if it falls inside the five-year look-back. The lady bird deed recorded afterward did nothing to undo that gift. Sequence matters. A lady bird deed only protects what the owner still fully owns at the time it is recorded.
  • 4. Confusing it with a traditional life estate deed. A standard life estate deed, without the enhanced retained powers, is a completed gift of the remainder interest the moment it is signed. That kind of deed can trigger a look-back penalty. The enhanced version, where the owner keeps the right to sell, mortgage, or revoke, is what keeps the transfer incomplete during life. Using the wrong form, or a form that blends the two, defeats the entire purpose.
  • 5. Title insurance and mortgage complications. Lenders and title companies sometimes balk at these deeds, especially when there is an existing mortgage or when the remainder beneficiaries later try to sell or refinance. A poorly drafted deed can cloud title for years, meaning the family discovers the problem only when they try to sell the house, often at the worst possible time.
  • 6. No contingent beneficiary. If the named remainder beneficiary dies before the owner and the deed does not name a backup, the property can end up back in the probate estate, undoing the very protection the deed was meant to provide.

Homestead and the Spouse Problem

Florida's homestead protections add another layer that a generic form rarely addresses. The Florida Constitution restricts who a homeowner can leave their homestead to if they are survived by a spouse or minor child. A surviving spouse generally must receive the homestead, or the spouse must knowingly waive that right. A downloaded form has no way of knowing whether the owner is married, whether a spouse needs to join in signing, or whether a separate waiver is required under Florida law.

⚠ Where families get caught off guard A lady bird deed that ignores a living spouse's homestead rights can be challenged or refused by a title company later, sometimes years after the owner has died and the family has moved forward assuming the plan worked.

Hector was widowed, so spousal joinder was not his issue, but it is one of the most common reasons these deeds fail for married Florida homeowners, and it is a question a form simply cannot answer.

When a Trust Does More Work Than a Deed

A lady bird deed is a simple, low-cost tool, and for a single asset like a homestead it often works well when done correctly. But it is not built to handle complexity. If a family is dealing with multiple properties, a blended family, a beneficiary with special needs, or a homeowner who anticipates needing broader asset protection alongside the home, a revocable trust under Florida law can coordinate all of it in one document, with successor trustee provisions and contingency planning a single deed cannot provide. For Hector, once his file was untangled, the fix involved correcting the deed itself, not switching to a trust, because his situation was ultimately a one-house, one-heir case. That will not be true for every family.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a lady bird deed be fixed after it is recorded incorrectly?
Often yes, through a corrective deed or, in some cases, a court proceeding, but the fix takes time and should be handled by a Florida attorney rather than another downloaded form.
Does adding a child as joint owner before a lady bird deed cause a problem?
It can. A joint ownership transfer is generally a completed gift and may trigger a Medicaid look-back penalty even if a lady bird deed is recorded afterward.
Do both spouses need to sign a lady bird deed in Florida?
If the property is homestead and there is a living spouse, the spouse's constitutional rights generally must be addressed through joinder or a valid waiver, or the deed can fail later.
Is a lady bird deed the same as a regular life estate deed?
No. A traditional life estate deed gives away the remainder interest immediately and can trigger a Medicaid penalty, while an enhanced (lady bird) deed lets the owner keep full control, so it is generally not treated as a disqualifying transfer.
Will Medicaid definitely leave the house alone if a lady bird deed is used?
Under Florida's current practice, homes passing through a validly executed lady bird deed generally avoid Medicaid estate recovery, but this is a policy choice rather than an absolute guarantee, and the deed must be done correctly for that protection to apply.

The Truestead Takeaway

Hector's family did the right thing by trying to protect his home, but a form downloaded online cannot account for prior transfers, witness rules, homestead law, or the difference between deed types, and any one of those gaps can undo months of planning right when it matters most. If your family is holding a lady bird deed you are not fully sure was done correctly, or you are considering one for a parent's home, it is worth having a Florida elder law attorney review the deed and the full ownership history before a Medicaid application is filed, not after.
