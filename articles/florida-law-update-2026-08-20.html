Florida's 2026 Probate Reform: Simpler Estates, Stronger Tools

Quick Answer

As of July 1, 2026, Florida raised the summary administration threshold from $75,000 to $150,000, increased several small-estate dollar limits, and gave personal representatives a new statutory remedy — F.S. § 733.6125 — to force uncooperative banks and financial institutions to honor valid estate authority.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 20, 2026

What Actually Changed on July 1, 2026

In my practice, the question I hear most often from families dealing with a loved one's estate is some version of: 'Does this really have to go through full probate?' As of July 1, 2026, the answer is yes less often than it used to be.

Florida's Legislature passed two companion bills — CS/HB 1337 (now Chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida) and CS/SB 1500 (Chapter 2026-85, Laws of Florida) — that reshape several corners of Florida's probate code. These came out of recommendations from the Florida Supreme Court's Workgroup on Uncontested Probate Proceedings, and the theme running through all of it is simple: keep straightforward estates out of court, and give personal representatives real tools when someone — often a bank — won't cooperate.

  • Summary administration threshold raised: Estates valued at $150,000 or less (excluding exempt property), up from the old $75,000 cap, can now use Florida's simplified summary administration procedure instead of full, formal administration.
  • Two-year rule unchanged: Regardless of value, summary administration remains available whenever the decedent has been deceased for more than two years — that threshold didn't move.
  • Small intestate estates: The value of certain intestate estates that can pass without any administration at all increased from $10,000 to $20,000.
  • Tax refund collection: A surviving spouse or child can now collect up to $5,000 in an income tax refund without opening a probate estate, up from the old $2,500 limit.

For an ordinary Florida family with a modest home, a bank account, and maybe a car, these changes can mean the difference between a probate case that wraps up in a matter of months versus one that drags on with full administration formalities.

New Leverage Against Uncooperative Banks

The part of this reform I think deserves more attention is the quieter half — the new enforcement mechanism at Florida Statutes § 733.6125. Anyone who has served as a personal representative knows the frustration: you have valid Letters of Administration, you present them to a financial institution, and the bank still won't release funds, close an account, or transfer a brokerage position without additional hoops that Florida law doesn't actually require.

Section 733.6125 gives personal representatives a clearer statutory path to go back to the probate court and ask a judge to enforce their existing powers when an institution won't honor them. Just as importantly, the Legislature built in a fee-shifting rule for the personal representative if they prevail in that enforcement proceeding — meaning the institution that stonewalled a legitimate estate matter can be on the hook for the personal representative's costs of forcing compliance.

Why This Matters: Before this change, a personal representative facing an uncooperative bank often had limited practical options short of expensive, generalized litigation. This new statute is a targeted remedy built specifically for that recurring problem.

The Florida Supreme Court moved quickly to implement this. On July 16, 2026, the Court issued Opinion No. SC2026-0690, adopting amendments to the Florida Probate Rules in response to the new legislation — including new Rule 5.025(a)(14), which classifies these enforcement proceedings under section 733.612 as adversary proceedings, meaning they follow the more formal procedural rules that apply to contested probate matters.

Trust Law Also Got an Update

Separately, Florida's trust code saw meaningful changes take effect through Senate Bill 262. Florida Statute § 736.04117 now gives 'authorized trustees' broader flexibility to modify an irrevocable trust or transfer its assets into a new trust — sometimes called 'decanting' — even where the new trust was created solely for that purpose. This can matter for older trusts drafted years ago with outdated tax provisions, rigid distribution language, or trustee succession terms that no longer fit a family's circumstances.

Florida also clarified, at § 736.151, that transferring Florida homestead property into a community property trust between spouses does not trigger a property tax reassessment. That's an important reassurance for married couples using trust planning around the homestead — it means the trust transfer itself shouldn't jeopardize Save Our Homes assessment limitations under F.S. § 193.155, though every homestead and trust structure should still be reviewed individually.

What This Means If You Already Have an Estate Plan

None of this changes the fundamentals — Florida still requires a validly executed will under F.S. § 732.502, a revocable trust is still governed by F.S. Chapter 736, and homestead protections under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution and F.S. § 732.4015 haven't been touched by this legislation. What has changed is the administrative path your family or personal representative may travel after a death.

If your current estate plan — or a family member's — was built with the old $75,000 summary administration ceiling in mind, it's worth having your plan reviewed. An estate that used to require full administration might now qualify for the faster, less expensive summary process. And if you're currently serving as a personal representative and a bank is refusing to cooperate, § 733.6125 may give you a more direct path forward than existed before July 2026.

⚠ A Note on Timing: These changes apply to estates and enforcement proceedings under the current statutory framework. If someone passed away or a proceeding was already underway before the effective date, how the new rules apply can depend on specific timing and procedural posture — this is exactly the kind of detail worth confirming with a Florida probate attorney rather than assuming.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the higher summary administration threshold mean my family's estate automatically qualifies?
Not automatically. Qualification depends on the total value of the estate excluding exempt property, whether there are unpaid creditor claims, and other factors under Florida's probate rules — it's worth having the specific estate reviewed.
What is 'summary administration' compared to full probate?
Summary administration is a simplified, faster court process available for smaller or older estates that avoids the more extensive formal steps required in full estate administration under Florida Statutes Chapter 733.
Can a bank still refuse to release a deceased person's funds to the personal representative?
A bank can still raise legitimate concerns, but Florida's new § 733.6125 gives personal representatives a clearer statutory route to ask the probate court to enforce their authority, with the possibility of recovering fees if they prevail.
Do these 2026 changes affect my existing revocable living trust?
The core rules governing revocable trusts under F.S. Chapter 736 remain the same, but recent amendments — including § 736.04117 on trust modification and § 736.151 on homestead community property trusts — may be relevant depending on your specific trust structure.
If my loved one passed away before July 1, 2026, do the new thresholds apply?
Application of new effective-date thresholds to estates opened before or around the transition date can involve specific procedural timing questions that should be confirmed with a Florida probate attorney rather than assumed.
Should I update my estate plan because of these changes?
These reforms mainly affect administrative procedure rather than the substance of wills, trusts, or homestead law, but any legislative update is a good occasion to have your existing plan reviewed to confirm it still fits your family's situation.

The Truestead Takeaway

The July 2026 probate reforms are genuinely good news for Florida families — more estates can now move through the simpler, faster summary administration process, and personal representatives finally have a real statutory hammer for banks that won't cooperate with valid estate authority. But procedural reforms like these interact with the specific facts of each estate, each trust, and each family's timing, so the sensible next step is the same one I'd give my own family: bring your will, trust, or open estate in for a review with a Florida attorney to see exactly how these changes apply to you.
