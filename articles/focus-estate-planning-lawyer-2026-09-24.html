Estate Planning Lawyer in Florida: A Straight Answer to What You're Looking For

Quick Answer

An estate planning lawyer helps you create legally valid documents (a will, trust, power of attorney, and healthcare directives) that control what happens to your property and your care if you become incapacitated or pass away. Florida does not technically require a lawyer to draft these documents, but Florida has strict execution rules, and a mistake in signing or witnessing can make a will or power of attorney entirely void.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 24, 2026

What Does an Estate Planning Lawyer Actually Do?

In my practice, I find most people search for an estate planning lawyer after a life event: a new grandchild, a health scare, a parent's diagnosis, or simply the realization that nothing is written down. What they're really looking for is peace of mind, someone who can translate their wishes into documents that will actually hold up when it matters.

An estate planning lawyer in Florida typically helps with:

  • Drafting a last will and testament that meets Florida's execution requirements
  • Creating a revocable living trust (governed by Florida Statutes Chapter 736) to avoid probate and manage assets during incapacity
  • Preparing a durable power of attorney under Florida Statutes Chapter 709, so someone you trust can handle finances if you can't
  • Preparing a healthcare surrogate designation (F.S. § 765.202) and living will (F.S. § 765.301) for medical decision-making
  • Addressing Florida's unique homestead rules, which affect how your primary residence can be devised (F.S. § 732.4015)
  • Coordinating beneficiary designations, jointly held property, and life insurance so they don't contradict your will or trust

What separates a good estate plan from a stack of forms downloaded online is that everything works together. A will that contradicts a beneficiary designation, or a trust that was never actually funded, can create the very disputes people hire a lawyer to avoid.

Do You Actually Need a Lawyer to Write a Will in Florida?

Legally, no. Florida does not require an attorney to draft a will. But Florida Statute § 732.502 sets out precise execution requirements: the will must be in writing, signed by the testator at the end of the document, and signed by two witnesses who sign in the presence of the testator and each other. If those formalities aren't followed exactly, the will can be declared invalid, and the estate passes instead under Florida's intestate succession laws, meaning the state's default distribution scheme controls, not your wishes.

⚠ A Common Mistake I see with online will kits and DIY documents is a missing witness signature, a notary substituted for a witness, or execution that doesn't happen in one continuous, properly witnessed signing. Any of these can render a will legally worthless in Florida probate court, and the error often isn't discovered until after the person has passed away, when it's too late to fix.

The same caution applies to powers of attorney. Chapter 709 has specific witness and notary rules, and documents that don't comply are frequently rejected by banks and financial institutions, even if a court would eventually honor them. If your durable power of attorney was signed before October 1, 2011, it likely does not meet Florida's current requirements and should be reviewed.

When Should You Hire an Estate Planning Lawyer Instead of Using a Template?

Templates can work for the simplest situations, but in my experience most families have at least one wrinkle that makes professional guidance worthwhile:

  • You own a home in Florida and want to understand how homestead protections under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution affect who can inherit it
  • You have a blended family, a minor child, a beneficiary with special needs, or a family member you want to intentionally exclude
  • You own property in more than one state, or you're a seasonal Florida resident (a "snowbird") with legal ties elsewhere
  • You want to avoid probate entirely through a properly funded revocable trust
  • Your estate is large enough that federal estate tax exposure is a realistic concern, even though Florida itself has no state estate tax
  • You have an existing plan that hasn't been reviewed in three to five years, or one built around old tax exemption formulas that may no longer function as intended

For more complex matters, look for an attorney who is Board Certified in Wills, Trusts and Estates by The Florida Bar. Certification requires years of substantial, documented experience in the specialty, ongoing continuing legal education, and a written examination, and it's renewed only if the attorney continues to meet those standards. It's one of the clearer signals of genuine focus in this area of law.

What Happens Without an Estate Plan in Florida?

When someone dies without a valid will, Florida's intestate succession statutes decide who inherits, not the person who died. Florida's probate process, governed by Chapters 731 through 735 of the Florida Statutes, then determines how the estate is administered: validating (or in this case, disregarding) any will, appointing a personal representative, notifying creditors, and distributing assets according to the statutory formula rather than personal wishes.

Probate itself isn't something to fear. It's a court-supervised process that exists to protect everyone involved, and Florida even offers a simplified summary administration option for smaller estates, with the qualifying threshold periodically adjusted by the legislature. But probate without a will almost always takes longer, costs more, and can create friction among family members who each believe they know what the deceased would have wanted.

Good to Know: Florida has no state estate or inheritance tax. That's a real advantage for Florida residents, though federal estate tax can still apply to larger estates, and out-of-state property you own may be subject to that state's rules.

How Much Does an Estate Planning Lawyer Cost, and How Do You Choose One?

Fees vary by firm, by the complexity of your family and assets, and by whether you need a simple will package or a full trust-based plan with tax planning. Many Florida estate planning attorneys, including our firm, offer a flat fee for standard document packages so you know the cost upfront, rather than an open-ended hourly bill.

When choosing an estate planning lawyer, I'd suggest asking:

  • How much of their practice is devoted to wills, trusts, and probate, versus other areas of law
  • Whether they are Board Certified in Wills, Trusts and Estates by The Florida Bar
  • Whether they will personally review your existing beneficiary designations and titling, not just draft new documents in isolation
  • How often they recommend reviewing and updating your plan

A sound general rule, one I follow with my own clients, is to revisit an estate plan every three to five years even without a major life event, because both your circumstances and Florida law can change in ways that affect whether your plan still does what you intended.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it worth hiring an estate planning lawyer in Florida, or can I do it myself?
You are legally allowed to draft your own will in Florida, but the state's execution requirements under F.S. § 732.502 are strict and unforgiving. Many DIY documents fail on a technicality, such as improper witnessing, which can void the document entirely and send the estate through intestate succession instead.
What's the difference between a will and a trust in Florida?
A will directs how your assets are distributed after death and goes through probate, while a revocable living trust, created under Florida Statutes Chapter 736, can hold assets during your lifetime and typically avoids probate for anything properly transferred into it. Many Florida plans use both together.
Does Florida have an estate tax?
No. Florida does not impose a state estate or inheritance tax. Federal estate tax can still apply to larger estates, and if you own property in another state, that state's tax rules may separately apply.
How often should I update my Florida estate plan?
A general guideline is every three to five years, or sooner after a major life event such as marriage, divorce, a new child or grandchild, a significant change in assets, or a move to or from Florida. Documents signed before October 1, 2011 in particular should be reviewed against current Chapter 709 requirements.
What does it mean if an attorney is 'Board Certified' in estate planning?
It means The Florida Bar has verified the attorney meets rigorous standards, including years of substantial, documented experience concentrated in wills, trusts, and estates, continuing legal education specific to the field, and passage of a written exam, with recertification required every five years.
What happens if I die in Florida without a will?
Your estate is distributed according to Florida's intestate succession laws, which follow a fixed statutory formula rather than your personal wishes, and the estate is administered through Florida's probate process under Chapters 731 through 735 of the Florida Statutes.
