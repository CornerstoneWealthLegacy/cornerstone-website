High Springs Truck Accident Lawyer: What Florida Law Says About Your Claim

Quick Answer

If you or a loved one was hurt in a truck crash near High Springs, Florida law generally gives you two years from the date of the crash to file a negligence lawsuit, and you can still recover damages as long as you were not more than 50 percent at fault. Because trucking cases involve multiple potentially liable parties and federal regulations on top of state law, talking to a Florida attorney early, before evidence disappears, makes a real difference.

High Springs sits along US 441 and close to US 27 and I-75, routes that carry a steady stream of commercial trucks moving freight, timber, and agricultural goods across north-central Florida. When a loaded semi-truck or box truck collides with a passenger vehicle, the physical mismatch alone often means serious injuries: spinal trauma, traumatic brain injury, internal bleeding, and broken bones are common. That is why so many Alachua County residents end up searching for a High Springs truck accident lawyer in the days after a crash.

There are usually more layers: the driver, the trucking company, sometimes a separate owner of the trailer or cargo, and federal safety regulations that do not apply to ordinary passenger vehicles. Understanding how Florida law treats each of these pieces is the first step toward protecting a claim.

How Long Do I Have to File a Truck Accident Claim in Florida?

Florida's statute of limitations for most negligence-based personal injury claims, including truck accidents, is two years from the date of the crash. This shorter deadline came from the 2023 tort reform legislation (HB 837), which reduced what had previously been a four-year window. Courts apply this deadline strictly, so missing it generally means losing the right to sue entirely, regardless of how strong the underlying claim might be.

If the crash tragically resulted in a death, Florida's wrongful death statute also generally applies a two-year limitations period, measured from the date of death rather than the date of the crash itself.

⚠ Don't wait to get answers. Two years sounds like a long time, but trucking companies often have accident response teams and insurance adjusters working within hours of a crash. Evidence like black box data, driver logs, and dashcam footage can be lost, overwritten, or destroyed if not preserved quickly. The earlier a lawyer can send a preservation letter, the better the odds of protecting critical evidence.

What If I Was Partly at Fault for the Accident?

Florida used to follow a pure comparative negligence rule, meaning an injured person could recover something even if they were mostly at fault. That changed with the 2023 reform. Florida now follows a modified comparative negligence standard under Florida Statute 768.81.

  • If you are found 50 percent or less at fault, you can still recover damages, reduced by your percentage of fault.
  • If you are found more than 50 percent at fault, you are generally barred from recovering any damages at all.

This makes the fault investigation in truck accident cases especially important. Insurance companies for trucking companies routinely try to shift blame onto the other driver, sometimes aggressively, because pushing a plaintiff's fault percentage above the 50 percent line can eliminate the claim entirely. A careful reconstruction of the crash, often using police reports, witness statements, and sometimes the truck's electronic data, is usually necessary to counter that strategy.

Who Can Be Held Responsible After a Truck Crash?

Truck accident liability in Florida often extends beyond just the driver. Depending on the facts, responsible parties may include:

  • The truck driver, for negligent conduct such as speeding, fatigued driving, distracted driving, or violating federal hours-of-service rules.
  • The trucking company, which can be liable for negligent hiring, inadequate training, poor vehicle maintenance, or pressuring drivers to violate safety regulations.
  • The vehicle owner, under Florida's dangerous instrumentality doctrine, which can impose vicarious liability on an owner whose vehicle causes injury while being operated with their permission.
  • A cargo loading company or maintenance contractor, in cases involving improperly secured loads or mechanical failure.

Because commercial trucks are required to carry higher minimum liability insurance than ordinary passenger vehicles under federal regulations, there is often more insurance coverage available in a serious truck accident case than in a typical car crash. That is one reason these claims frequently involve more than one insurance company and more than one round of negotiation.

Good to know: Florida requires every driver to carry at least $10,000 in Personal Injury Protection (PIP) coverage, and to preserve PIP benefits you generally must seek medical treatment within 14 days of the crash. Even in a serious truck accident case, PIP is usually just the starting point, not the full picture of available compensation.

What Compensation Is Available, and Are There Limits?

Florida does not cap compensatory damages in truck accident cases. That means a jury can award whatever amount it finds supported by the evidence for medical expenses, lost wages, future earning capacity, and pain and suffering, there is no statutory ceiling on these categories.

In cases involving especially reckless conduct, such as a driver who was intoxicated or a trucking company that knowingly ignored serious safety violations, punitive damages may also be available under Florida Statute 768.72. Punitive damages are meant to punish and deter, not just compensate, and Florida law generally limits them to three times the compensatory damages awarded, or a set statutory amount, whichever is greater, though exceptions exist for particularly egregious conduct. Florida personal injury verdicts in 2026 have continued to show that well-documented trucking cases, built around clear evidence of negligence, can result in substantial jury awards and settlements.

What Should I Do After a Truck Accident Near High Springs?

What I tell Florida clients after any serious truck collision is to focus on three things early: medical care, documentation, and timing.

  • Get medical treatment promptly, even if you feel fine initially. Many serious injuries, including traumatic brain injuries and internal injuries, have delayed symptoms, and prompt treatment also protects your PIP benefits.
  • Preserve evidence, including photos of the scene, vehicle damage, and any visible injuries, along with contact information for witnesses.
  • Be cautious with insurance adjusters, particularly those representing the trucking company, before speaking with an attorney. Statements made early can be used later to argue comparative fault.

Because trucking cases often move faster than typical car accident claims, and because the two-year filing deadline does not pause for negotiations, getting a Florida attorney involved early allows evidence to be preserved and deadlines to be tracked properly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have to file a truck accident lawsuit in Florida?
Generally two years from the date of the crash under Florida's current statute of limitations for negligence claims, following the 2023 tort reform. Wrongful death claims generally follow a two-year deadline from the date of death.
Can I still recover damages if I was partly at fault for the truck accident?
Yes, as long as you are found 50 percent or less at fault under Florida's modified comparative negligence rule. If you are found more than 50 percent at fault, you generally cannot recover damages.
Is the trucking company responsible, or just the driver?
It depends on the facts. The driver can be personally liable for negligent conduct, while the trucking company may also be liable for issues like negligent hiring, maintenance failures, or safety violations, and the vehicle owner may face liability under Florida's dangerous instrumentality doctrine.
Does Florida cap how much I can recover in a truck accident case?
No, Florida does not cap compensatory damages such as medical expenses, lost wages, or pain and suffering in truck accident cases. Punitive damages, where available, are generally limited under Florida Statute 768.72.
What is PIP and does it cover a truck accident injury?
Personal Injury Protection (PIP) is required minimum coverage for Florida drivers, generally $10,000, and you typically must seek medical treatment within 14 days of the crash to preserve those benefits, though serious truck accident injuries usually require pursuing compensation beyond PIP alone.
Why does it matter if my case is handled by a local Alachua County attorney?
An attorney familiar with the High Springs and Alachua County area understands local roads, courts, and the types of commercial traffic common to the region, which can help in reconstructing how a crash happened and identifying all responsible parties.

The two-year filing deadline, the modified comparative fault rule, and the layered liability questions involving drivers, trucking companies, and vehicle owners all make these cases more complex than an ordinary car accident claim. If you or a family member has been hurt in a truck accident near High Springs, the sensible next step is to have your situation reviewed by a Florida attorney who can evaluate the evidence, identify who may be responsible, and make sure nothing falls through the cracks before deadlines pass.
