Florida Homestead Exemption, Lady Bird Deeds, and What's Changing in 2026

Quick Answer

Florida voters will decide a major homestead exemption expansion this November, but for now the current homestead exemption still applies. Meanwhile, Lady Bird Deeds remain a solid, unchanged tool for passing a home to loved ones outside probate, and new federal reporting and condo disclosure rules took effect earlier in 2026.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Real Estate Attorney August 9, 2026

A Homestead Exemption Amendment Is Headed to Voters This November — But Nothing Has Changed Yet

In my practice, this is the question I'm getting most from homeowners and retirees right now: "Is my property tax bill about to go down?" Not yet — and not automatically. A constitutional amendment passed during the June 2026 special legislative session is now on the November 3, 2026 ballot. If approved, it would eventually replace the current homestead exemption structure with a tiered system: a $25,000 exemption applied against school millage, plus an additional exemption on non-school millage that would grow — up to $150,000 beginning January 1, 2027, and up to $250,000 beginning January 1, 2028.

For the current 2026 tax year, the homestead exemption homeowners are actually receiving remains $51,411. The proposed changes need 60 percent voter approval in November to take effect at all, and even then, the larger exemption amounts phase in over 2027 and 2028 — they are not retroactive and would not apply to a 2026 closing.

What I tell clients: If you're buying a home this year, budget based on today's tax rates and today's exemption amount. Don't discount a purchase price or stretch your affordability calculation on the assumption that this amendment will pass or that it will lower your bill soon. Treat any future savings as a bonus, not a given.

Lady Bird Deeds Remain a Reliable Way to Avoid Probate on Your Homestead

While the tax amendment gets the headlines, the tool I use most often for Florida homeowners hasn't changed at all this year: the Lady Bird Deed, formally known as an enhanced life estate deed. This deed lets you keep complete control of your home during your lifetime — you can still sell it, mortgage it, or change your mind and revoke the deed entirely, without needing permission from your named beneficiaries. When you pass away, the property transfers automatically to the people you named, entirely outside the Florida probate process.

Florida doesn't have a statute that calls this deed out by name, but the mechanism is well-established under Florida's general property law framework, supported by F.S. § 689.225 (Florida's version of the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities, which governs future interests in real property), and reinforced by the homestead protections in Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution. County clerks across the state routinely accept and record these deeds, and Florida courts have recognized this form of deed for decades.

  • Keeps your Save Our Homes tax benefit and homestead exemption intact during your lifetime
  • Avoids probate on the home at death, which saves your family time and cost
  • Does not trigger federal gift tax treatment during your lifetime because you haven't given up control
  • Generally preserves the beneficiary's stepped-up tax basis at your death

Because this deed touches your homestead rights, your estate plan, and often a mortgage or existing trust, this is not a form you should pull off the internet and sign without review. The wording has to be precise, and how it interacts with your will, your trust, or a surviving spouse's rights matters a great deal.

New Federal Reporting Rules Now Apply to Certain Cash Real Estate Deals

Here's a development that surprised some of my clients this year. Starting March 1, 2026, the federal government began requiring certain residential real estate transactions to be reported to the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, or FinCEN. This applies to specific high-risk, non-financed (cash) transfers — the kind of deal most likely to involve an LLC or a trust taking title, rather than an individual buyer with a mortgage.

The good news for most Florida buyers and sellers: in a typical transaction, the burden of this reporting falls on the title company, closing attorney, or settlement/escrow agent handling your file — not on you personally. If you're buying or selling through an entity, or paying all cash, it's worth asking your closing agent directly whether your transaction is subject to this reporting requirement so there are no surprises at the closing table.

Closing Costs and Documentary Stamp Tax: What Hasn't Changed

Florida's documentary stamp tax on deeds remains at $0.70 per $100 of the purchase price in most counties, under F.S. § 201.02. Miami-Dade County remains the exception, with a lower base rate of $0.60 per $100 plus an additional surtax on non-single-family residential property. No changes to these rates occurred in 2026.

This also matters if you're transferring your home into a revocable living trust as part of your estate plan — a step I recommend often. There have been no changes in 2026 to how documentary stamp tax applies to trust transfers; the same rules and exemptions that existed before still apply. Whether your particular transfer qualifies for an exemption depends on the specifics of your mortgage and how the trust is structured, so this is worth reviewing with your attorney rather than assuming.

⚠ A Note on DIY Deeds: Whether it's a quitclaim deed, a warranty deed, or a Lady Bird Deed, an improperly drafted or recorded deed can create title problems that surface years later — often when your family is trying to sell the property or settle your estate. A deed that looks simple on its face can still create real complications if it doesn't account for your mortgage, your marital status, or your existing estate plan.

If You're Buying a Condo, New Disclosure Rules Are Now in Effect

For Florida buyers looking at condominiums, take note: as of January 1, 2026, updated provisions of the Florida Condominium Act require condo associations to make more information available upfront to prospective buyers. This includes budgets, board meeting minutes, structural inspection reports, reserve studies, and governing documents — all of which should now be more readily accessible before you sign a contract.

Given the scrutiny Florida condo buildings have faced in recent years around reserves and structural inspections, I'd encourage any condo buyer to actually request and review these documents rather than treating this as a formality. They tell you a great deal about the financial health of the association and whether a special assessment may be coming.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will my property taxes go down in 2026 because of the ballot amendment?
Not this year. The amendment is on the November 2026 ballot and, even if approved by the required 60% of voters, the expanded exemption amounts phase in starting January 1, 2027, with the largest increase not arriving until 2028.
Is a Lady Bird Deed the same as a quitclaim or warranty deed?
No. A quitclaim or warranty deed typically transfers ownership immediately, while a Lady Bird Deed lets you keep full ownership and control during your lifetime and only passes the property automatically at death, avoiding probate.
Do I need to worry about the new FinCEN reporting rule as a regular home buyer?
Most Florida buyers using a mortgage and buying as individuals won't be directly affected, since the rule targets certain cash transactions involving entities like LLCs or trusts, and the reporting duty generally falls on the title or closing agent.
Does putting my home in a trust trigger documentary stamp tax?
It depends on your specific mortgage and trust structure; there were no 2026 changes to the underlying rules, but you should have an attorney review whether an exemption applies to your particular transfer.
What documents should I ask for before buying a Florida condo now?
As of January 1, 2026, you can request the association's budget, recent meeting minutes, structural inspection reports, reserve studies, and governing documents before you sign a purchase contract.
Should I write my own Lady Bird Deed to save money?
I'd caution against it. A Lady Bird Deed interacts with your homestead status, your mortgage, and your broader estate plan, and small drafting errors can create title problems for your family later.

The Truestead Takeaway

None of the developments in this update require you to rush into a decision, but they're all good reasons to have your Florida property and estate plan reviewed this year — whether that means confirming how a pending deed fits with your homestead rights, understanding how a trust transfer would be taxed, or simply making sure your closing team knows about the new federal reporting rule if you're buying with cash or an entity. I'd encourage anyone with questions about their specific home, deed, or closing to sit down with a Florida attorney and look at the actual documents rather than relying on general rules of thumb.
