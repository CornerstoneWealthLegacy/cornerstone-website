Collier County Medicaid Planning Attorneys: What Naples-Area Families Need to Know

Quick Answer

In Florida, Medicaid planning (structuring income, assets, and trusts to help a loved one qualify for long-term care benefits) must legally be handled by a licensed Florida attorney, not a non-attorney "Medicaid planner." Collier County families searching for help should look for an elder law attorney familiar with Florida's income cap, asset limits, and five-year look-back rules, ideally well before a nursing home stay becomes urgent.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney September 30, 2026

Why This Decision Belongs With Collier County Medicaid Planning Attorneys, Not a General Planner

In my practice, one of the first things I tell Naples and Collier County families is this: Florida takes the unauthorized practice of law around Medicaid planning very seriously. This isn't just a technicality. The Florida Supreme Court has made clear that structuring assets, drafting trusts, and giving legal advice about Medicaid eligibility is the practice of law, and it must be performed by a licensed Florida attorney.

That matters because there is an entire industry of non-attorney "Medicaid planners" who advertise heavily to retirees. These individuals are not licensed by the Florida Bar, are not required to carry malpractice insurance, and are not subject to the same ethical and educational standards attorneys are. If a non-attorney's plan causes your loved one's Medicaid application to be denied or delayed, you may have very limited legal recourse.

⚠ Know Who You're Hiring Before signing any engagement letter or paying a fee, ask directly: "Are you a licensed Florida attorney, and is Medicaid planning part of your active law practice?" A reputable elder law attorney will answer this plainly and show you their Florida Bar number.

Collier County has a number of experienced elder law practitioners in Naples and the surrounding area. When you're comparing firms, look for attorneys who concentrate specifically in elder law and Medicaid planning, not general practitioners who occasionally touch the subject.

How Florida Medicaid Eligibility Actually Works

Florida's Medicaid program for long-term care (sometimes called the Institutional Care Program or the Statewide Medicaid Managed Care Long-Term Care program) has strict financial rules. Understanding them is the starting point for any planning conversation.

  • Income limit: Florida sets a monthly income limit for long-term care Medicaid eligibility. As of 2026, that figure is commonly cited at $2,982 per month for an applicant. If an applicant's income exceeds this cap, they are not automatically disqualified, but they generally need a Qualified Income Trust (often called a Miller Trust) to become eligible.
  • Asset limit: A single Medicaid applicant generally must have no more than $2,000 in countable assets. When only one spouse is applying and the other remains at home, Florida's Community Spouse Resource Allowance rules allow the healthy spouse to keep a significantly larger share of the couple's joint assets. This allowance changes periodically, so current figures should be confirmed at the time of application.
  • Exempt assets: Certain assets are not counted toward the asset limit, most notably a primary residence (subject to equity limits and other conditions), one vehicle, and personal belongings. Homestead protection under Florida law works alongside these Medicaid exemption rules, though the two legal frameworks serve different purposes.

These rules govern eligibility under Florida Statutes Chapter 409, which addresses social and economic assistance programs in Florida, including Medicaid.

The Five-Year Look-Back Period: Why Timing Matters

One of the most misunderstood rules I encounter is the Medicaid look-back period. Florida Medicaid reviews an applicant's financial transactions going back 60 months (five years) from the date of application. Gifts, asset transfers for less than fair value, or certain trust funding during that window can trigger a penalty period during which the applicant is ineligible for benefits, even if they otherwise meet the income and asset limits.

This is precisely why elder law attorneys encourage families to start planning conversations early, ideally years before a nursing home or assisted living need becomes urgent. Early planning allows for strategies that simply are not available, or are far more limited, once a crisis has already begun.

Crisis Planning Still Exists If a loved one already needs care now, don't assume it's too late. Florida elder law attorneys regularly help families with "crisis planning" even after care has started, using legal tools available under current law to protect assets that can still be protected. The options are simply different from those available with five years of lead time.

Common Planning Tools Collier County Elder Law Attorneys Use

Every family's situation is different, and the right approach depends heavily on marital status, the type of care needed, and the family's specific assets. Broadly, the tools an elder law attorney may discuss include:

  • Qualified Income Trusts (Miller Trusts): Used when an applicant's income exceeds Florida's monthly cap, allowing excess income to be redirected so the applicant can still qualify.
  • Asset restructuring within legal limits: Converting countable assets into exempt assets in ways that comply with Medicaid rules and the look-back period.
  • Spousal protections: Ensuring the community spouse (the one remaining at home) retains the maximum resource allowance and income protections available under Florida and federal Medicaid rules.
  • Homestead planning: Coordinating Medicaid exemption rules for a primary residence with Florida's homestead protections under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, and considering how the home will pass at death, including Florida's homestead devise restrictions under F.S. § 732.4015.
  • Coordination with estate planning: Making sure powers of attorney (F.S. Chapter 709), healthcare surrogate designations (F.S. § 765.202), and any trusts are consistent with the Medicaid plan, not working against it.

An experienced Collier County elder law attorney will walk through which of these tools, if any, fit your family's actual facts. There is no one-size-fits-all plan.

What to Ask When Choosing Among Collier County Medicaid Planning Attorneys

Naples and the broader Collier County area have a good number of attorneys who list elder law or Medicaid planning among their services. Not all of them handle it with the same depth. When you're vetting a firm, I'd suggest asking:

  • How much of your practice is dedicated specifically to Medicaid and elder law planning, versus general estate planning?
  • Do you handle both proactive planning (years before care is needed) and crisis planning (care has already started)?
  • Will you personally handle the Medicaid application, or coordinate with the Department of Children and Families on our behalf if needed?
  • How do you coordinate Medicaid planning with our existing will, trust, or power of attorney?
  • What is your fee structure, and is it flat-fee or hourly for this type of work?

A clear, patient answer to each of these questions is a good sign you're speaking with someone who truly practices in this area, not someone who dabbles in it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do Medicaid planning myself without an attorney in Florida?
You can apply for Medicaid on your own, but structuring trusts, transfers, or asset restructuring to become eligible is considered the practice of law in Florida and generally should be handled by a licensed attorney to avoid mistakes that trigger penalty periods.
Is it too late to plan if my family member already needs nursing home care?
No. Elder law attorneys regularly perform crisis planning even after care has begun, using legal strategies still available under current Florida Medicaid rules, though the options are more limited than with advance planning.
Will my house be counted against me for Medicaid eligibility?
A primary residence is generally treated as an exempt asset for Medicaid eligibility purposes, subject to equity limits and other conditions, but the rules around the home change after the Medicaid recipient passes away, so it should be discussed specifically with an elder law attorney.
What happens if I gave money to family within the last five years?
Gifts or transfers for less than fair value made within Florida's 60-month look-back period can create a penalty period that delays Medicaid eligibility, so any past transfers should be disclosed to and reviewed by your elder law attorney before applying.
What is a Miller Trust and do I need one?
A Miller Trust, formally called a Qualified Income Trust, is a legal tool used when an applicant's monthly income exceeds Florida's Medicaid income cap; an elder law attorney can determine whether your specific income situation requires one.
How is a non-attorney Medicaid planner different from an elder law attorney?
A non-attorney Medicaid planner is not licensed by the Florida Bar, is not required to carry malpractice insurance, and providing legal advice on Medicaid planning without a law license can expose that person to criminal liability under Florida law, leaving families with little recourse if something goes wrong.

The Truestead Takeaway

Medicaid planning is one of those areas where the details genuinely matter: Florida's income cap, asset limits, the five-year look-back period, and spousal protections all interact in ways that are easy to get wrong without experience. What I'd tell my own family in Collier County is this: start the conversation with a licensed Florida elder law attorney before a crisis forces your hand, but know that even if care has already begun, options still exist. The sensible next step is a candid review of your specific income, assets, and timeline with an attorney who practices Medicaid planning regularly, not a general recitation of the rules from the internet.
