Florida Lady Bird Deeds

Does a Lady Bird Deed Cover a Mobile Home in Florida?

Quick Answer

A Florida lady bird deed only transfers real property, so it covers the land under a mobile home automatically, but it covers the home itself only if the home's title has been retired so the home legally became part of the real estate; otherwise the home passes separately as personal property.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 25, 2026

Betty's Situation: One Piece of Dirt, Two Kinds of Property

Betty is 80 and lives in a manufactured home on land she owns outright in Zephyrhills. She is a composite, not an actual client, but her situation is one I see often in my practice. She wants her home to pass to her daughter without probate, and someone told her a lady bird deed would take care of it. That is true for the land. It is not automatically true for the home.

A Florida lady bird deed, also called an enhanced life estate deed, lets Betty keep full control of her property for life, including the right to sell it or change her mind, and it passes the property to her named beneficiary at her death without probate court involvement. But a deed is a real estate instrument. It only moves real property. A mobile home that still carries a separate title, the way a car does, is not automatically real property just because it sits on land you own.

When the Mobile Home Is Still Personal Property

Under Florida law, a manufactured or mobile home usually starts out as personal property, titled through the Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles, essentially the same agency and the same concept as a car title. As long as that title stays active, the home is legally separate from the land beneath it, even if Betty owns both.

This matters enormously at death. If Betty's lady bird deed transfers only the land, and her mobile home title is never addressed, the home does not automatically follow the deed. It may need to pass through a different process, sometimes a simplified title transfer through DHSMV, sometimes through probate, depending on how the title is held and whether there is a named beneficiary or joint owner on the title itself.

  • Land with a recorded lady bird deed: passes to the named beneficiary automatically at death.
  • Mobile home with an active DHSMV title and no beneficiary designation: may require separate handling, possibly probate.
  • Result if nothing is done about the title: two different processes, two different outcomes, for one household.

Retiring the Title: Making the Home Part of the Real Estate

The fix, when it fits the homeowner's situation, is often to retire the mobile home's title. Florida allows an owner who owns both the home and the land, and who has permanently affixed the home to that land, to retire the separate personal property title through the county property appraiser and DHSMV. Once that is done, the home is treated as part of the real property, the same as a site-built house.

For Betty, this is the step that lets one lady bird deed do the whole job. If her home's title is retired and the home is legally merged into the land, then her lady bird deed on the land carries the home right along with it. Her daughter would inherit the whole parcel, home included, without probate, the same way she would inherit a conventional house and lot.

Why This Step Gets Missed Many mobile home owners in Florida never retired the title because it was not required to live there, get insurance, or pay property taxes as real estate in every case. It only becomes a problem when the family tries to sell or transfer the property later and discovers the home was never legally joined to the land.

Leased-Lot Communities: Where a Lady Bird Deed Does Not Apply

Many Florida retirees live in mobile home parks or 55-and-older communities where the resident owns the home but leases the lot from the park owner or a cooperative. In that setup, there is no land ownership for a deed to transfer. Betty owns her land outright, so this does not apply to her, but it is worth naming because so many Florida families assume otherwise.

If the land is leased, a lady bird deed cannot be used at all, because there is no real property interest for the deed to convey. The home, if still personal property, passes according to its title and any beneficiary designation on file with DHSMV, or through probate if none exists. The lease itself typically ends or transfers according to the park's own rules and the lease agreement, not through a deed.

Homestead Protection on a Mobile Home

Florida's homestead protections, both the property tax exemption under the Florida Constitution and the creditor protections, are not limited to site-built houses. A mobile home on land the owner occupies as a permanent residence can qualify for homestead status the same as any other home, provided the ownership and occupancy requirements are met.

A lady bird deed does not interfere with that exemption during the owner's lifetime, because the owner keeps a life estate, which is a form of ownership that satisfies homestead rules. This is one reason the lady bird deed is popular for Florida retirees: it avoids probate at death without disturbing the tax savings during life.

⚠ Married Homeowners, Take Note If Betty were married and her mobile home and land were homestead property, Florida's Constitution generally requires her spouse to join in any deed or will that affects that homestead. A lady bird deed prepared without addressing spousal rights can be challenged or clouded later, regardless of whether the underlying asset is a house or a mobile home.

Betty's Paperwork, Put Together

For someone in Betty's position, the practical checklist usually looks like this: confirm whether the mobile home's title has been retired so the home is legally part of the real estate, confirm the legal description of the land matches county records, prepare and record a lady bird deed naming her daughter as beneficiary, and confirm there is no surviving spouse whose joinder would be required.

If the title has not been retired, that step generally needs to happen before, or alongside, the deed work, so that one document does the job the family expects it to do. Frequently Asked Questions

Does a lady bird deed automatically include a mobile home?
Only if the mobile home has already been legally converted to real property by retiring its separate DHSMV title. Otherwise the deed transfers the land, but the home may still need to pass separately.
How do I know if my mobile home's title has been retired?
Check with your county property appraiser's office or DHSMV. If the home is taxed and appraised as real property along with the land, the title has likely been retired; if it still has a separate vehicle-style title, it has not.
Can I use a lady bird deed if I lease my mobile home lot?
No. A lady bird deed only transfers ownership interests in real property, and a leased lot means you do not own the land, so there is nothing for the deed to convey.
Does homestead protection apply to a mobile home in Florida?
Yes. A mobile home used as a permanent residence on land you own can qualify for Florida's homestead tax exemption and constitutional protections, the same as a traditional house, provided the usual ownership and occupancy requirements are met.
If I'm married, does my spouse need to sign the lady bird deed for our mobile home?
If the property is homestead, Florida's Constitution generally requires spousal joinder on a deed affecting it, whether the home is a mobile home or a site-built house. This should be reviewed with a Florida attorney before signing.

The Truestead Takeaway

Betty's case shows why a lady bird deed and a mobile home title are two separate legal questions that happen to sit on the same lot. The land passes cleanly under a properly drafted deed, but the home only travels with it if its title has been retired and merged into the real property; if it hasn't, the home may need its own paperwork or, without planning, its own trip through probate. Anyone in Betty's position, or helping a parent in her position, should have both the land title and the mobile home title reviewed together, not separately, before assuming one document covers everything.
