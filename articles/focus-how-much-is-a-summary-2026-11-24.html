How Much Is a Summary (Administration) in Florida Probate?

Quick Answer

In Florida, "summary" refers to summary administration, a simplified probate process available when the non-exempt estate is worth $150,000 or less (for deaths on or after July 1, 2026) or when the decedent has been dead more than two years. Court filing fees run a few hundred dollars, and attorney fees for handling the process typically range from about $1,500 to $3,500.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney November 24, 2026

What Does "Summary" Mean in Florida Probate?

When Floridians search "how much is a summary," they are almost always asking about summary administration, Florida's shortened form of probate under Chapter 735 of the Florida Statutes. It is not a separate document or a report you pay someone to write. It is a court process, an alternative to full, formal probate, that lets smaller or older estates close out faster and with less expense.

In a summary administration, no personal representative is appointed and no "letters of administration" are issued. Instead, the court enters an order that distributes the decedent's assets directly to the beneficiaries or heirs named in the petition. That streamlined structure is exactly why people ask how much it costs and how it compares to a full probate case.

How Much Does an Estate Need to Be Worth to Qualify?

The dollar figure most people are really asking about is the eligibility threshold, the maximum size an estate can be and still use summary administration.

Florida raised this threshold through CS/HB 1337 (Chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida), effective July 1, 2026. Under the updated law:

  • Summary administration is now available when the value of the entire estate subject to administration in Florida, less property exempt from creditor claims, does not exceed $150,000.
  • This higher $150,000 cap applies to decedents who pass away on or after July 1, 2026. Estates of people who died before that date are generally measured against the prior, lower threshold that was in effect at the time of death.
Why many estates qualify even when they look bigger on paper: Homestead property and other assets exempt from creditors' claims are not counted toward the $150,000 cap. A family home, for example, often does not count against the limit, which means an estate that looks much larger than $150,000 on the surface may still qualify for summary administration.

The Other Path: Two Years Since Death

Many people do not realize there is a second, completely separate way to qualify for summary administration, and it has nothing to do with the dollar value of the estate. Under Florida law, summary administration is also available whenever the decedent has been dead for more than two years, regardless of how large the estate is.

This time-based path exists because Florida's statute of limitations generally bars most creditor claims against an estate after two years from death. Once that window has closed, the law allows families to use the simpler summary process even for larger estates, since there is little risk of outstanding creditors coming forward.

So, How Much Does Summary Administration Actually Cost?

Cost comes in two pieces: the court's filing fee and the attorney's fee for preparing and handling the petition.

  • Clerk filing fees are modest and set by the local clerk of court. Current published fees are generally around $345 for a summary administration involving an estate valued at $1,000 or more, and around $235 for an estate valued at less than $1,000.
  • Attorney fees are not set by statute for summary administration the way they sometimes are in formal probate. Most Florida attorneys charge a flat fee for a straightforward summary administration, commonly somewhere in the range of $1,500 to $3,500. The exact fee depends on the number and type of assets, whether there are any creditor issues, and how many beneficiaries need to be identified and served.
⚠ A note on variability: These figures are general ranges based on common practice, not a quote for any specific case. An estate with real estate, out-of-state assets, unclear heirs, or a contested creditor claim can cost more and take longer, even within the summary administration process. Your actual cost should be discussed directly with the attorney handling your matter.

What Else Changed in the 2026 Update?

The same 2026 legislation that raised the summary administration threshold also increased a related small-estate limit. The amount of a decedent's personal property that can pass to heirs without any formal administration at all, sometimes called a "disposition without administration," was raised from $10,000 to $20,000. Together, these changes reflect a broader legislative effort in Florida to make small and mid-size estates easier and less costly to settle, keeping more families out of lengthy, expensive formal probate.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is a summary administration in Florida?
There is no single price, but you can generally expect a clerk filing fee of a few hundred dollars plus an attorney's flat fee, commonly in the range of $1,500 to $3,500, depending on the complexity of the estate.
What is the estate value limit for summary administration in Florida?
For deaths on or after July 1, 2026, the non-exempt estate must be valued at $150,000 or less. For deaths before that date, the older, lower threshold that was in effect at the time of death generally applies.
Does the family home count toward the $150,000 limit?
Often no. Homestead property and other assets exempt from creditors' claims are generally excluded from the calculation, so an estate that looks larger on paper may still qualify.
Can I use summary administration if the estate is worth more than $150,000?
Yes, if the decedent has been dead for more than two years. In that situation, Florida law allows summary administration regardless of the estate's size.
Is summary administration the same as not needing probate at all?
No. Summary administration is still a court process, just a shorter and simpler one than formal probate. Very small amounts of personal property may qualify for an even simpler disposition without administration, now up to $20,000 under the 2026 update.
Do I need an attorney for summary administration in Florida?
Florida does not always require an attorney for summary administration, but because the petition, notice requirements, and asset descriptions must meet specific legal standards, many families choose to have a Florida probate attorney prepare and file it.

The Truestead Takeaway

If you are asking how much a summary administration costs or whether your family's estate qualifies, the honest answer is that it depends on the estate's value, what's exempt, and how long it has been since the death. The 2026 increase to a $150,000 threshold means more Florida families than ever will qualify for this faster, less expensive alternative to formal probate. The sensible next step is to have a Florida probate attorney review the actual assets, debts, and date of death before you file anything, so you use the right process the first time.
