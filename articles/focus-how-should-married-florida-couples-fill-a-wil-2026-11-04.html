How Should Married Florida Couples Fill A Will

Quick Answer

In Florida, each spouse should fill out and sign a separate will (Florida does not recognize one joint will for both spouses), each will must meet the state's strict signing and witnessing rules, and both spouses need to account for Florida's built-in protections for surviving spouses, including the elective share and special homestead rules, before deciding how to leave property to each other.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney November 4, 2026

The short answer: two wills, not one

When clients ask me how married Florida couples should fill a will, the first thing I clear up is that there is no such thing, under Florida law, as a single joint will that both spouses sign together. A will is treated as a personal statement of one person's wishes. So each spouse needs their own, separate will.

What most married couples actually want, and what I help them build, are mirror image wills: two separate documents that are coordinated with each other, often leaving property to the surviving spouse first and then to the same group of beneficiaries (usually children) if the spouse doesn't survive. The wills are similar in structure and intent, but each spouse signs their own document, with their own witnesses, and each can technically be changed later unless the couple has entered into a mutual will agreement.

  • Separate wills: Each spouse has an individual document reflecting their own final wishes.
  • Mirror image wills: Coordinated separate wills, commonly used by married couples, often leaving everything to the surviving spouse first.
  • Mutual will agreements: A written agreement, incorporated into each spouse's will, that the terms won't be changed or revoked. These are more common when there are children from a prior relationship and both spouses want to lock in certain provisions.

How Florida law requires a will to be filled out and signed

However a married couple decides to divide their property, the document itself has to be executed exactly the way Florida Statute Section 732.502 requires, or it risks being thrown out entirely in probate. In my practice, I see more wills fail on these formalities than on anything involving what the person actually wanted.

  • The testator (the person making the will) must be at least 18 years old, or an emancipated minor, and of sound mind when signing.
  • The will must be in writing and signed at the end by the testator, or by someone else signing on the testator's behalf, in the testator's presence and at the testator's direction.
  • The testator must sign, or acknowledge an already existing signature, in the presence of at least two witnesses.
  • Those two witnesses must then sign the will in the presence of the testator and in the presence of each other.

Florida also allows wills to be made self proving under Section 732.503. This involves the testator and witnesses signing sworn affidavits before a notary or other officer authorized to take oaths, with a certificate attached to the will. A self proved will speeds up probate considerably because the court doesn't need to track down witnesses later to confirm the signatures were valid.

⚠ Formalities matter more than intent A will that clearly expresses what someone wanted, but that skips a witness signature or isn't signed in the right order, can be declared void in Florida. Good intentions do not substitute for correct execution.

Florida's spousal protections that override what a will says

This is the part that surprises a lot of married couples: Florida law builds in protections for spouses that can apply regardless of what a will says, or even if there's no will mentioning the marriage at all.

  • Pretermitted spouse protection: If someone marries after signing their will and never updates it, and that spouse survives them, the surviving spouse generally receives a share equal to what they would have gotten if there had been no will at all, unless the will shows an intent not to provide for the new spouse, the spouse is already provided for elsewhere (like a prenuptial agreement), or the spouse waived their rights.
  • Elective share: Under Florida Statute Section 732.2065, a surviving spouse generally cannot be fully disinherited. They have the right to claim an elective share equal to 30% of the elective estate, even if the will says otherwise, unless that right was validly waived in a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement.

I tell couples this isn't a reason to panic, it's a reason to plan with full information. If both spouses understand these protections going in, there are rarely surprises, and in most long term marriages the couple wants to provide generously for each other anyway. The protections mainly matter in second marriages, blended families, or situations where a prenuptial agreement already addresses these rights.

Homestead property has its own special rules in a will

If the couple owns a Florida homestead, the will cannot simply say "leave the house to whoever I want." Florida Statute Section 732.4015 restricts how homestead property can be devised when the owner is survived by a spouse or minor child.

  • If there's a surviving spouse and no minor children, the owner can leave the homestead to the spouse, but generally cannot leave it to someone else instead of the spouse without the spouse's agreement.
  • If there's a minor child, the homestead generally cannot be left outright to anyone, including the surviving spouse, by will. The law directs how it passes instead.

This trips people up constantly. A couple might draft a will that says "I leave my homestead to my spouse," assuming that settles it, not realizing that if they have a minor child, a different rule under Florida law determines how that homestead actually passes, regardless of the will's language. This is one of the clearest reasons homestead planning for married couples with children should be reviewed by a Florida attorney rather than handled with a generic template.

Why this matters for mirror image wills: Couples often assume "leave everything to my spouse, then to the kids" works automatically for the house. With Florida homestead, that assumption can be wrong, especially with minor children in the picture.

Electronic wills: a newer option for Florida couples

Florida law also permits electronic wills. An electronic signature can satisfy the signing requirement, and witnesses can appear and sign electronically through qualified audio-video communication technology, so long as the process is supervised by a notary public following the applicable statutory requirements. This can be convenient for couples who want to execute coordinated wills at the same time without both being in the same physical room, though the technical requirements are detailed and must be followed precisely to avoid the same kind of execution problems that invalidate paper wills.

For most married couples I work with, traditional paper execution with a self proving affidavit remains the simplest, most court-tested route, but electronic wills are a legitimate and growing option worth discussing with your attorney.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can married couples in Florida sign one will together?
No. Florida does not recognize a single joint will signed by both spouses. Each spouse needs their own separate will, even if the two wills are coordinated (often called mirror image wills).
Does a Florida will automatically leave everything to the surviving spouse?
Not automatically. The will has to say so. However, Florida law does give a surviving spouse certain protections, like the elective share and pretermitted spouse rights, that can apply even when a will doesn't provide for them or wasn't updated after marriage.
Can a Florida spouse be completely disinherited in a will?
Generally, no. Under Florida Statute Section 732.2065, a surviving spouse can claim an elective share equal to 30% of the elective estate, regardless of what the will says, unless that right was validly waived by prenuptial or postnuptial agreement.
How many witnesses does a Florida will need?
Two. The testator must sign, or acknowledge their signature, in the presence of two witnesses, and those witnesses must sign in the presence of the testator and each other, as required under Florida Statute Section 732.502.
Can a married couple leave their homestead to anyone they choose?
Not always. If there's a surviving spouse or minor children, Florida Statute Section 732.4015 limits how homestead property can be devised in a will, and the usual rules a couple might expect do not necessarily apply.
Are electronic wills valid for married couples in Florida?
Yes, Florida law allows electronic wills, including electronic signatures and remote witnessing by audio-video technology, as long as the process is supervised by a notary public and meets the statutory requirements.

The Truestead Takeaway

For married Florida couples, the safest path is almost never a single shared document, it's two properly executed, coordinated wills that account for Florida's spousal protections and, if you own a home, the state's homestead devise rules. These are areas where a technically correct looking will can still fail to do what you intended, particularly with homestead property or blended families. If you and your spouse haven't had your wills reviewed together, or you're starting from scratch, it's worth sitting down with a Florida estate planning attorney to make sure both documents work the way you expect them to.
