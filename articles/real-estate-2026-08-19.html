Florida's Proposed Homestead Exemption Expansion: What Homeowners Should Know Before November 2026

Quick Answer

Florida voters will decide in November 2026 whether to significantly expand the homestead property tax exemption, potentially raising the non-school portion to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028 — but nothing changes yet, and the current $25,000 base exemption plus the CPI-indexed additional exemption remains in place today.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Real Estate Attorney August 19, 2026

What's Actually on the Ballot This November

If you've heard chatter at the dock, the clubhouse, or your homeowners' association meeting about a big change to Florida property taxes, you're hearing about something real — but it isn't law yet. In my practice, I try to separate what's proposed from what's in effect, because clients sometimes plan around news headlines before the ink is dry.

Earlier this year, the Florida Legislature passed a joint resolution proposing a constitutional amendment to expand the homestead exemption. It will appear on the November 3, 2026 general election ballot, and under Florida's constitution, a proposed amendment needs 60 percent voter approval to become law. That's a meaningfully higher bar than a simple majority, so nothing is guaranteed.

As proposed, the measure would keep a $25,000 exemption applied against school district taxes, but would raise the exemption applied against non-school taxes — the county, city, and special-district portion of your bill — to as much as $150,000 starting in 2027, and up to $250,000 starting in 2028, for homeowners who are permanent Florida residents as of the end of this year.

What Florida's Homestead Exemption Looks Like Right Now

For 2026, before any ballot measure takes effect, Florida's homestead exemption structure is what most longtime residents already know: a base $25,000 exemption that applies to all property taxes, including school taxes, and an additional exemption of a similar size that applies only to non-school taxes on the portion of assessed value between $50,000 and $75,000. Combined, that additional tier now adjusts annually based on the Consumer Price Index, a change Florida voters approved back in November 2024.

This homestead exemption is separate from — but works alongside — the protections in Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, which shields a Florida homestead from forced sale by most creditors, and the Save Our Homes assessment cap under F.S. § 193.155, which limits how much your home's assessed value can increase each year for tax purposes regardless of what's happening to market value.

  • Homestead exemption: Reduces the taxable value of your home.
  • Save Our Homes cap: Limits annual increases in assessed value.
  • Portability: Lets you carry accumulated Save Our Homes savings to a new Florida homestead.
  • Article X, § 4 protection: Shields the homestead from most creditor claims, separate from taxes entirely.

These are four distinct protections that often get lumped together in casual conversation, but each has its own rules and its own statute.

Who Would Benefit, and Who Should Pay Attention

Under the proposal, eligibility for the expanded, higher exemption amounts would hinge on being a permanent Florida resident as of December 31, 2026 — meaning the property is your legal homestead, not a second home, rental, or investment property. Snowbirds who split time between Florida and another state should think carefully about whether their Florida property currently qualifies as their permanent residence, because homestead status has always required more than just owning Florida real estate; it requires Florida being your true, permanent home.

Why this matters for local budgets: A significant expansion of the non-school exemption would reduce revenue for counties, cities, and special districts that rely on property taxes. That's part of why the measure requires the higher 60% voter threshold, and why some local governments have raised concerns publicly. None of that affects your rights as a homeowner today — it's simply useful context for understanding the debate.

What This Means for Deeds, Trusts, and Estate Planning Right Now

I regularly meet with clients who want to transfer their Florida homestead into a revocable trust, add a family member to the deed, or use a Lady Bird deed (an enhanced life estate deed) to pass the home to heirs outside of probate. A frequent question I get is whether a pending or future change to the homestead exemption should change that plan. Generally, it shouldn't — the exemption amount is a property-tax question, while your deed, trust, and estate plan address ownership, transfer at death, and creditor protection, which are governed by different rules entirely.

That said, how you title your homestead does matter. Transferring a homestead into a properly drafted revocable trust under Florida law typically preserves both the homestead tax exemption and the constitutional protections, but the details matter — and a poorly drafted transfer can jeopardize either the exemption, the devise restrictions on homestead property under F.S. § 732.4015, or both. A Lady Bird deed, by contrast, lets you retain full control of the property during your lifetime while automatically passing it to your named beneficiaries at death, without probate and generally without disturbing your homestead exemption.

⚠ A Word of Caution: I've seen homeowners use do-it-yourself deed templates found online to add a child's name to the title or attempt a Lady Bird deed on their own. Florida's homestead devise rules are unusually strict, and an improperly drafted deed can create unintended co-ownership, trigger unwanted tax reassessment, complicate a future sale, or fail entirely to accomplish the Medicaid or probate-avoidance goal it was meant to serve. This is an area where a small drafting mistake can have large, sometimes irreversible, consequences.

Closing Costs, Documentary Stamps, and What Buyers and Sellers Should Expect in 2026

For anyone buying or selling in Florida this year, the tax landscape around the transaction itself hasn't changed. Florida imposes a documentary stamp tax under F.S. § 201.02 on the transfer of real property, generally figured at a set rate per $100 of the sales price, with a modestly different structure in Miami-Dade County. Sellers typically pay this tax at closing along with the cost of an owner's title insurance policy, while real estate commissions remain the largest single line-item cost for most sellers.

Florida law doesn't require either party to purchase title insurance, but it's standard practice in nearly every financed transaction, and I recommend it in cash transactions too — a clean title search and an owner's policy protect you against liens, errors, or competing claims that surface after you've already closed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Has the Florida homestead exemption already increased for 2026?
No. The current 2026 exemption remains the existing $25,000 base plus the CPI-indexed additional exemption. The proposed expansion to $150,000 and eventually $250,000 would only take effect if voters approve it on the November 3, 2026 ballot, with changes phased in starting in 2027.
What vote threshold does the amendment need to pass?
Florida constitutional amendments require 60 percent voter approval, a higher bar than a simple majority, so the outcome is not guaranteed even with legislative support.
Does transferring my homestead into a trust affect my exemption?
It can be done without losing the exemption, but only if the trust is properly drafted to comply with Florida's homestead and trust rules. This is an area where individualized legal review matters.
Is a Lady Bird deed the same as a quitclaim deed?
No. A quitclaim deed transfers your interest immediately with no retained rights, while a Lady Bird (enhanced life estate) deed lets you keep full control and the ability to sell or change your mind during your lifetime, with the property passing to your named beneficiary automatically at death.
Do I need title insurance when buying property in Florida?
Florida law does not require it, but it's standard practice in nearly all transactions and provides important protection against undiscovered title defects or claims.
Should snowbirds worry about the proposed homestead changes?
Only Florida properties that qualify as your permanent, legal homestead would benefit from the expanded exemption if it passes, so snowbirds should confirm their residency and homestead status rather than assume eligibility.

The Truestead Takeaway

The proposed homestead exemption expansion is a genuine, significant development worth watching, but it's a ballot measure, not current law, and it addresses your tax bill, not your deed, your trust, or your estate plan. Whether you're deciding how to title your Florida homestead, considering a Lady Bird deed, or preparing to buy or sell this year, the sensible next step is the same one I'd give my own family: have your specific deed, trust, or closing documents reviewed by a Florida attorney before you sign anything, so the plan actually accomplishes what you intend.
