Medicaid Compliant Annuity: What Florida Families Need to Know

Quick Answer

A Medicaid compliant annuity converts a Florida applicant's excess countable savings into a stream of income, so those dollars no longer count against Medicaid's asset limit. To work, it must meet strict federal requirements, including naming the State of Florida as a beneficiary, or the entire purchase can be treated as a disqualifying gift instead of a protective planning tool.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney October 10, 2026

What Is a Medicaid Compliant Annuity?

In my practice, I meet Florida families most often at the worst possible moment: a parent or spouse needs nursing home care right now, and they've just learned that Medicaid will not pay a dime until countable assets are reduced to a very small number. A Medicaid compliant annuity is one of the few tools that can help in that exact crisis, because it doesn't require giving assets away or waiting out a penalty period. Instead, it converts a lump sum of cash into a fixed stream of monthly income.

Here's the plain-English version of why that matters: Florida's Medicaid program for long-term care counts cash, investments, and most savings as available resources. Income, by contrast, is treated differently. When structured correctly, an annuity takes a pile of countable savings and turns it into an income stream that Medicaid does not count as a resource at all, which can allow an applicant to qualify for benefits far sooner than they otherwise could.

The catch, and it's a significant one, is that the annuity must satisfy a specific set of federal rules. An ordinary commercial annuity you might buy from an insurance agent almost certainly will not qualify. It has to be purpose-built to meet Medicaid's requirements, which is why these are sometimes called 'DRA annuities,' a reference to the federal Deficit Reduction Act that tightened these rules nationally.

The Federal Rules Every Compliant Annuity Must Follow

Because Florida's Medicaid program for long-term care operates under federal Medicaid law, the requirements for a compliant annuity come from federal statute, not a Florida-specific code section. In practical terms, a Medicaid compliant annuity generally must be:

  • Irrevocable: once purchased, the terms, including the payment amount, duration, and beneficiaries, cannot be changed, and the annuity has no cash surrender value.
  • Non-assignable: it cannot be sold, transferred, or used as collateral.
  • Actuarially sound: the payment term must be based on recognized life expectancy tables for the annuitant, so the payments are expected to return the full purchase price during that person's expected lifetime.
  • Paying in equal installments: typically equal monthly payments, with no balloon payments or deferred lump sums at the end.
  • Naming the State of Florida as a remainder beneficiary: the state's Medicaid agency must be named to receive any remaining payments, up to the amount of benefits paid on the recipient's behalf, if the annuitant dies before the annuity is exhausted. Florida must be named in first position, or second position if there is a community spouse or a minor or disabled child who is named ahead of the state.
⚠ Why This Matters So Much Missing even one of these requirements, especially the beneficiary designation naming the State of Florida, does not just create a small penalty. Federal law treats a non-compliant annuity purchase as a disqualifying transfer of the entire purchase price, which can create a lengthy period of Medicaid ineligibility exactly when a family can least afford it.

Who Actually Uses a Medicaid Compliant Annuity in Florida?

In my experience, these annuities come up most often for married couples, specifically to protect the spouse who is not entering a nursing home (often called the 'community spouse'). Florida Medicaid rules allow that spouse to keep a certain amount of the couple's countable assets, known as the Community Spouse Resource Allowance, but amounts above that allowance normally have to be spent down before the applicant spouse qualifies.

A Medicaid compliant annuity offers another path: rather than spending down excess assets on care (or giving them away and waiting out a transfer penalty), the couple can use those excess funds to purchase a compliant annuity in the community spouse's name. This converts the excess assets into an income stream for the community spouse, who gets to keep that income, while the applicant spouse's countable assets are reduced enough to qualify for Medicaid.

Single applicants can sometimes use this strategy too, though it is less common and requires even more careful planning, since the applicant spouse is typically both the owner and the person whose life expectancy drives the payment schedule, and the state will be the primary remainder beneficiary.

Florida's 2026 Asset and Income Figures (and Why You Should Verify Them)

Florida's Medicaid asset limits and spousal allowances are adjusted periodically, and the specific dollar figures change from year to year. As a general matter, a single Medicaid long-term care applicant in Florida must typically reduce countable assets to a very small threshold, commonly cited around $2,000, and a community spouse is allowed to keep a protected resource allowance that is recalculated periodically and has historically fallen in the range of roughly $150,000 to $165,000, depending on the year.

A Note on Numbers I have intentionally avoided locking in a single, precise 2026 dollar figure for Florida's Community Spouse Resource Allowance in this article, because the figures I've seen from different sources for 2026 do not agree with each other. These allowances are indexed and do change. Before relying on any specific number for a real application, confirm the current figure directly with the Florida Department of Children and Families or with an elder law attorney who is tracking the current year's published figures.

Why This Is Not a Do-It-Yourself Project

I say this to every family who calls me about a Medicaid compliant annuity: the annuity itself is only one piece of a larger Medicaid planning puzzle, and it has to be coordinated with everything else, including the five-year lookback period for prior transfers, the timing of the Medicaid application, and whether other planning tools (like a properly drafted trust, or spending on exempt assets such as a homestead or a vehicle) make more sense for a particular family's situation.

Very few insurance carriers even offer a true Medicaid compliant annuity product, because the payment terms and interest rates required to meet the actuarial soundness rule are narrow and often unattractive compared to a typical commercial annuity. An attorney experienced in Florida elder law can help identify an appropriate product, confirm the beneficiary designations meet federal requirements, and make sure the annuity purchase is timed correctly relative to a Medicaid application, so a protective strategy doesn't accidentally become a disqualifying transfer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a Medicaid compliant annuity the same as a regular annuity?
No. A regular commercial annuity almost never meets Medicaid's requirements. A Medicaid compliant annuity must be irrevocable, non-assignable, pay in equal installments, be actuarially sound, and name the State of Florida as a remainder beneficiary, among other conditions.
What happens if the annuity doesn't meet all the federal requirements?
If even one requirement is missing, Medicaid can treat the full purchase price of the annuity as a disqualifying transfer of assets, which can create a significant period of ineligibility for benefits.
Can a single Medicaid applicant in Florida use this strategy, or only married couples?
Married couples use this strategy most often, typically to protect assets for the community spouse, but single applicants can sometimes use a compliant annuity as part of a broader plan. It requires careful structuring because the state will be the primary remainder beneficiary.
Does buying a Medicaid compliant annuity trigger the five-year lookback penalty?
A properly structured compliant annuity that meets all federal requirements is generally not treated as a disqualifying transfer, which is the entire point of the tool. However, the lookback period still applies to other gifts or transfers made in the prior five years, so the annuity has to be evaluated alongside the applicant's full transfer history.
How much does a Medicaid compliant annuity cost to set up?
The research available does not support a specific fee figure, and costs vary by insurance carrier, annuity size, and the attorney fees involved in structuring and reviewing the plan. A Florida elder law attorney can give a more specific estimate after reviewing a family's particular assets and goals.
Where can I find the current Florida Medicaid asset limits and spousal allowance figures?
These figures are updated periodically and should be confirmed directly through the Florida Department of Children and Families or with a Florida elder law attorney, since published figures from different sources can vary and this article intentionally avoids stating an unverified exact dollar amount for the current year.

The Truestead Takeaway

A Medicaid compliant annuity can be a genuinely powerful tool for a Florida family facing a long-term care crisis, but it only works when every federal requirement is met precisely, and it only makes sense as part of a coordinated plan that accounts for the five-year lookback, the current asset and income limits, and the rest of the family's goals. If you or a loved one is facing a Medicaid spend-down situation, the sensible next step is to have a Florida elder law attorney review the full financial picture before any annuity is purchased, not after.
