Florida's 2026 Probate Reform: Bigger Thresholds, Faster Estates, New Enforcement Tools

Quick Answer

Effective July 1, 2026, Florida significantly raised the dollar thresholds that let smaller estates skip formal probate, and gave personal representatives a new legal tool to compel uncooperative banks and financial institutions to honor valid letters of administration.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 12, 2026

What Actually Changed on July 1, 2026

In my practice, I hear the same worry from families every week: "Do we really have to go through full-blown probate for this?" As of July 1, 2026, the answer is "probably not" for a lot more Florida families than before. Florida's Legislature passed a package of probate reforms — most notably CS/HB 1337 (Chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida) and CS/HB 131 (Chapter 2026-85, Laws of Florida) — that grew out of the Florida Supreme Court's Workgroup on Uncontested Probate Proceedings. Both bills passed the Legislature unanimously, which tells you this wasn't controversial policy — it was housekeeping that was long overdue.

The headline change: summary administration — Florida's simplified, faster probate alternative to formal administration — is now available for estates valued at $150,000 or less (excluding exempt property), up from the old $75,000 ceiling that had been in place for years. That threshold lives in Florida Statutes §§ 735.201–735.2063, as amended effective July 1, 2026.

The Legislature also raised two smaller but meaningful dollar figures: the value of an intestate estate that can be disposed of without any administration at all increased from $10,000 to $20,000, and a surviving spouse or child can now collect up to $5,000 of a decedent's income tax refund without opening a probate estate — double the old $2,500 limit.

Why the $150,000 Summary Administration Threshold Matters

Summary administration is meaningfully faster and less expensive than formal administration — there's no personal representative to formally appoint, less court supervision, and typically fewer attorney's fees. For years, the $75,000 ceiling meant that even a modest Florida home, a car, and a bank account could push a family into full formal probate simply because property values had climbed while the statute stood still.

Doubling that threshold to $150,000 means many more Florida estates — particularly smaller estates involving a paid-off condo, some savings, and personal property — can now qualify for the streamlined process. This won't eliminate probate for everyone, and it doesn't change the analysis for estates with real property still carrying debt, contested heirs, or complex assets. But for a lot of ordinary Florida families, this is a genuine simplification.

  • Summary administration cap: raised from $75,000 to $150,000 (excluding exempt property)
  • Disposition without administration: raised from $10,000 to $20,000
  • Income tax refund collection without probate: raised from $2,500 to $5,000

New Enforcement Power When Banks Won't Cooperate

One of the quieter but genuinely useful pieces of this reform addresses a frustration I've seen play out in real time with client families: a personal representative has valid letters of administration from the court, but a bank or financial institution still refuses to release funds, demands extra paperwork the law doesn't require, or simply stalls.

CS/SB 1500 created a new statute — Florida Statutes § 733.6125 — that gives personal representatives a direct path to court when a financial institution won't recognize valid letters or imposes conditions the law doesn't authorize. This is effective July 1, 2026, alongside the other probate changes.

What this means practically: A personal representative no longer has to simply absorb months of delay from an uncooperative institution. There is now a defined statutory mechanism to bring the matter before the probate court and seek enforcement.

The Florida Supreme Court followed up quickly: on July 16, 2026, it issued Opinion No. SC2026-0690, amending the Florida Probate Rules in response to Chapter 2026-57. Among the changes, Rule 5.025 now classifies proceedings to enforce a personal representative's authority under this new statute as adversarial in nature, unless a court orders otherwise — meaning it follows a more formal, contested-litigation track within the probate case rather than the informal, non-adversarial procedures used for routine estate administration.

Trust Law Also Shifted in 2025 — Still Relevant Now

Separately from this year's probate reforms, Florida updated its trust statutes through Senate Bill 262, effective June 20, 2025, and these changes remain very much in play for anyone administering or drafting a Florida trust in 2026.

  • Florida Statute § 736.04117 now gives "authorized trustees" broader flexibility to modify an existing trust or move ("decant") its assets into a new trust — even one created solely for that purpose — subject to statutory guardrails.
  • Florida Statute § 736.1110 clarifies how lifetime gifts a settlor made can count toward, or satisfy, future distributions the trust otherwise calls for.

If you have a revocable living trust (governed generally by Florida Statutes Chapter 736) that hasn't been reviewed since before mid-2025, these provisions are worth discussing with your attorney — particularly the decanting flexibility, which can matter a great deal for older trusts that no longer fit a family's current circumstances.

What Hasn't Changed

It's worth being clear about what this legislative session did not touch. I did not find, and am not aware of, any 2026 statutory changes to Florida's homestead protections under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, the homestead devise restrictions in F.S. § 732.4015, the Save Our Homes assessment cap and portability rules under F.S. § 193.155, or Medicaid estate recovery rules affecting elder law planning. Those remain governed by existing, well-established Florida law.

⚠ Don't assume your old plan automatically benefits. Raised thresholds and new enforcement tools are helpful, but they don't retroactively simplify an estate that's already in formal administration, and they don't substitute for a will, trust, or beneficiary designations that are actually up to date. If your estate plan predates 2025, it's worth a review.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the new $150,000 threshold mean my family's estate automatically avoids probate?
It means the estate may qualify for summary administration, Florida's simplified probate process, rather than being required to go through full formal administration — but whether it actually qualifies depends on the specific assets, debts, and circumstances, which should be reviewed with a Florida probate attorney.
When exactly did these probate changes take effect?
The core statutory changes from Chapter 2026-57 and Chapter 2026-85, Laws of Florida, took effect July 1, 2026, and the related Florida Probate Rules amendments were adopted by the Florida Supreme Court on July 16, 2026.
What is the new statute personal representatives can use against uncooperative banks?
Florida Statutes § 733.6125, effective July 1, 2026, lets a personal representative bring a court proceeding when a financial institution refuses to honor valid letters of administration or imposes conditions the law doesn't require.
Are these enforcement proceedings against banks handled the same as routine probate matters?
No. Under the Florida Supreme Court's amendment to Probate Rule 5.025, these enforcement proceedings are treated as adversarial in nature — meaning a more formal, contested process — unless the court orders otherwise.
Do the 2025 trust law changes affect trusts I already have?
They can. Senate Bill 262's changes to trustee decanting authority (F.S. § 736.04117) and how lifetime gifts count toward distributions (F.S. § 736.1110) apply to the administration of existing Florida trusts, which is a good reason to have an older trust reviewed.
Did anything change for homestead property or Medicaid planning in 2026?
Not based on currently available information. Florida's homestead protections, Save Our Homes cap, and Medicaid estate recovery framework remain governed by existing law; always confirm current rules with a Florida elder law attorney before relying on them.

The Truestead Takeaway

This year's probate reforms are genuinely good news for Florida families — higher thresholds mean more estates can use the faster, less expensive summary administration process, and the new enforcement statute gives personal representatives real leverage against institutions that drag their feet. But thresholds and tools only help if your underlying plan — will, trust, beneficiary designations — is current and correctly drafted for how Florida law works today. If your estate plan or trust predates 2025, or if you're currently serving as a personal representative running into resistance from a bank, it's worth having a Florida attorney review your specific situation rather than assuming the old rules still apply.
