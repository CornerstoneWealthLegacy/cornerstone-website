What Does a Real Estate Attorney in Parrish, FL Actually Do?

When Floridians search for a real estate attorney Parrish FL, they're usually facing one of a handful of situations: buying or selling a home, dealing with a title problem, resolving a boundary or easement dispute, or trying to understand how their property fits into a broader family or estate plan. Parrish has grown quickly in recent years, and with that growth comes more property transactions, more new construction closings, and more questions about how Florida law protects (or exposes) a homeowner's biggest asset.

In my practice, I tell clients that a real estate attorney's job is to make sure the paperwork matches reality, and that reality matches Florida law. That includes:

  • Reviewing or preparing purchase contracts and closing documents
  • Conducting or reviewing a title search to catch liens, judgments, or ownership gaps before closing
  • Preparing and recording deeds correctly, including deeds that account for homestead status
  • Resolving boundary line disputes, easement conflicts, or HOA issues
  • Advising on land use, zoning, or permitting questions for a specific parcel

Not every closing needs a lawyer in the room, but any transaction with a wrinkle, an inherited property, an unusual title history, a family transfer, or a homestead question, benefits from having an attorney review the file before signing.

Why Your Parrish Property and Your Florida Estate Plan Are Connected

Here's something many homeowners don't realize until it's a problem: your home isn't just a real estate asset, it's also an estate planning asset, and Florida treats it differently than almost any other state.

Florida's homestead protections come from Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, and they provide strong creditor protection for a primary residence. But that same constitutional protection creates restrictions on who you can leave your homestead to. Under Florida Statute § 732.4015, if you have a spouse or minor children, you generally cannot devise (leave by will) your homestead property to anyone other than your spouse and/or minor children, with narrow exceptions. This surprises a lot of people who assume they can leave their house to anyone they choose.

This is where a real estate attorney and an estate planning attorney's work overlaps directly. If you're buying property in Parrish, refinancing, adding a family member to a deed, or transferring property into a trust, the way that deed is drafted can affect your homestead exemption, your property tax treatment under Florida's Save Our Homes cap (F.S. § 193.155), and how that property passes when you die.

Why this matters: A deed that looks simple on paper (for example, adding a child's name to the title) can unintentionally affect your homestead tax exemption, your Save Our Homes portability, or your ability to leave the home the way you intended. It's worth having both the real estate side and the estate planning side reviewed together.

Do I Need a Real Estate Attorney or an Estate Planning Attorney in Parrish?

Many Florida firms, including Truestead Law, handle both because the two areas so often intersect. Here's a general way to think about which need is which:

  • Real estate attorney focus: buying or selling property, title defects, boundary disputes, contract disputes, closing review, new construction issues, landlord-tenant matters
  • Estate planning attorney focus: wills, revocable trusts, powers of attorney, healthcare directives, and making sure your home and other assets pass the way you want without unnecessary probate delay
  • Overlap situations: transferring your home into a revocable trust, adding a family member to a deed, handling an inherited property from a loved one's estate, or resolving a homestead question after a death in the family

If your situation is a straightforward purchase or sale, a real estate-focused attorney is usually the right fit. If you're dealing with a property that's part of a larger family, inheritance, or long-term planning picture, it's worth talking to an attorney who understands both sides.

Avoiding Probate Delays on Florida Real Estate

One of the most common reasons Parrish homeowners eventually need legal help isn't a purchase or sale at all, it's what happens to the property after a loved one passes away without proper planning. Florida probate is a public, court-supervised process, and it can take significantly longer, and cost significantly more, than families expect.

Florida probate attorney fees for ordinary services are set by statute as a percentage of the estate's value, with additional fees possible for extraordinary services. On an estate worth several hundred thousand dollars, that can mean tens of thousands of dollars in legal fees alone, before court costs, before the months (sometimes longer) the family waits for the property to be transferred or sold.

A revocable living trust, governed under Florida Statutes Chapter 736, is one of the most common tools Florida homeowners use to avoid this outcome. When a home is properly titled in the name of a trust, it generally passes to beneficiaries without going through probate court, on your timeline, privately, and often faster.

⚠ A word of caution: Moving homestead property into a trust, or adding someone to a deed, has real consequences for tax exemptions, creditor protection, and Medicaid planning. This is not a do-it-yourself deed change. Have both the deed and your overall estate plan reviewed together before recording anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a real estate attorney to close on a home in Parrish, FL?
Florida law does not require an attorney at every residential closing, but many buyers and sellers choose to have one review the contract and title work, especially when there's an unusual title history, a family transfer, or homestead property involved.
Can I leave my Parrish home to anyone I want in my will?
Not necessarily. Under Florida Statute § 732.4015, if you have a spouse or minor children, homestead property generally must pass to them rather than to another person, with limited exceptions. This is a constitutional protection built into Florida law, not just a will-drafting choice.
What's the difference between a real estate attorney and an estate planning attorney?
A real estate attorney typically handles transactions like purchases, sales, title issues, and disputes. An estate planning attorney handles wills, trusts, powers of attorney, and how assets pass at death. Many firms, including Truestead Law, handle both because Florida homestead and property law connect the two areas closely.
How does putting my home in a trust affect my Florida homestead exemption?
In many cases a properly drafted revocable trust can preserve homestead tax exemption and creditor protection, but the deed and trust language must be handled correctly. This is an area where the details matter, and it should be reviewed by an attorney familiar with both real estate and estate planning.
Why does probate take so long for Florida real estate?
Florida probate is a court-supervised, public process with statutory fee structures and required notice periods, which can extend the timeline for transferring or selling real estate that wasn't already in a trust or otherwise set up to avoid probate.
