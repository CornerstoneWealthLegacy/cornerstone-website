Florida's July 2026 Probate Reform: What Changed and What It Means for Your Family

Quick Answer

As of July 1, 2026, Florida raised the summary administration threshold from $75,000 to $150,000, increased several small-estate transfer limits, and created new enforcement tools so personal representatives can force cooperation from banks and other institutions holding a decedent's assets.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney July 20, 2026

What Actually Changed on July 1, 2026

In my practice, I spend a fair amount of time explaining to Florida families why an estate that sounds "simple" still ends up in a lengthy, formal probate case. As of July 1, 2026, that conversation gets easier for a lot of people, thanks to Chapter 2026-57, Laws of Florida (originating as CS/HB 1337). The Legislature passed it unanimously, and it puts into effect recommendations from a Florida Supreme Court workgroup that spent roughly two years studying how to make routine, uncontested probate cases faster and less expensive.

The headline change is to summary administration — the streamlined, faster alternative to full probate. Under amended Florida Statutes section 735.201, an estate can now qualify for summary administration when the value of the property subject to administration in Florida, minus property that's exempt from creditors, does not exceed $150,000. Before July 1, 2026, that ceiling was $75,000. Doubling it means many more middle-class Florida estates — a modest bank account, a car, some personal property, maybe a small investment account — can skip formal administration entirely.

Florida's probate rules were also amended to match: the Florida Supreme Court adopted corresponding changes to Probate Rules 5.025, 5.425, and 5.530, effective alongside the statute.

Smaller Estates Get Even Simpler Treatment

The reform didn't stop at summary administration. Several smaller-dollar thresholds moved up as well, all aimed at letting families access a decedent's modest assets without court involvement:

  • Disposition without administration — the simplest possible process, generally used for very small intestate estates with no real property — increased from $10,000 to $20,000.
  • Federal income tax refunds payable directly to a surviving spouse or child without probate increased from $2,500 to $5,000.
  • Bank account funds that a financial institution may release directly to a qualifying family member without a court order increased from $1,000 to $2,000.

None of these numbers are enormous, but together they matter. A surviving spouse trying to close out a modest joint account or collect a final tax refund often just needs the bank or the IRS to cooperate — not a judge's order. Raising these thresholds means fewer families have to open a probate case just to reach a few thousand dollars.

A New Tool Against Stonewalling Institutions

Here's the part of this reform that I think will matter most in practice, even though it doesn't grab headlines the way a dollar-threshold increase does. Anyone who has served as a personal representative knows the frustration: you have a court order, you have letters of administration, and yet a bank, brokerage, or safe-deposit box custodian drags its feet, demands paperwork the law doesn't require, or simply doesn't respond. Historically, the personal representative's only real recourse was to sue — and the cost of that litigation often exceeded whatever was at stake, so institutions faced little practical consequence for delay.

New Florida Statutes section 733.6125 changes that calculus. In a proceeding to enforce a personal representative's authority under the Probate Code, the court is now required to award taxable costs, including attorney fees, to a prevailing personal representative. That's a meaningful shift — it puts financial pressure back on institutions to cooperate promptly rather than stall.

Safe-Deposit Boxes, Clarified Chapter 2026-57 also confirms that a Florida-appointed personal representative must be given access to a decedent's safe-deposit box, may remove its contents, and may pay any accumulated rental charges and terminate the lease — closing off a recurring source of friction between families and banks.

What This Means If You're Settling an Estate Now

If you're currently administering a Florida estate, or expect to soon, these changes are worth discussing with your attorney regardless of when the death occurred — the new thresholds and enforcement tools apply to proceedings going forward, not just to future deaths. An estate that didn't qualify for summary administration under the old $75,000 limit may qualify now under the new $150,000 ceiling, which can meaningfully shorten the timeline and reduce cost.

It's also a good moment to revisit your own estate plan. Summary administration and the small-estate transfer rules are backstops for families who didn't use a revocable trust or other probate-avoidance planning under Florida Statutes Chapter 736. They're helpful, but they're not a substitute for a plan tailored to your assets, your family, and your wishes. What I tell Florida clients is that these reforms make the safety net a little wider — they don't replace the value of building your own plan in advance.

⚠ One Caution These thresholds are about the value of the probate estate — not necessarily your entire net worth. Homestead property, certain exempt assets, and non-probate assets (like jointly titled property or accounts with beneficiary designations) are treated differently. Whether a particular estate actually qualifies for summary administration depends on the full picture, and that should be reviewed by a Florida probate attorney.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the new $150,000 summary administration threshold apply to estates where the person already died before July 1, 2026?
Generally, procedural changes like this apply to proceedings filed or pending on or after the effective date, so many estates being administered now can benefit from the higher threshold. The specifics should be confirmed with a Florida probate attorney based on your case's current status.
Does real property count toward the $150,000 summary administration limit?
Yes, real property located in Florida generally counts toward the value of the estate subject to administration, though property that is constitutionally exempt homestead is typically excluded from that calculation. How your specific property is characterized should be reviewed by an attorney.
Can I still use summary administration if there are debts or creditors?
Summary administration can still be available with creditors, but the process and protections differ from formal administration, and the personal representative or petitioner needs to handle creditor claims carefully. This is an area where individualized legal review matters.
What is the new fee-shifting rule under section 733.6125 actually meant to fix?
It's designed to stop institutions like banks from ignoring or delaying a personal representative's legitimate requests, since previously the cost of litigating exceeded the cost of simply complying. Now a prevailing personal representative can recover attorney fees and costs in an enforcement action.
Do these changes affect trusts or only probate estates?
Chapter 2026-57 is a probate reform and doesn't rewrite the Florida Trust Code. Trust administration continues to be governed primarily by Florida Statutes Chapters 736 through 739, along with other recent trust-law updates.
Should I redo my estate plan because of this law?
Not necessarily — this reform mainly streamlines court processes for smaller estates rather than changing what plan is right for you. It's still worth an occasional review with your attorney, especially if your estate's value or family circumstances have changed.

The Truestead Takeaway

This reform is genuinely good news for Florida families dealing with modest estates — it means more people can avoid formal probate, collect small balances faster, and push back effectively when an institution drags its feet. But thresholds and exemptions are fact-specific, and the right path for a given estate — summary administration, disposition without administration, formal probate, or something a trust would have avoided altogether — still depends on the details. If you're currently settling a loved one's estate, or if your own plan hasn't been reviewed in a while, now is a sensible time to talk with a Florida probate attorney about how these changes apply to you.
