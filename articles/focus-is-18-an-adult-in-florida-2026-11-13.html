Is 18 an Adult in Florida?

Quick Answer

Yes. In Florida, a person becomes a legal adult at age 18 under Florida Statutes section 743.07, which is Florida's age of majority statute. At 18, a Floridian gains the right to sign contracts, vote, consent to their own medical care, and manage their own finances, though a few specific rights (like drinking alcohol at 21) kick in later.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney November 13, 2026

Is 18 an Adult in Florida? The Direct Answer

Yes. In my practice, this is one of the most common questions I get from parents of high school seniors and young adults heading off to college: is 18 an adult in Florida? The answer is straightforward. Under Florida Statutes section 743.07, the age of majority in Florida is 18. Once a person turns 18, Florida law treats them as a legal adult, with full authority to make their own decisions, sign their own documents, and be legally responsible for their own affairs.

This single statute is the backbone of Florida's legal definition of adulthood. It applies broadly across contract law, healthcare decision-making, and personal autonomy. What surprises a lot of families, though, is that turning 18 does not automatically mean a parent can still make medical or financial decisions for that young adult, or that every other age-based right or restriction also shifts to 18. That is where the details matter.

What Does Turning 18 Actually Change in Florida?

Once a Floridian reaches their 18th birthday, they gain real, meaningful legal capacity. Here is what I tell Florida families to expect:

  • Contracts: An 18-year-old can sign binding contracts, including leases, loan agreements, and employment contracts, without a parent's signature.
  • Voting: Florida residents can register to vote and cast a ballot once they turn 18.
  • Medical decision-making: At 18, a person has the legal right to consent to their own medical treatment and to access their own medical records. This is a big one, because it also means a parent generally loses the automatic legal right to get medical information about their adult child or to make treatment decisions for them.
  • Financial independence: An 18-year-old can open bank accounts, apply for credit, take out loans, and is personally responsible for any debt they incur.
  • Marriage and legal name changes: Adults 18 and older can marry without parental consent and can petition for a legal name change on their own behalf.

This last point about healthcare is why I often recommend that families of a newly-turned-18-year-old consider two simple documents: a healthcare surrogate designation under Florida Statutes section 765.202, and a durable power of attorney under Florida Statutes Chapter 709. These let a young adult name someone, often still a parent, who can step in and help with medical or financial decisions if the young adult is unable to act for themselves, such as after an accident or during a serious illness. Without these documents in place, even a loving parent may have no legal authority to help.

What Doesn't Change at 18 in Florida

Here is the part that trips people up: Florida sets 18 as the general age of majority, but several specific rights and restrictions are tied to different ages entirely. Reaching 18 does not unlock everything at once.

  • Alcohol: The legal drinking age in Florida remains 21, regardless of the general age of majority.
  • Firearm purchases from licensed dealers: Since 2018, Florida law (Florida Statutes section 790.065) has made it a third-degree felony for a licensed dealer to sell or transfer most firearms to anyone under 21.
  • Child support: Child support obligations in Florida typically end when a child turns 18, but Florida Statutes section 743.07 allows a court to extend support past 18 in specific situations, such as when the child has a qualifying mental or physical incapacity that began before age 18, or when the child is between 18 and 19, still in high school, and reasonably expected to graduate before turning 19.
  • Car rental and certain insurance rates: Many private companies set their own age minimums above 18 for things like car rentals, which is a business policy rather than a Florida law.
A Note on Emancipation: Florida law also allows a minor younger than 18 to become legally emancipated, meaning a court grants them many of the rights and responsibilities of an adult before their 18th birthday. This generally requires either a legal marriage or, for minors 16 or older, a court petition demonstrating that emancipation serves the minor's best interest. Emancipation is a separate legal process and is not automatic.

Why This Matters for Estate Planning

In my estate planning practice, the moment a child turns 18 is a quiet but important milestone that a lot of families overlook. Once your son or daughter is a legal adult under Florida Statutes section 743.07, you as a parent no longer have automatic authority over their healthcare or finances, even though you may still be paying for college or supporting them in every practical way.

This is why I encourage parents of newly-adult children to have a conversation about two documents: a healthcare surrogate designation (so you can be contacted and make medical decisions if your adult child is incapacitated) and a durable power of attorney (so you can help manage finances, insurance claims, or paperwork if needed). These are not complicated or expensive documents, but without them, a parent may find themselves locked out of a hospital conversation or unable to help with a simple financial matter at the exact moment their adult child needs them most.

⚠ A Common Misunderstanding: Many parents assume that because they are still listed as an emergency contact, or because their child is still on the family health insurance plan, they automatically retain legal decision-making authority. Once a child turns 18, that assumption is incorrect under Florida law. Legal authority to access records or make decisions requires a properly executed document, not just a family relationship.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 18 the legal age of adulthood in all situations in Florida?
For most purposes, yes, 18 is Florida's general age of majority under Florida Statutes section 743.07. However, specific exceptions exist, such as the drinking age of 21 and firearm purchase restrictions from licensed dealers until age 21.
Can a parent still access a child's medical records once they turn 18 in Florida?
Not automatically. Once a child turns 18, they alone have the legal right to consent to their own medical treatment and control access to their medical records, unless they sign a document, such as a healthcare surrogate designation, authorizing a parent to be involved.
Does child support automatically end when a child turns 18 in Florida?
Generally yes, but Florida Statutes section 743.07 allows a court to extend support if the child has a qualifying disability that arose before age 18, or if the child is between 18 and 19, still enrolled in high school, and expected to graduate before turning 19.
What is emancipation, and is it different from turning 18?
Emancipation is a separate legal process that can grant a minor younger than 18 many adult legal rights and responsibilities, typically through marriage or a court petition available to minors 16 or older. It is not automatic and requires court involvement, unlike simply reaching 18.
What documents should an 18-year-old in Florida consider having in place?
Many Florida families have a newly-adult child sign a healthcare surrogate designation and a durable power of attorney, so a trusted person, often a parent, can step in to help with medical or financial decisions if the young adult is ever unable to act for themselves.
Can an 18-year-old sign a will or other estate planning documents in Florida?
Yes, an 18-year-old generally has the legal capacity needed to execute a will under Florida's will execution requirements and to sign other estate planning documents, provided they meet the applicable competency and formal execution requirements.

The Truestead Takeaway

Turning 18 in Florida is a real legal milestone: under Florida Statutes section 743.07, your son or daughter becomes a legal adult with the right to make their own medical, financial, and legal decisions, and parental authority does not automatically carry over. What I tell Florida families is simple: treat the 18th birthday as a planning checkpoint, not just a celebration. A short conversation about a healthcare surrogate designation and a durable power of attorney can make all the difference if your young adult is ever in an accident or medical emergency. If you have a child approaching or past 18, it is worth having your family's documents reviewed by a Florida attorney to make sure everyone involved has the legal authority they expect to have.
