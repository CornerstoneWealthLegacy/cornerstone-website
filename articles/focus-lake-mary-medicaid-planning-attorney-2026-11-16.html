Lake Mary Medicaid Planning Attorney: Protecting Savings Before a Nursing Home Bill Arrives

Quick Answer

A Lake Mary Medicaid planning attorney helps Seminole County families legally qualify a loved one for Florida Medicaid long-term care benefits, often through tools like Qualified Income Trusts and properly drafted powers of attorney, without forcing the family to exhaust every dollar of savings first.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney November 16, 2026

What does a Lake Mary Medicaid planning attorney actually do?

When a family in Lake Mary, Heathrow, or anywhere else in Seminole County is facing a nursing home stay, the first question is almost always the same: do we have to spend everything down before Medicaid will help? In my practice, I tell clients that the honest answer is usually no, not if you plan correctly and get the right documents in place before (or shortly after) a crisis hits.

A Lake Mary Medicaid planning attorney focuses on helping applicants meet Florida's strict income and asset tests for long-term care Medicaid (often called nursing home Medicaid) while protecting as much of the family's savings and home equity as the law allows. That work typically includes:

  • Reviewing countable versus exempt assets and restructuring ownership where appropriate
  • Establishing a Qualified Income Trust (also called a Miller Trust) when a client's income exceeds Florida's cap
  • Drafting or updating a durable power of attorney with the specific Medicaid planning authority Florida law requires
  • Advising on the five-year lookback period and any prior gifts or transfers
  • Coordinating protections for a healthy spouse who remains in the community

This is not a do-it-yourself process. Florida's Medicaid rules are detailed, and small mistakes, like an improperly signed power of attorney or an undocumented transfer to a family member, can delay eligibility for months.

What are Florida's 2026 Medicaid income and asset limits?

Florida is what's called an income cap state. That means there is a hard ceiling on gross monthly income for long-term care Medicaid eligibility, and unlike some states, Florida does not allow an applicant to simply spend down excess income each month to qualify. For 2026, a single applicant whose gross monthly income from all sources exceeds roughly $2,982 is considered over the income limit.

On the asset side, a single applicant generally cannot hold more than $2,000 in countable assets on the date of application. Countable assets typically include things like bank accounts, investment accounts, and additional real estate. Certain assets are exempt, including, in many cases, a primary Florida homestead, one vehicle, and prepaid burial arrangements that meet specific requirements.

Married couples get more breathing room. When one spouse needs nursing home care and the other remains at home, Florida's spousal impoverishment rules allow the community spouse to retain a larger share of the couple's combined assets and a protected amount of income, so the healthy spouse is not left destitute.

What is a Qualified Income Trust, and why might I need one?

Because Florida does not permit an income spend-down, an applicant whose income exceeds the cap cannot simply qualify by paying down medical bills. Instead, Florida law authorizes a specific tool under F.S. § 409.6225: the Qualified Income Trust, sometimes called a Miller Trust.

In simple terms, excess income is deposited each month into this irrevocable trust account rather than being counted directly against the applicant. Once the trust is properly established and funded, the income no longer counts toward Florida's income cap for Medicaid purposes. For 2026, this trust becomes mandatory for any applicant whose gross monthly income exceeds the state's threshold (approximately $2,901 to $2,982, depending on the specific benefit being sought).

Setting up a Qualified Income Trust correctly, and keeping it funded and administered properly every single month, is one of the most common reasons families in Lake Mary bring in an elder law attorney rather than attempting this process alone through the state's online application.

Can I protect my home, savings, or give gifts to my family before applying?

This is where Florida's five-year lookback period matters most. When someone applies for long-term care Medicaid, the Department of Children and Families reviews financial records going back five years to check for gifts or transfers made for less than fair value. If an improper transfer is found, it can trigger a penalty period during which the applicant is ineligible for benefits, even though they may now have very little money left.

This is exactly why proactive planning, done well before a crisis, is so valuable. Tools that may help include certain types of irrevocable trusts, properly structured personal care agreements with family caregivers, and careful use of exempt assets like the homestead. It's worth noting that a revocable trust offers no Medicaid protection under F.S. Chapter 736, because the person creating it still controls the assets. Only irrevocable arrangements, structured and timed correctly, can shield resources from being counted.

⚠ Timing matters. Gifts and transfers made within five years of applying for long-term care Medicaid can create a penalty period of ineligibility. If a loved one may need nursing home care in the near future, any past transfers should be reviewed with an attorney before applying, not after.

What documents does Florida law require for Medicaid planning?

A standard, generic power of attorney is often not enough to carry out Medicaid planning strategies in Florida. Under F.S. § 709.2202, a durable power of attorney must contain specific, individually initialed authority (sometimes called "superpowers") before an agent can take certain Medicaid planning actions on someone else's behalf, such as creating or funding trusts, making gifts within Medicaid guidelines, or restructuring assets. Florida law does not allow a single blanket authorization to cover all of these powers; each one must be separately initialed by the person signing the document.

Without this specific authority already in place, a family member may have to petition the court for a guardianship under Chapter 744 just to get the legal authority to plan, which is slower, more expensive, and far more public than having the right documents signed in advance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to spend down all of my savings before Florida Medicaid will help with nursing home costs?
Not necessarily. While Florida does require an applicant's countable assets to fall under a strict limit, properly structured planning, such as irrevocable trusts, exempt asset purchases, and spousal protections, can preserve more of a family's resources than an unplanned spend-down would.
What is the Florida Medicaid income limit for 2026?
For 2026, a single long-term care Medicaid applicant whose gross monthly income exceeds roughly $2,982 is considered over Florida's income cap. Anyone over that amount generally needs a Qualified Income Trust to become eligible.
What happens if I gave my child money or property in the last five years?
Florida applies a five year lookback period to most gifts and transfers made before a long-term care Medicaid application. Transfers made for less than fair value during that window can trigger a penalty period of ineligibility, so prior gifts should be reviewed carefully with an attorney before applying.
Will my spouse lose our house and savings if I need nursing home care?
Florida's spousal impoverishment rules are designed to prevent that outcome. A community spouse (the one remaining at home) is generally allowed to keep a protected amount of income and assets, and the primary homestead typically receives strong protection when residency requirements are met.
Can I use my regular power of attorney for Medicaid planning?
Often not. Florida law under F.S. § 709.2202 requires specific, individually initialed authority for an agent to take certain Medicaid planning actions, such as creating trusts or making gifts. A generic or older power of attorney may need to be updated before it can be used for this purpose.
Is a revocable living trust helpful for Medicaid planning?
Generally no. Because the person who created a revocable trust still controls the assets inside it, those assets usually remain countable for Medicaid purposes. Irrevocable trusts, structured and timed correctly, are the tools typically used for asset protection in this context.

The Truestead Takeaway

Medicaid planning in Florida is a matter of precise timing and precise paperwork, not guesswork. The rules around income caps, the five year lookback, Qualified Income Trusts, and spousal protections are specific and unforgiving of small mistakes, but they also offer real, legal ways to protect a family's savings and home when structured correctly. If you or a loved one in the Lake Mary area may need nursing home care now or in the next few years, the sensible next step is to have your financial picture and existing documents reviewed by a Florida elder law attorney before you apply, not after.
