Florida Lady Bird Deeds

Both Deeds Say Life Estate. Here Is the Difference That Matters

Quick Answer

An enhanced (lady bird) life estate deed lets the owner sell, mortgage, or change beneficiaries alone, while an ordinary life estate deed gives the remainder beneficiaries a real, present interest that generally requires their signature before the property can be sold or refinanced, and can trigger a Medicaid transfer penalty.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 25, 2026

Carmen's Situation

Carmen is 74 and lives in Kissimmee. She is a composite of the kind of client I see often in my practice, not an actual person I have represented. Back in 2019, Carmen signed a life estate deed that named her son as the remainder beneficiary, thinking it worked the same way as the "lady bird deed" she had heard her neighbors talk about. Now she wants to sell her house and move closer to her daughter in Ocala. She has just learned those two deeds are not the same thing at all, and the difference is about to matter a great deal.

A quick refresher: with either type of deed, the owner keeps the right to live in the home for life. The property then passes to the named beneficiaries at death without going through probate. Where the two deeds part ways is what the owner can do with the property while still alive.

The Power to Sell, Mortgage, or Change Your Mind

This is the heart of the difference, and it comes down to a few words of drafting language.

  • Enhanced life estate deed (lady bird deed): The deed explicitly states that the owner keeps full power and authority to sell, mortgage, rent, or otherwise deal with the property in fee simple, without needing the remainder beneficiaries to sign off or even be told. The owner can also revoke the deed or name different beneficiaries entirely.
  • Ordinary life estate deed: The deed creates a present, vested interest in the remainder beneficiaries the moment it is signed. In most cases, selling or mortgaging the property afterward requires the life tenant and the remainder beneficiaries to agree and sign together. The life tenant generally cannot remove a beneficiary or undo the arrangement without that beneficiary's cooperation.

Lawyers sometimes describe this as the remainder interest being vested in an ordinary life estate deed, versus subject to divestment in an enhanced deed, meaning the owner can wipe out the beneficiary's interest at any time simply by selling the property or recording a new deed.

For Carmen, this is the whole problem. Her 2019 deed gave her son a real ownership interest in the house the day it was recorded. She cannot simply list the house and sign a closing statement on her own.

Why the Medicaid Rules Are Not the Same

Because an ordinary life estate deed hands over a real interest in the property right away, Florida's Medicaid program treats it as a completed gift of the remainder interest. If Carmen or anyone in her position applies for Medicaid long term care benefits within the look back period after signing that kind of deed, the transfer can trigger a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay for care.

A lady bird deed works differently. Because the owner keeps full control, including the power to revoke the deed or sell the home outright, Florida's Medicaid policy treats the recording of an enhanced life estate deed as no transfer at all. Nothing has really left the owner's hands until death. That is one of the main reasons families choose the enhanced version over the traditional one when Medicaid planning is part of the goal.

Why this matters for Carmen: the type of deed she signed in 2019 does not affect her Medicaid eligibility today unless she has applied or plans to apply for long term care benefits. But it very much affects her ability to sell the house right now, on her own.

What Carmen Has to Do Now

Carmen's 2019 deed named her son as remainder beneficiary and gave him a vested interest in the home. To sell the house and move near her daughter, Carmen generally needs her son's cooperation. In practice, this usually means one of two paths:

  • Her son joins in the deed to the new buyer, formally releasing his remainder interest so the sale can close with clear title.
  • Carmen and her son together sign a new deed that undoes the old arrangement, then Carmen is free to sell, refinance, or later record a new lady bird deed naming whoever she wishes.

Either approach depends entirely on her son's willingness to sign. If the family relationship is good, and in Carmen's case it is, this is usually a straightforward fix. It does mean an extra document, another notary and witness signing, and another trip to the courthouse to record it in Osceola County. Had Carmen's original deed been drafted as an enhanced life estate deed instead, she would have been able to sell the home entirely on her own, list it, negotiate, and close, without ever needing her son's signature.

⚠ A note on homestead: if Carmen were married and the home is her homestead, Florida law would also require her spouse to join in any deed affecting the homestead, on top of whatever the life estate deed requires. A homestead cannot be conveyed or devised by a married owner acting alone.

Fixing an Ordinary Life Estate Deed Going Forward

For families in Carmen's position, the good news is that an ordinary life estate deed is not a permanent trap. With the remainder beneficiary's cooperation, it can be unwound and, if desired, replaced with a properly drafted enhanced life estate deed that restores the parent's full control. This requires the remainder beneficiary's signature, a new deed with the enhanced language a title examiner expects to see, and recording in the county where the property sits. Florida has no statute that creates the lady bird deed; it rests on long standing case law, title standards published by The Florida Bar, and decades of practice, which is exactly why the specific reserved powers language in the deed has to be drafted correctly the first time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Carmen just sell the house without her son's permission?
Generally no. Because her 2019 deed is an ordinary life estate deed, her son holds a vested remainder interest, and in most cases the life tenant and remainder beneficiary must both sign to convey clear title to a buyer.
Is there a Florida statute that governs lady bird deeds?
No. Florida has no statute creating the lady bird deed. It is recognized through long standing case law, title standards published by The Florida Bar, and established title insurance practice.
Does Florida have a transfer on death deed as an alternative?
No. Florida does not offer a transfer on death deed for real estate. The lady bird deed and the ordinary life estate deed are the tools Florida families use to avoid probate on a home.
If Carmen's son signs a new deed to release his interest, does he owe any tax?
Releasing or transferring a remainder interest can have documentary stamp tax and gift tax implications depending on the facts, and this should be reviewed with a Florida attorney or tax professional before signing.
Would an enhanced life estate deed have avoided this problem for Carmen?
Yes. If Carmen's original deed had included enhanced life estate language reserving her full power to sell, mortgage, or revoke without her son's joinder, she could sell the home today without needing his signature at all.
Does recording a lady bird deed affect Medicaid eligibility?
Under current Florida Medicaid policy, recording a lady bird deed is generally not treated as a transfer because the owner keeps full control, including the power to revoke it, unlike an ordinary life estate deed which can trigger a transfer penalty if Medicaid is applied for within the look back period.

The Truestead Takeaway

Carmen's 2019 deed did exactly what an ordinary life estate deed is designed to do: it locked in her son's future ownership the day it was signed, which is precisely why she needs his cooperation to sell now. That is not a mistake, it is simply a different tool than the one many families think they are getting when they hear the phrase life estate. If you or a parent signed a deed years ago and are not sure whether it reserved full power to sell, mortgage, and change beneficiaries, or whether it needs a remainder beneficiary's signature before anything can be done, have a Florida attorney read the actual deed. The fix is usually straightforward with everyone's cooperation, and going forward, a properly drafted enhanced life estate deed can keep that same flexibility in the owner's hands for good.
