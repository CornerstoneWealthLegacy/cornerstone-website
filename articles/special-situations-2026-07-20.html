Estate Planning for Special Situations: Blended Families, Snowbirds & Business Owners

Quick Answer

Florida's default inheritance and elective-share laws often don't match what blended families, snowbirds, or business owners actually want — a stepchild inherits nothing unless named, a spouse can claim a share of the estate regardless of the will, and dual-state residents risk having two states fight over their estate. A tailored plan, not the default statute, is what actually protects your family.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney July 19, 2026

Why "One-Size-Fits-All" Estate Planning Doesn't Work for Everyone

In my practice, I see the same pattern again and again: a family assumes that because they have a will, or because Florida is a friendly place to live, their estate will sort itself out the way they intend. For a lot of Florida families, that's true. But for blended families, snowbirds splitting time between two states, business owners, and families with a special-needs loved one, Florida's default rules can produce results nobody actually wanted.

That's not a flaw in the law — it's just what happens when a general statute meets a specific family situation. The fix isn't complicated, but it does require a plan built around your facts, not just a form pulled off a shelf.

Blended Families: Why Your Stepchildren May Inherit Nothing

Here's what surprises a lot of remarried clients: under Florida law, a stepchild is not treated as your legal descendant unless you legally adopted them (Fla. Stat. § 732.108). That means if you die without specifically naming a stepchild in your will or trust, they generally receive nothing from your estate — no matter how close your relationship was.

At the same time, Florida gives a surviving spouse real protections that override a will, including the elective share under Fla. Stat. § 732.2065 and constitutional homestead rights. In plain terms, a spouse generally cannot be completely cut out of the estate, and homestead property has its own rules about who can inherit it, regardless of what the will says.

Put those two rules together, and you get the classic blended-family trap: leave everything to your new spouse, and your children from a prior marriage may end up with nothing once your spouse passes — because your spouse is free to change their own will later. Leave everything divided among children, and your spouse could be left without enough to live on.

  • Wills and trusts should specifically name stepchildren if you want them to inherit.
  • A marital trust can let a surviving spouse benefit from certain assets during their lifetime while locking in what eventually passes to your children.
  • A written waiver of spousal elective-share rights, entered into knowingly and with proper disclosure, is a tool some blended families use — but it has real legal requirements and should be prepared by an attorney, not assumed to be a simple form.
What I tell blended-family clients: The goal isn't choosing between your spouse and your kids. The goal is a structure — often separate trusts or a marital trust — that provides for both, in the order and amount you actually intend.

Snowbirds: Which State Actually Claims You?

Florida's lack of a state income tax and lack of a separate estate tax make it an attractive legal home for retirees who split their year between Florida and a northern state. But attraction isn't the same as legal residency, and if you don't clearly establish Florida as your domicile, your former state may still claim you — and your estate — for tax and probate purposes.

Steps that help establish Florida domicile include obtaining a Florida driver's license, registering to vote in Florida, filing a Declaration of Domicile with your county clerk, moving significant financial accounts and personal property to Florida, and spending the majority of the year in the state while keeping records of your time here.

This matters even more for residents of certain northern states with their own estate tax and exemption thresholds — some of which include "cliff" provisions where exceeding the exemption by even a modest amount can subject the entire estate to tax, not just the excess. If you split time between Florida and a state with its own estate tax, this is exactly the kind of detail that should be reviewed with an attorney familiar with both jurisdictions, since exemption amounts and thresholds change and should be verified currently rather than assumed.

There's also a practical probate issue: real estate is generally probated in the state where it sits, unless it's titled in a trust or otherwise structured to avoid that. Snowbirds who own a home in Florida and a home up north can end up with two separate probate proceedings unless their plan accounts for both properties.

Business Owners: Protecting the Company You Built

According to the U.S. Small Business Administration, roughly only 30% of family-owned businesses survive into the second generation, and just 12% make it to the third. Estate planning failures are a big part of that statistic — not because families don't care, but because the business plan and the personal estate plan were never coordinated.

For Florida business owners, I generally recommend reviewing:

  • A buy-sell agreement that spells out what happens to your ownership interest at death, disability, or retirement.
  • Your LLC operating agreement or shareholder agreement, to confirm the transfer provisions actually match what your will or trust says.
  • Funding for a buyout — often through life insurance — so the business isn't forced to liquidate assets or take on debt to buy out a deceased owner's interest.
  • Beneficiary designations on any business-owned insurance policies, so proceeds land where the plan intends rather than in probate.

For 2026, the federal gift and estate tax exemption has increased substantially, giving business owners more room to transfer ownership interests during life — sometimes at a discounted value for gift tax purposes when a minority, non-controlling interest is transferred. This is a meaningful planning opportunity, but the exemption amount and rules should be confirmed with your attorney and CPA before acting, since federal tax law changes and individual circumstances vary.

⚠ A Note on Federal Estate Tax Exemption Changes Federal exemption amounts have shifted significantly in recent years and are subject to further legislative change. Don't rely on a number you read somewhere online — confirm the current exemption and portability rules with your attorney before making gifting or transfer decisions.

Special-Needs Beneficiaries: Protecting Benefits While Providing for Family

For families with a loved one who receives means-tested government benefits — such as Supplemental Security Income or Medicaid — an outright inheritance can actually do harm, disqualifying them from benefits they depend on. A properly drafted special-needs trust allows a loved one to receive supplemental support (things benefits don't cover) without jeopardizing eligibility.

This is an area where the details matter enormously — the type of trust, who serves as trustee, and how the trust is funded all affect whether it works the way the family intends. It's not a do-it-yourself project, and I'd encourage any family in this situation to work directly with an attorney experienced in Florida special-needs and elder law planning.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I remarry, does my new spouse automatically inherit everything?
Not necessarily everything, but Florida law gives a surviving spouse significant protections — including the elective share and homestead rights — that generally cannot be waived away simply by a will. The specific outcome depends on your estate plan and should be reviewed with an attorney.
Can I disinherit my stepchildren without doing anything?
Under current Florida law, a stepchild you never legally adopted is not a default heir, so if you do nothing, they typically inherit nothing unless you specifically name them in your will or trust.
I'm a snowbird — how do I know which state is my legal domicile?
Florida looks at objective factors like your driver's license, voter registration, a filed Declaration of Domicile, where your financial accounts are based, and how much time you actually spend in the state. There's no single magic step; it's the overall pattern that establishes domicile.
Does Florida have its own estate tax?
No. Florida does not impose a separate state estate tax on people who died after December 31, 2004. However, snowbirds may still be subject to another state's estate tax depending on their legal domicile.
What happens to my business interest if I die without a succession plan?
Without a buy-sell agreement and coordinated estate plan, your ownership interest generally passes according to your will or Florida's intestacy laws, which can create conflict among surviving family members, co-owners, or heirs who may have no interest in running the business.
Can I leave an inheritance directly to a family member who receives Medicaid or SSI?
An outright inheritance can jeopardize eligibility for means-tested benefits. A properly structured special-needs trust is generally the tool used to provide support without disqualifying the beneficiary — this should be set up with an attorney experienced in this area.

The Truestead Takeaway

None of these situations — blended families, snowbird residency, business ownership, or a special-needs beneficiary — are solved by Florida's default statutes alone; each requires a plan built around your specific family and assets. If any of this sounds like your situation, the sensible next step is a review of your current will, trust, and beneficiary designations with a Florida attorney who can confirm how the law applies to your facts today.
