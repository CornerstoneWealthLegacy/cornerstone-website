Will or Trust? What Florida Families Need to Know in 2026

Quick Answer

A will alone almost always means probate; a properly funded revocable living trust, paired with a simple pour-over will, is the main way Florida families avoid or shrink probate. In 2026, Florida also raised the dollar threshold for simplified summary probate, which matters even for families who stick with a will.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney July 14, 2026

Why This Comes Up in Almost Every Florida Consultation

In my practice, the very first question most Florida clients ask is some version of: "Do I need a trust, or is a will enough?" It's a fair question, because both documents do real work, but they do different work. A will tells the probate court who gets what and who's in charge — but it has to go through probate to be effective. A revocable living trust, when it's actually funded during your lifetime, lets assets pass to your beneficiaries without a probate case at all for anything titled in the trust's name.

Neither document is automatically "better." What matters is matching the tool to your family, your assets, and — increasingly relevant this year — Florida's updated rules for smaller estates.

The 2026 Update: A Higher Threshold for Summary Probate

Florida offers a simplified probate process called summary administration for estates below a certain value, and as of July 1, 2026, that asset threshold rose to $150,000. This is meaningful because summary administration is faster, cheaper, and far less burdensome than full formal probate.

For families whose estate — excluding certain exempt property and homestead — now falls under that higher number, a will (rather than a trust) may get through probate more efficiently than it would have last year. That said, summary administration is still a court process with its own filing requirements, notice periods, and creditor considerations. It is not the same as avoiding probate entirely through a funded trust, and whether an estate actually qualifies depends on the specific assets and debts involved.

Why this matters: If your plan was built years ago around an older, lower probate threshold, it may be worth revisiting whether a will-based plan still makes sense for your family's numbers, or whether a trust remains the better fit.

How a Revocable Living Trust Actually Avoids Probate

Florida's revocable trusts are governed by the Florida Trust Code, Chapter 736 of the Florida Statutes. A revocable trust lets you keep full control of your assets during your lifetime — you can amend it, add assets, remove assets, or revoke it entirely — while naming a successor trustee to step in and distribute assets privately after your death, without a probate case for anything the trust actually owns.

The key word is owns. A trust document by itself does nothing for an asset that was never transferred into it. This is called funding the trust, and it's the step people most often skip:

  • Real estate — Title must actually change. This is typically done by recording a new deed with the county clerk of court, transferring the property from your individual name into the name of the trust.
  • Bank and investment accounts — Ownership must be re-titled or beneficiary/transfer-on-death designations updated to reflect the trust.
  • Business interests and other assets — These generally need a formal assignment into the trust.

Anything left outside the trust at death may still have to go through probate — which is exactly why most trust-based plans include a pour-over will as a backstop.

⚠ Homestead requires extra care Florida homestead property carries special constitutional protections and tax benefits under Article X, Section 4 and Article VII, Section 6 of the Florida Constitution, along with the Save Our Homes assessment cap. Transferring a homestead into a revocable trust can usually be done without losing the homestead exemption, but it typically requires specific language in both the trust and the deed. This is not a do-it-yourself step — get it reviewed before recording anything.

The Pour-Over Will: Your Trust's Safety Net

Even the most carefully funded trust benefits from a companion document called a pour-over will. Under Florida Statute § 732.513, a pour-over will directs that any assets left in your individual name at death — anything you forgot to transfer, or acquired shortly before passing — "pour over" into your already-existing trust, to then be distributed under the trust's terms.

Two rules matter here. First, the trust must already exist (or be signed the same day as the will) and must be clearly identified in the will. Second, a pour-over will is still a will under Florida law, meaning it must meet the same execution formalities as any other will under Fla. Stat. § 732.502: signed by the testator at the end of the document, in the presence of two witnesses, who sign in the testator's presence and each other's.

Most Florida pour-over wills are also made self-proving under Fla. Stat. § 732.503 — adding a notarized affidavit at signing — so the probate court can admit the will without tracking down witnesses later. It's a small extra step at signing that can save real time and expense if the will ever needs to be probated.

Beneficiary Designations Can Override Your Whole Plan

Here's something I see trip up even careful clients: life insurance policies, retirement accounts, and payable-on-death bank accounts pass according to their own beneficiary designation forms — not according to your will or trust. If those forms are outdated, name an ex-spouse, or simply say "estate," they can quietly override years of careful planning.

Whether you choose a will-based plan or a trust-based plan, reviewing beneficiary designations on every account alongside your estate documents is one of the simplest, highest-value things a Florida family can do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I still need a will if I have a revocable living trust?
Yes. A pour-over will works alongside your trust to catch any assets that weren't formally transferred into it during your lifetime, and it's also where you name guardians for minor children — something a trust cannot do.
Does a trust protect my home's homestead tax exemption in Florida?
Generally, yes, when the trust and deed are drafted with the correct language, but homestead has unique constitutional and tax protections that require careful handling — this is worth reviewing with an attorney before any transfer is recorded.
What is 'funding' a trust, and why does it matter so much?
Funding means actually re-titling assets — real estate, accounts, and other property — into the name of the trust. An unfunded trust provides no probate avoidance for the assets left out of it, no matter how well the trust document itself is written.
Does the new $150,000 summary probate threshold mean I don't need a trust?
Not necessarily. It may simplify probate for smaller, straightforward estates, but it doesn't eliminate probate, doesn't address privacy or out-of-state property, and whether it applies depends on your specific assets and debts — it's one factor among several in choosing a plan.
Can I sign a valid will electronically in Florida?
Florida does allow electronic wills, but they involve specific requirements — including signing before a notary and using a qualified custodian to store both the eWill and the recorded notary session — that differ from a traditional paper will.
What happens to my retirement account if I forget to update the beneficiary form?
Retirement accounts and life insurance generally pass according to the beneficiary designation on file with the institution, regardless of what your will or trust says, so outdated forms can unintentionally redirect assets away from your intended plan.

The Truestead Takeaway

Choosing between a will and a trust isn't about which document sounds more sophisticated — it's about matching your plan to your assets, your family, and Florida's current probate rules, including the 2026 increase in the summary administration threshold. If you already have a trust, the real question is whether it's actually funded; if you're relying on a will, it's worth understanding what probate will look like for your estate under today's rules. Either way, I'd encourage you to have your plan, your deeds, and your beneficiary designations reviewed together by a Florida attorney rather than in isolation.
