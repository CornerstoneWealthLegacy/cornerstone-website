Moving to Florida Estate Planning: What New Residents Need to Know

Quick Answer

Your out-of-state will, trust, and powers of attorney generally remain legally valid after you move to Florida, but Florida's homestead protections, spousal rights, and probate procedures are different enough that most relocating families need at least some updates to make their plan work the way they expect.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 5, 2026

Is My Out-of-State Will Still Valid in Florida?

In my practice, this is usually the first question a new Florida resident asks me, and the reassuring answer is: yes, generally. If your will, trust, or other estate planning document was properly executed under the laws of the state where you signed it, Florida will typically recognize it as valid. Florida law doesn't require you to throw out a will just because you crossed the state line.

But valid is not the same as optimal. A will drafted in New York, Ohio, or Illinois was written with that state's rules in mind — not Florida's homestead protections, not Florida's spousal rights, and not Florida's specific requirements for who can serve as your personal representative. Your document may still "work," but it may not work the way you and your original attorney intended, and it can create unnecessary friction, delay, or expense for your family during probate.

This is why moving to Florida estate planning review is so important even when nothing is technically broken. A document that was airtight in your old state can have real gaps once Florida law is applied to it.

Florida Homestead: The Rule That Surprises the Most New Residents

Florida's homestead protections are unlike anything most other states have, and they are written directly into our state constitution (Art. X, § 4, Fla. Const.), not just statute. Once your Florida home qualifies as your homestead, powerful protections and restrictions attach automatically — whether your will mentions them or not.

Here's the part that catches people off guard: if you're survived by a spouse or minor child, Florida law restricts who you can leave your homestead to in your will. Under F.S. § 732.4015, an attempt to devise homestead property to someone other than a surviving spouse or minor child can be disregarded by the probate court, and the property will instead pass according to Florida's homestead succession rules — regardless of what your will says.

⚠ Common Mistake New Florida homeowners sometimes leave their home to an adult child, a trust, or someone other than a spouse without realizing Florida's homestead rules may override that instruction if they're survived by a spouse. This is one of the most frequent — and most avoidable — surprises I see in Florida probate.

Homestead also brings valuable benefits, including protection from most creditor claims against the home and, once you file for the homestead exemption with your county property appraiser, potential property tax savings under Florida's Save Our Homes cap (F.S. § 193.155) and portability rules if you're moving from another Florida homestead.

Spousal Rights: Florida's Elective Share

Florida law gives a surviving spouse rights that exist independent of what a will says. Under F.S. § 732.2065, a surviving spouse may be entitled to an elective share of the deceased spouse's estate — a protection designed to prevent a spouse from being completely disinherited. The specific rules for how this share is calculated, and which assets count toward it, differ from many other states' spousal protection laws.

If your prior estate plan was built around your former state's spousal election or community property rules, it's worth having a Florida attorney confirm that your plan still accomplishes what you and your spouse intend now that Florida law applies.

Who Can Serve as Your Personal Representative in Florida?

Florida has its own eligibility rules for who may serve as your personal representative (what other states often call an executor). Florida is generally more restrictive than many states about non-relatives who live out of state serving in this role, so if your will names an old friend or advisor from your previous state who isn't a Florida resident and isn't a close relative, that nomination may not be honored the way you expect.

Florida also has specific requirements for a will to be self-proving under F.S. § 732.503. If your will was not made self-proving at the time it was signed, your witnesses (or their equivalents) may need to sign oaths before a court official after your death before the will can be admitted to probate — an extra step, and sometimes a real headache, for your family if witnesses have moved, are unavailable, or are no longer living.

Powers of Attorney and Healthcare Documents Need Fresh Eyes Too

Wills aren't the only documents affected. Durable powers of attorney (governed in Florida by F.S. Chapter 709) and healthcare documents like your healthcare surrogate designation (F.S. § 765.202) and living will (F.S. § 765.301) are often drafted with state-specific language that Florida banks, hospitals, and institutions may be reluctant to honor if it doesn't match Florida's statutory form or requirements. I've seen well-meaning families delayed at a hospital or bank simply because the document, while legally valid, didn't look like what the Florida institution was used to processing.

Updating these documents to Florida-compliant versions isn't just a technicality — it can meaningfully reduce friction for whoever is helping manage your affairs when it matters most.

The Good News: Florida's Tax Climate and 2026 Probate Improvements

Relocating to Florida does bring real advantages. Florida has no state estate tax, no state inheritance tax, no state gift tax, and no state income tax — a meaningful benefit for many retirees and families moving from higher-tax states. On the federal side, for 2026 the federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 per individual with a top rate of 40% above that threshold, and the annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient, per donor. These are federal numbers that apply regardless of which state you live in, but they're worth factoring into your overall plan.

2026 Probate Update: Effective July 1, 2026, Florida raised the asset threshold for its streamlined summary administration process from $75,000 to $150,000. This change, part of a broader modernization of Florida probate law, means more Florida estates may now qualify for a faster, simpler probate process instead of full administration.

Combined with generally favorable probate procedures for smaller estates, Florida's legal environment can work in your family's favor — but only if your documents are actually built around Florida law.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to redo my will just because I moved to Florida?
Not necessarily — a properly executed out-of-state will is generally still legally valid in Florida. However, it's common for it to need updates to align with Florida's homestead rules, personal representative requirements, and self-proving affidavit standards.
What happens if my will tries to leave my Florida home to someone other than my spouse?
Under Florida homestead law, if you're survived by a spouse or minor child, an attempted devise of homestead property to someone else may be disregarded by the probate court, with the home instead passing according to Florida's homestead succession rules regardless of the will's language.
Are my old power of attorney and healthcare directive still good in Florida?
They may remain technically valid, but Florida institutions are sometimes hesitant to accept documents that don't match Florida's statutory language, which can cause delays. Many Florida attorneys recommend updating these documents to Florida-compliant versions after a move.
Does Florida have a state estate tax?
No. Florida has no state estate tax, inheritance tax, or gift tax. Federal estate tax rules still apply, and for 2026 the federal exemption is $15,000,000 per individual.
What changed in Florida probate law in 2026?
Effective July 1, 2026, Florida raised the summary administration asset threshold from $75,000 to $150,000, allowing more estates to qualify for this faster, simpler form of probate instead of full estate administration.
How do I establish Florida domicile for estate planning purposes?
Common steps include updating your driver's license and voter registration to Florida, changing your primary banking and advisor relationships, spending sufficient time in Florida to satisfy residency requirements, and updating your estate planning documents to reflect Florida law.

The Truestead Takeaway

Moving to Florida doesn't automatically invalidate your existing estate plan, but Florida's homestead protections, spousal elective share rights, personal representative rules, and 2026 probate updates mean your documents may not function the way you expect once Florida law applies to them. The sensible next step is having a Florida attorney review your will, trust, powers of attorney, and healthcare directives against current Florida law — not to rebuild everything from scratch, but to confirm your plan actually works the way you intend now that you call Florida home.
