Living Trusts Nokomis: What Florida Homeowners Need to Know

Quick Answer

A living trust lets Nokomis homeowners transfer their home, boat, and other assets into a trust they still control during life, so those assets pass directly to family after death without going through Florida probate court. For most Nokomis residents with waterfront or Intracoastal-area property, a properly funded revocable living trust is one of the most effective ways to keep an estate private, efficient, and out of the courthouse.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 31, 2026

Why Living Trusts Come Up So Often in Nokomis

When people in Nokomis start searching for living trusts, it's usually because they own something they don't want tied up in court after they're gone — a home near the Intracoastal Waterway, a boat, a second property, or a family parcel that's been passed down for a generation. Nokomis is a small, tight-knit community, and I hear the same concern again and again in my practice: clients want their family to inherit smoothly, quietly, and quickly, without a judge, a public court file, or months of delay.

A revocable living trust is designed for exactly that. You create the trust, transfer (or "fund") your assets into it, and continue managing everything yourself during your lifetime — you can buy, sell, refinance, or spend as you always have. When you pass away, your named successor trustee distributes the trust assets directly to your beneficiaries according to your instructions, generally without opening a probate case at all.

How a Florida Revocable Living Trust Actually Works

Florida's trust law is found in the Florida Trust Code, Chapters 736 through 739 of the Florida Statutes. One important Florida-specific rule: under F.S. § 736.0602, a trust is presumed revocable unless the trust document specifically says it's irrevocable. That's the opposite of the default rule in many other states, and it matters because it means Florida law favors flexibility — you can amend or revoke your trust at any time while you're competent, adjusting beneficiaries, trustees, or terms as your life changes.

  • You keep control. As grantor and typically as your own initial trustee, you manage the trust assets exactly as before.
  • You name a successor trustee. This person steps in if you become incapacitated or after you pass away, without court appointment.
  • The trust must be funded. A trust only controls assets that have actually been retitled into it — your home, bank accounts, investment accounts, and other property need to be transferred by deed or account change. An unfunded trust accomplishes very little.

Because the trust operates during incapacity too, it also functions as a practical alternative to guardianship if you're ever unable to manage your own affairs, working alongside a durable power of attorney under Chapter 709 and a healthcare surrogate designation under F.S. § 765.202.

Your Nokomis Home, Homestead Protection, and the Trust

Many Nokomis clients worry that putting their homestead into a trust will cost them their homestead tax exemption or trigger reassessment under the Save Our Homes cap. In general, that concern is unfounded when the trust is properly drafted. Under F.S. § 196.041, a home held in a revocable trust is treated as your own equitable ownership for homestead purposes, so long as the trust reserves your beneficial use of the property for life. Your homestead exemption and Save Our Homes assessment limitation under F.S. § 193.155 generally continue undisturbed.

Florida's constitutional homestead protections — found in Art. X, § 4 and Art. VII, § 6 of the Florida Constitution — and the homestead devise restrictions in F.S. § 732.4015 also remain relevant considerations when planning around a Nokomis homestead, since Florida law limits how a homestead can be devised if you're survived by a spouse or minor child, whether the property passes by will or by trust.

Waterfront and Family Property: Homes along the Intracoastal or in longtime Nokomis family hands often carry sentimental and financial weight beyond their tax basis. A living trust lets you specify exactly how such property is shared, sold, or kept in the family — details a generic will often can't address as precisely.

Living Trust vs. Will: Probate in Florida

A will is still a foundational document — and I recommend every Nokomis client have one even alongside a trust — but a will alone must go through Florida probate court before assets reach your beneficiaries. Florida has streamlined probate somewhat: an estate can often qualify for summary administration if the total estate (excluding property exempt from creditors) is valued at $75,000 or less, or if two years have passed since the death. Larger estates typically require full, formal administration, which takes longer and becomes part of the public record.

Assets properly titled in a living trust generally bypass this process entirely. Your successor trustee can begin distributing trust property to beneficiaries relatively quickly after death, often without hiring probate counsel, and without the matter becoming a public court filing. For Nokomis families juggling out-of-state heirs, blended families, or a desire for privacy about who inherits what, this difference is often the deciding factor in choosing a trust-based plan.

Trustee Duties and Beneficiary Rights Under Florida Law

The Florida Trust Code doesn't just create trusts — it regulates how they're run. Trustees owe beneficiaries duties of loyalty, prudent administration, and reasonable communication, and beneficiaries have statutory rights to information, periodic accountings, and, if necessary, court intervention if a trustee mismanages assets. This matters for Nokomis families where a successor trustee might be a sibling, adult child, or trusted friend managing shared property or accounts for multiple beneficiaries.

⚠ Choose Your Trustee Carefully Disputes among Florida families most often arise when a trust is poorly drafted, outdated, or when a trustee misunderstands their legal duties — especially with jointly inherited waterfront or investment property. Naming a trustee who understands their responsibilities, and updating your trust after major life changes, meaningfully reduces future conflict.

Estate Tax: Usually a Non-Issue for Floridians

Florida has no state estate tax and no inheritance tax. On the federal side, the estate tax exemption is set at $15 million per person for deaths in 2026, meaning the overwhelming majority of Nokomis families never come close to owing federal estate tax. For most clients, the value of a living trust isn't tax avoidance — it's probate avoidance, privacy, incapacity planning, and control over how and when beneficiaries receive their inheritance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a living trust if I already have a will?
A will alone generally requires probate, while a properly funded revocable living trust can bypass probate for the assets it holds. Many Nokomis clients use both: a trust for major assets and a simple will to catch anything left outside it.
Will putting my Nokomis home in a trust affect my homestead exemption?
Generally no. Under F.S. § 196.041, a home held in a properly drafted revocable trust that reserves your beneficial interest for life is still treated as your homestead for exemption and Save Our Homes purposes.
Can I change or cancel my Florida living trust later?
Yes. Under F.S. § 736.0602, Florida trusts are revocable by default unless the document says otherwise, so you can amend or revoke it at any time while you're competent to do so.
What happens to my trust assets if I become incapacitated?
Your named successor trustee can step in to manage trust assets without a court-appointed guardianship, which is one of the main practical benefits of a funded living trust.
Does Florida have an estate tax I need to plan around?
No. Florida has no state estate or inheritance tax, and the federal estate tax exemption ($15 million per person in 2026) means most Florida families are not affected by federal estate tax either.
Is a living trust only useful for wealthy families?
No. Trusts are commonly used by ordinary homeowners primarily for probate avoidance, privacy, and incapacity planning, not just tax reduction, making them useful for a wide range of Nokomis families.

The Truestead Takeaway

For many Nokomis homeowners, a properly drafted and fully funded revocable living trust is one of the simplest ways to keep a home, boat, or family property out of probate court and in the hands of the people you choose, on your timeline, and privately. The details that matter most — how your homestead is titled, who serves as trustee, and whether the trust is actually funded with your assets — are specific to your situation, so it's worth having a Florida estate planning attorney review your current documents, or help you build a plan if you don't have one yet.
