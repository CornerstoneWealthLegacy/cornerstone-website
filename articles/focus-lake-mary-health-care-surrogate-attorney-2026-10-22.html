Lake Mary Health Care Surrogate Attorney: What Florida Law Requires

Quick Answer

A Lake Mary health care surrogate attorney helps you prepare a legally valid Designation of Health Care Surrogate under Florida Statutes Chapter 765, so someone you trust can make medical decisions for you if you're ever unable to make them yourself. The document must be in writing, signed by you, and witnessed by two adults, but it doesn't need to be notarized or filed anywhere.

What does a Lake Mary health care surrogate attorney actually do?

In my practice, when someone searches for a Lake Mary health care surrogate attorney, they're usually not looking for a specialized court role or a Seminole County-specific form. They're looking for local, trustworthy help putting together a document that Florida law treats the same way whether you sign it in Lake Mary, Sanford, or Miami: the Designation of Health Care Surrogate, governed by Florida Statutes Chapter 765.

What a Lake Mary attorney adds isn't a different set of rules, it's local accessibility, careful drafting, and making sure the document actually reflects what you want rather than a generic fill-in-the-blank form. What is a health care surrogate under Florida law?

Under Florida Statutes § 765.202, a health care surrogate designation is a written document naming a competent adult to make health care decisions for you, receive your medical information, or both. The person you name is called your surrogate, and you are the principal.

  • The document must be in writing.
  • You (the principal) must sign it.
  • Two adult witnesses must sign in your presence.

Notably, no notary is required, and the document is not filed with any court or state registry. That simplicity is intentional: Florida wants this tool to be realistically accessible to everyone, not locked behind extra formality.

Who can (and can't) witness a Florida health care surrogate designation?

The witness rules matter, and this is one of the places I see homemade forms go wrong. Florida law requires two adult witnesses, with these restrictions:

  • The person you're naming as your surrogate cannot also serve as a witness.
  • At least one witness must be someone who is neither your spouse nor a blood relative.

If these rules aren't followed correctly, the document's validity can be called into question at exactly the moment your family needs it to work without a fight. This is one reason I encourage clients to have the document prepared and witnessed properly the first time, rather than relying on a form downloaded from an unfamiliar source.

When does my surrogate's authority actually start?

This is one of the most important choices in the document, and it's governed by Florida Statutes § 765.204. You have two basic options:

  • Springing authority: your surrogate's power only becomes active once you're determined to be incapacitated (generally by your attending physician, sometimes with a second physician's confirmation depending on the circumstances).
  • Immediate authority: you can expressly state in the document that your surrogate may act and receive your health information right away, without waiting for an incapacity determination.

Neither option is automatically "better." Some clients want the extra layer of protection that comes with requiring a capacity determination first. Others, particularly those who want a spouse or adult child to be able to talk to doctors and coordinate care without any bureaucratic delay, prefer immediate authority. I walk through both approaches with clients so the choice is intentional, not accidental.

Good to know: Your health care surrogate's authority generally covers medical treatment decisions, surgeries, medications, hospital placement, end-of-life care decisions, and access to your medical records so they can actually advocate for you.

Can my surrogate make mental health treatment decisions too?

Florida law treats mental health decision-making with extra care. You may designate a separate surrogate specifically for mental health treatment decisions, which becomes relevant if you're later determined by a court to be incompetent to consent to mental health treatment and a guardian advocate is appointed.

If your document doesn't specifically address this, the law doesn't leave a gap: unless you expressly state otherwise, the court will assume that the same health care surrogate you named for general medical decisions is also your choice for mental health treatment decisions. Many clients are comfortable with that default. Others want to name a different person for this specific role, and that's a conversation worth having when the document is drafted rather than guessed at later.

How does this fit with a living will and durable power of attorney?

I rarely recommend a health care surrogate designation in isolation. In my practice, it typically works alongside two other documents:

  • A living will (governed by Florida Statutes § 765.301), which states your wishes about life-prolonging procedures in specific end-of-life circumstances, separate from who has the authority to act.
  • A durable power of attorney (governed by Florida Statutes Chapter 709), which covers financial and legal decisions rather than medical ones.

Together, these three documents answer the two questions every family eventually faces: who speaks for me, and what do I actually want. A health care surrogate designation answers the first question for medical matters; a living will helps answer the second.

⚠ A note on self-prepared forms: Generic online forms often get the witness restrictions wrong, or fail to clearly state whether the surrogate's authority is immediate or springing. Because this document is used at a moment of medical crisis, ambiguity is the worst possible outcome. Having it reviewed by a Florida attorney before you need it is far better than discovering a problem in a hospital waiting room.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a lawyer to create a health care surrogate designation in Florida?
No, Florida law does not require an attorney to prepare this document. However, because the witness requirements and effective-date language are easy to get wrong and hard to fix after the fact, many Floridians choose to have it drafted or reviewed by an attorney as part of a broader estate plan.
Does a Florida health care surrogate designation need to be notarized?
No. Florida Statutes Chapter 765 requires two adult witnesses, but does not require notarization, and the document is not filed with any court or government registry.
Who should I choose as my health care surrogate?
Most people choose a spouse, adult child, or another trusted family member or friend who understands their values and is willing to act decisively. The person must be a competent adult, and they cannot serve as one of the required witnesses to the document.
What's the difference between a health care surrogate and a health care power of attorney?
In Florida, these terms are generally used interchangeably to describe the same Chapter 765 document. It is distinct from a durable power of attorney under Chapter 709, which covers financial and legal matters rather than medical decisions.
Can I name a different person to handle mental health treatment decisions?
Yes. Florida law allows you to designate a separate surrogate specifically for mental health treatment decisions. If you don't name someone separately, your general health care surrogate is presumed to hold that authority as well.
Can I change or revoke my health care surrogate designation later?
Yes, as long as you remain competent, you can revoke or update your designation at any time. It's a good practice to review it periodically, especially after major life changes like a divorce, death of the named surrogate, or a move.

The Truestead Takeaway

A health care surrogate designation is a short, inexpensive document that solves a problem families often don't think about until it's urgent: who is legally authorized to talk to your doctors and make decisions if you can't. The statutory rules under Florida Statutes Chapter 765 are not complicated, but the witness requirements and the choice between immediate and springing authority are details worth getting right the first time. If you're in the Lake Mary area and don't yet have this document, or haven't looked at it in years, it's worth a conversation with a Florida attorney to make sure it still reflects your wishes and fits with the rest of your estate plan.
