Florida Estate Planning Foundations: What Every Family Needs to Know

Quick Answer

A complete Florida estate plan typically includes a will, a revocable trust, a durable power of attorney, and a healthcare surrogate designation — four documents that together protect you during life and guide your family after you're gone.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney July 6, 2026

Why 'I'll Get to It Later' Is the Most Expensive Estate-Planning Strategy

In my practice, the clients who face the most stress — and the most unnecessary legal fees — are rarely the ones who made the wrong estate-planning choices. They're the ones who made no choices at all. When a Florida resident dies without a will, or becomes incapacitated without signed legal documents in place, the state's default rules take over. Those rules are impersonal, slow, and often not what the person would have wanted.

The good news is that the foundational documents of a Florida estate plan are straightforward to understand, and getting them right doesn't have to be complicated. Here's what I'd want my own family to know.

The Florida Will: Your First Building Block

A Last Will and Testament is the cornerstone of most estate plans. Under F.S. § 732.502, a Florida will must be in writing, signed by the testator (the person making the will) in the presence of two witnesses, who must also sign in the testator's presence. Florida does not recognize handwritten (holographic) wills unless they were validly executed in another state that allows them.

Your will accomplishes several critical things:

  • Names the people or charities who inherit your assets (beneficiaries).
  • Appoints a personal representative — Florida's term for the executor — to manage your estate through probate.
  • Designates a guardian for minor children, one of the most important decisions any parent will ever make.
  • Establishes testamentary trusts for young beneficiaries, if needed.

One thing a will cannot do: bypass Florida probate. Any asset passing under your will must go through the court-supervised probate process, which takes time and is a public record. That's exactly why many Florida families pair a will with a revocable trust.

Florida Tip: Even if you have a revocable trust, you still need a will — specifically a "pour-over will" that catches any assets you forgot to transfer into your trust and funnels them to it at death.

The Revocable Living Trust: Privacy, Speed, and Control

A revocable living trust — governed by F.S. Chapter 736 — is a legal arrangement you create during your lifetime. You transfer ownership of your assets into the trust, typically serving as your own trustee while you're alive and well. You can change, amend, or revoke it at any time before death or incapacity.

Why do so many Florida families use one? Three reasons stand out:

  • Probate avoidance: Assets held in trust pass directly to your beneficiaries without going through probate — saving months and court costs.
  • Privacy: Unlike a will, a trust is not filed with the court and does not become a public record.
  • Seamless incapacity planning: If you become unable to manage your affairs, your named successor trustee steps in immediately — no court petition required.

A trust is especially valuable in Florida because of the state's substantial snowbird and second-home population. If you own real property in multiple states, a trust holding that property can eliminate the need for ancillary (out-of-state) probate proceedings — a significant saving in both time and expense.

Durable Power of Attorney: Who Manages Your Finances If You Can't?

A durable power of attorney (DPOA) authorizes a trusted person — your agent — to handle financial and legal matters on your behalf. In Florida, the document is governed by F.S. Chapter 709. The word "durable" is critical: it means the document remains effective even if you later become incapacitated. A non-durable power of attorney terminates the moment you lose capacity — precisely when you need it most.

Florida law has specific execution requirements for a DPOA to be valid: it must be signed by the principal and two witnesses, and acknowledged before a notary public. Florida also requires that a DPOA be executed with particular language to authorize certain "superpowers" — such as making gifts, changing beneficiary designations, or creating trusts — so generic, out-of-state, or internet-downloaded forms can fall dangerously short.

⚠ Important: Many Florida banks and financial institutions will scrutinize a power of attorney closely. A document that doesn't meet Florida's statutory requirements may be rejected, leaving your family scrambling for a court-ordered guardianship — a costly and time-consuming process.

Healthcare Surrogate and Living Will: Your Voice in a Medical Crisis

Two documents govern your healthcare wishes in Florida, and they serve different purposes.

A Designation of Healthcare Surrogate, authorized under F.S. § 765.202, names the person you trust to make medical decisions on your behalf if you are unable to make or communicate them yourself. This person speaks for you in real time — working with your doctors, consenting to or refusing treatment, and making judgment calls your written documents may not anticipate.

A Living Will, governed by F.S. § 765.301 and following sections, is your written declaration of the life-prolonging procedures you do or do not want when you have a terminal condition, end-stage condition, or are in a persistent vegetative state. It speaks when you cannot.

Together, these two documents are often called your advance directives. Without them, your family may disagree about your care, or a hospital may default to maximum intervention — regardless of what you would have wanted. These are arguably the most personal documents in any estate plan, and I encourage every adult Floridian, at any age, to have them.

How Florida's Homestead Rules Affect Your Estate Plan

No Florida estate-planning conversation is complete without touching on homestead. Florida's homestead laws are among the strongest in the country, and they interact with estate planning in ways that surprise many families.

Under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, your primary residence is protected from most creditor claims — a powerful shield. But that same homestead status comes with devise restrictions under F.S. § 732.4015: if you are married or have minor children, Florida limits who you can leave your homestead to in your will or trust. You generally cannot disinherit a surviving spouse or minor child from your homestead, regardless of what your documents say.

On the tax side, the homestead exemption under Article VII, Section 6 of the Florida Constitution and F.S. § 196.031 can reduce your assessed value for property tax purposes, and the Save Our Homes cap under F.S. § 193.155 limits annual assessment increases. These benefits are not automatically portable to a trust — proper title and deed work matters. How you hold title to your home has real consequences for both your taxes and your estate plan, which is why deed transfers should always be reviewed by a Florida attorney before signing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need both a will and a revocable trust in Florida?
Many Florida families benefit from having both. The trust avoids probate and provides incapacity planning for assets transferred into it, while a pour-over will captures any assets left outside the trust and names a guardian for minor children — something a trust cannot do.
What happens if I die without a will in Florida?
Florida's intestate succession laws — found in F.S. Chapter 732 — determine who inherits, generally following a priority of spouse, then descendants. The result may not match your wishes, and the process still goes through probate court.
Can I use an online will or power of attorney form for Florida?
Florida has strict execution and content requirements for these documents, and generic online forms frequently fail to meet them. A power of attorney that doesn't comply with Florida's statutory requirements may be rejected by banks and institutions when you need it most.
Does a revocable trust protect my assets from creditors?
No. Because you retain control of a revocable trust during your lifetime, its assets are generally still reachable by your creditors. The trust's primary benefits are probate avoidance, privacy, and smooth incapacity management — not asset protection.
How often should I update my Florida estate plan?
A good rule of thumb is to review your plan every three to five years, or after any major life event — marriage, divorce, the birth of a child or grandchild, a significant change in assets, or a move to or from Florida. Florida law and your personal circumstances both evolve.
Does putting my home in a revocable trust affect my homestead exemption?
It can, if done incorrectly. Florida law does allow a properly structured revocable trust to hold homestead property without losing the exemption, but the trust must meet specific requirements. This is one area where working with a Florida estate-planning attorney — not a DIY approach — is especially important.

The Truestead Takeaway

A Florida estate plan built on these four foundations — a will, a revocable trust, a durable power of attorney, and advance healthcare directives — gives you control over what happens to your assets, your healthcare, and your family, both during your lifetime and after. Florida's homestead rules, probate process, and statutory requirements for document execution add layers of complexity that make professional guidance genuinely worthwhile here. If you haven't reviewed your plan recently, or if you don't yet have one, the sensible next step is a conversation with a Florida estate-planning attorney who can look at your actual situation — not a generic checklist.
