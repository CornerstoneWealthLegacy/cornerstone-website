Forms for 18 Year Olds to Sign in Florida

Quick Answer

In Florida, a newly-turned 18-year-old should generally sign four documents: a Durable Power of Attorney, a Designation of Health Care Surrogate, a Living Will, and a HIPAA authorization — and, ideally, a simple will. None of these are required by law, but without them, parents lose the automatic legal authority they had while their child was a minor.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 11, 2026

What forms should an 18 year old actually sign?

I get this question every August from parents whose kids just graduated or are heading off to college. The moment a child turns 18 in Florida, something changes legally that most families never think about: the parent's automatic right to see medical records, talk to a doctor, or handle the young adult's financial affairs simply disappears. Your son or daughter is now a legal adult, full stop — and Florida law treats them exactly like any other adult, with no special exception for family.

The good news is that fixing this takes a short stack of paperwork, not a lawsuit or a guardianship. The forms I recommend most often for 18-year-olds are:

  • Durable Power of Attorney (DPOA) — names someone (usually a parent) to handle financial and legal matters
  • Designation of Health Care Surrogate — names someone to make medical decisions if the young adult can't communicate
  • Living Will — states end-of-life care wishes
  • HIPAA Authorization — allows doctors and hospitals to share medical information with named people
  • A simple Last Will and Testament — not urgent for most 18-year-olds, but appropriate once they have any assets, a car titled in their name, or strong wishes about who should handle things if something happened to them

None of this is required by Florida law. An 18-year-old is under no legal obligation to sign anything. But without these documents, if that young adult is ever in an accident or gets seriously ill — even temporarily — parents can find themselves locked out of the hospital's information, unable to pay a bill from a frozen account, or forced into an emergency guardianship proceeding through the courts.

Why can't parents just handle things once their child turns 18?

Under Florida law, parental authority over a child's medical and financial affairs ends automatically at 18 — there's no grace period and no exception for a child still living at home, still on the family insurance, or still in college. At that point the young adult is a legal stranger to hospitals, banks, and schools for privacy purposes, unless they've signed something authorizing family involvement.

This catches families off guard most often in a hospital setting. Federal privacy law and Florida practice mean a hospital generally cannot discuss an 18-year-old patient's condition with a parent unless the patient has authorized it — even if the parent is standing in the waiting room and paying the insurance premium. A signed HIPAA authorization and health care surrogate designation solve this cleanly, in advance, before there's ever a crisis.

What does the Durable Power of Attorney actually do?

A Durable Power of Attorney lets your 18-year-old name someone — typically a parent — as their agent to handle financial and legal matters on their behalf: banking, paying bills, dealing with a landlord, signing for a car title, or managing a financial aid or tax issue while they're away at school. "Durable" simply means the authority keeps working even if the young adult later becomes incapacitated, which is the whole point of having it in place before it's needed.

Florida's power of attorney statute, F.S. Chapter 709, requires specific language and proper witnessing and notarization for a DPOA to be valid and accepted by Florida banks and institutions. This is one area where I'd caution against a generic online form.

⚠ Watch out for out-of-state or internet templates. Florida has its own execution requirements for powers of attorney under F.S. Chapter 709. A form written for another state, or a bare-bones internet download, may be rejected outright by a Florida bank, hospital, or title company right when your family needs it to work.

Do I need a healthcare surrogate form, a living will, or both?

These are related but different documents, and I recommend both for an 18-year-old:

  • A Designation of Health Care Surrogate (Florida's version of a medical power of attorney, governed by F.S. § 765.202) names a person to make medical treatment decisions if your young adult is unable to communicate their own wishes — after surgery, following an accident, or during a serious illness.
  • A Living Will (F.S. § 765.301) states, in the person's own words, what kind of end-of-life care they do or don't want if they're ever in a terminal, persistent vegetative, or end-stage condition. It's not a pleasant thing to think about for an 18-year-old, but it removes the guesswork for family in the rare, worst-case scenario.

Pair both with a HIPAA authorization so the named surrogate — and any other people your family wants informed, like both parents — can actually get information from doctors and hospitals in the first place.

Does an 18-year-old need a will too?

Under Florida law, any person 18 years of age or older who is of sound mind may make a valid will (an emancipated minor may also do so, even if under 18). Florida's requirements are straightforward: the will must be in writing, signed by the person at the end, and signed by at least two witnesses in the presence of the person and each other. Florida does not recognize oral wills, and does not recognize an unwitnessed handwritten will, regardless of the circumstances.

For most 18-year-olds, a will isn't urgent the way the health care and financial forms are — an 18-year-old rarely has a large estate, and Florida's intestacy law already provides a default plan (typically to parents) if a young, unmarried person without children dies without a will. Still, if your 18-year-old owns a car, has savings, or simply wants to name who should handle their affairs and personal belongings, there's no reason to wait. Florida law doesn't require a special form or particular wording — just proper execution with the right witnesses.

Good to know: There's no statutory, state-issued form for a will, a power of attorney, or a health care surrogate designation in Florida. What matters legally is that the document meets the execution requirements — the right signatures, the right witnesses, and where required, notarization — not that it comes from a particular website or template.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are these forms legally required for every 18-year-old in Florida?
No. Florida law doesn't require anyone to sign a power of attorney, health care surrogate designation, living will, or will. These documents are strongly recommended so that trusted family members retain legal authority to help — they are not mandatory.
Can I use a form I found online for my child's power of attorney?
Be cautious. Florida has specific execution requirements under F.S. Chapter 709, and a generic or out-of-state template may not meet them — meaning a bank or hospital could refuse to honor it right when it's needed. Having it reviewed by a Florida attorney helps ensure it will actually work.
Does turning 18 automatically cut parents out of medical decisions?
Yes, in the sense that hospitals generally cannot share medical information with or take direction from a parent of an adult patient unless the patient has authorized it. A signed HIPAA release and health care surrogate designation restore that access on the young adult's own terms.
What happens if my 18-year-old is in an accident and hasn't signed anything?
Parents may find themselves unable to get medical updates, pay bills from the young adult's accounts, or make decisions on their behalf — sometimes requiring an emergency guardianship proceeding through the court, which takes time and money that advance planning avoids.
Does an 18-year-old need a will if they don't own much?
Not urgently in most cases — Florida's intestacy laws provide a default distribution plan if someone dies without a will. But if your 18-year-old owns a car, has savings, or has specific wishes, a properly executed will (signed with two witnesses per F.S. Chapter 732) is inexpensive and straightforward to put in place.
Is a Living Will the same thing as a Last Will and Testament?
No. A Living Will (F.S. § 765.301) only addresses end-of-life medical care wishes. A Last Will and Testament (F.S. Chapter 732) addresses who inherits property and who administers the estate after death. Most people benefit from having both, but they serve entirely different purposes.

The Truestead Takeaway

The forms an 18-year-old should sign in Florida aren't complicated, but they only work if they're done right — with Florida's specific witnessing, notarization, and statutory language requirements met. In my practice, I encourage every family with a newly-adult child to put a Durable Power of Attorney, Health Care Surrogate designation, Living Will, and HIPAA authorization in place before there's ever a reason to need them, and to consider a simple will once the young adult has any assets or clear wishes of their own. If you're a Florida parent trying to get this right for a child who just turned 18, it's worth having a Florida attorney review or prepare the actual documents rather than relying on a generic download.
