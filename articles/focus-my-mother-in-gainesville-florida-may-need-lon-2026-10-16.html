My Mother in Gainesville, Florida May Need Long Term Care Within a Year: What Trust Planning and Lady Bird Deeds Can (and Can't) Do

Quick Answer

If your mother may need nursing home level care within a year, a new irrevocable Medicaid trust won't help her qualify in time, because transfers within five years of applying trigger a penalty period under Florida's look-back rules. But a Lady Bird deed can still protect her Gainesville home from Medicaid estate recovery after she passes, without affecting her eligibility now, and other tools like a Qualified Income Trust or spend-down planning may help this year. This situation calls for a Florida elder law attorney, soon.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Elder Law Attorney October 16, 2026

The honest answer: timing changes everything here

I hear versions of this question often, usually from an adult child who just watched a parent's health change quickly. My mother in Gainesville, Florida may need long term care within a year. What Florida trust planning options could help with Medicaid eligibility while protecting her home and savings, and how do look-back rules and Lady Bird deeds work here? Let me give you the direct answer first, then the reasoning.

If your mother might need nursing home level care within the next twelve months, an irrevocable Medicaid Asset Protection Trust (MAPT) set up today will not help her qualify for Medicaid during that window. Florida's Medicaid program looks back 60 months (five years) from the date of application and reviews transfers for less than fair value, including assets moved into most irrevocable trusts. A transfer inside that window creates a penalty period, not protection. That said, a Lady Bird deed on her homestead is a different animal entirely. It generally does not count as a disqualifying transfer, it does not trigger a look-back penalty, and it can still protect her house from Florida's Medicaid estate recovery program after she passes. The two tools answer two different problems, and in a one-year window, the deed is usually the more realistic fix for the home, while eligibility for her care itself often turns on other strategies.

Florida's five-year look-back, explained in plain terms

When someone applies for long-term care Medicaid in Florida, the Department of Children and Families reviews the 60 months before the application date for any gifts or below-market transfers of assets. This is required under Florida Administrative Code 65A-1.712. If your mother gave away money, deeded property for no payment, or funded certain trusts during that period, DCF can impose a penalty period, a span of months during which Medicaid won't pay for her nursing home care, even though she otherwise qualifies.

  • The penalty is calculated by dividing the value transferred by Florida's average private-pay nursing home cost, a figure the state updates annually (as of 2026, that divisor is $10,645 per month).
  • The penalty period doesn't start until she is otherwise eligible and has applied, which can make the timing worse, not better.
  • Transfers to a spouse, and certain transfers involving a disabled child or caregiver child who meets specific residency requirements, can be treated differently. These exceptions are fact-specific and should be reviewed with an attorney rather than assumed.

This is why I tell families: if a parent may need care within a year, the five-year clock is simply not going to run out in time for a brand-new irrevocable trust to help with eligibility for that care.

Could an irrevocable Medicaid trust still make sense?

Sometimes, yes, but not for the reason most people assume. A Medicaid Asset Protection Trust can be a smart tool when someone is still healthy and planning five or more years ahead, because once the five-year window has passed, assets properly titled in the trust are excluded from Medicaid's resource calculation entirely, with no penalty. The trust is irrevocable, meaning your mother would give up direct control and the ability to revoke it, which is precisely what allows those assets to eventually sit outside Medicaid's reach.

If your mother is already inside that one-year window, funding a new MAPT now generally will not help her qualify for care soon. It may still be worth discussing with an elder law attorney as part of a longer-range family plan, for example if there is a reasonable chance she won't need facility-level care for a while, or to protect assets for a healthy spouse or for later generations. But it should not be sold to you as a quick fix.

⚠ Don't transfer assets under pressure Rushing to retitle a home or move savings into an irrevocable trust in the months before an expected Medicaid application is one of the most common and costly mistakes families make. It can trigger a penalty period precisely when your mother needs coverage to begin. Talk to a Florida elder law attorney before moving anything.

How a Lady Bird deed works for a Gainesville home

A Lady Bird deed, formally an enhanced life estate deed, is well recognized under Florida common law and widely used by Florida elder law attorneys. Your mother would sign a deed that names a remainder beneficiary (often an adult child) but she keeps full control during her lifetime: she can live in the home, sell it, mortgage it, or change her mind and name someone else entirely, all without anyone else's consent.

  • Because she retains that full control, Florida treats the deed as an incomplete gift during her life, so it is generally not counted as a disqualifying transfer under Medicaid's look-back rules.
  • Because the property passes directly to the named beneficiary at her death by operation of the deed, it avoids probate.
  • Because it is not a probate asset, it generally falls outside the reach of Florida's Medicaid estate recovery program, which under current law recovers only from the deceased recipient's probate estate.

For a Gainesville homestead, this combination is often exactly what families are looking for: the home stays protected for Medicaid eligibility purposes while she's alive, and it's shielded from estate recovery after she passes, all without the five-year waiting period that trusts require.

Homestead still matters separately Florida's homestead protections under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution and the Save Our Homes assessment limits under F.S. 193.155 are separate from Medicaid planning. A Lady Bird deed is generally structured to preserve these benefits, but it should be reviewed alongside her overall estate plan, not treated in isolation.

What can actually help within the next year

If eligibility within twelve months is the real goal, the planning conversation usually centers on tools that don't depend on a five-year runway:

  • Spend-down and allowable asset conversions. Florida Medicaid allows certain assets to be exempt or converted, such as prepaying funeral expenses through an irrevocable funeral contract, paying off debt, or making home modifications, reducing countable assets toward the $2,000 limit without penalty.
  • Qualified Income Trust (Miller Trust). If her monthly income exceeds Florida's income cap, a Qualified Income Trust can bring her into compliance for eligibility purposes, this is a well-established and routine tool, not a look-back issue.
  • Community Spouse Resource Allowance. If her husband (your father, if living) remains at home, Florida law allows the healthy spouse to retain a protected share of countable assets, separate from what your mother needs to spend down.
  • The homestead exemption itself. A primary residence is generally an exempt asset for Medicaid eligibility purposes up to Florida's equity limit, so the home may not need to be sold or transferred at all just to qualify her for care.

Every one of these depends on her specific numbers, marital status, and health timeline, which is exactly the kind of fact-specific review that shouldn't be guessed at from a general article.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can we still protect my mother's savings if she needs care within a year?
Possibly, but not usually through a new irrevocable trust. Strategies like spend-down of countable assets, a Qualified Income Trust, or the Community Spouse Resource Allowance (if a spouse is living) are not subject to the five-year look-back and can often be used right away. An elder law attorney should review her specific assets and timeline.
Does a Lady Bird deed help my mother qualify for Medicaid now?
No. A Lady Bird deed doesn't change whether she qualifies for Medicaid, since her homestead is typically already an exempt asset for eligibility purposes. Its real value is avoiding probate and protecting the home from Medicaid's estate recovery claim after she passes.
What happens if she transferred money or property to family in the last few years?
Any transfer for less than fair value within the 60 months before her Medicaid application can trigger a penalty period that delays coverage. The exact impact depends on the amount, timing, and recipient, and should be reviewed with an attorney before she applies.
Is it too late to do any planning if she may need care within a year?
It's not too late to plan, it just changes which tools apply. New irrevocable trusts generally won't help with eligibility in that timeframe, but Lady Bird deeds, income trusts, exempt asset planning, and spousal protections can often still be used effectively.
Will Florida take her house after she passes if she's on Medicaid?
Florida's Medicaid estate recovery program generally only reaches assets in the deceased recipient's probate estate. A properly executed Lady Bird deed keeps the home out of probate, which under current Florida law generally keeps it outside the reach of estate recovery.
Does this advice apply the same way outside Gainesville?
Florida Medicaid rules, look-back rules, and Lady Bird deed law are statewide, so the framework applies the same whether your mother lives in Gainesville, Tampa, or Miami. Local nursing home costs and available facilities may differ, but the legal rules do not.

The Truestead Takeaway

When a parent's need for long-term care may be close at hand, the planning conversation should shift from long-range trust strategies to what can realistically help within the time you have, which usually means looking hard at exempt assets, income trusts, spousal protections, and a Lady Bird deed for the home rather than a brand-new irrevocable trust. Every family's numbers and timeline are different, and Florida's Medicaid rules reward careful, early review far more than quick transfers made under pressure. The sensible next step is a sit-down with a Florida elder law attorney who can look at your mother's actual assets, income, and health trajectory and tell you, specifically, what will and won't work in her case.
