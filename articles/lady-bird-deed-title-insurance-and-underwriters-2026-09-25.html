Why a Title Company Balked at a Lady Bird Deed, and How the Closing Got Fixed

Quick Answer

Title companies sometimes hesitate on lady bird deeds not because the deed is invalid, but because the file lacks the follow-up paperwork proving the owner has died and the beneficiary now holds clear title. A recorded death certificate, a properly worded original deed, and often a short affidavit usually resolve it.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney September 25, 2026

Nick's situation: a house, a deed, and a stalled closing

Nick is a composite example I'm using to walk through a real and common scenario, not an actual client. He's 66, lives in St. Augustine, and inherited his late mother's house through a lady bird deed she signed years earlier. As regular readers of this series already know, a lady bird deed lets the owner keep full control of the property during life, including the right to sell, mortgage, or revoke it, and it passes the home to the named beneficiary at death without probate.

Nick found a buyer, opened escrow, and expected a routine closing. Instead, the title company's underwriter flagged the file. The buyer's lender wanted answers before it would fund the loan. Nothing was wrong with Nick's ownership. The problem was that the file didn't yet contain the proof a title company needs to insure it.

Why underwriters hesitate on lady bird deeds

There is no Florida statute that creates the lady bird deed by name. It works because Florida property law lets an owner convey real estate in whatever lawful form they choose, and because deeds of this kind satisfy the state's general requirements for executing and recording real property transfers. Florida's title standards (persuasive guidance that most underwriters follow, though not binding law) confirm that the holder of this kind of enhanced life estate can sell or mortgage the property alone during life, and that the named beneficiary takes full ownership at the owner's death.

Even so, every title company sets its own underwriting policy, and a lady bird deed asks an underwriter to trust two things: that the deed's wording actually reserved the owner's full powers, and that the chain of title after death was properly completed. When either piece looks thin, the underwriter asks questions before issuing a policy.

In Nick's case, the underwriter wanted three things: a certified copy of his mother's death certificate recorded in St. Johns County, confirmation of whether the home was her homestead and whether that affected the transfer, and a close read of the deed's original language to confirm it reserved the full bundle of rights a lady bird deed requires.

The wording problems that cause the most trouble

Many of the title hiccups I see trace back to deed language, not to any defect in the underlying idea. Common problems include:

  • Missing reserved powers. If the deed doesn't clearly state that the owner retains the right to sell, convey, mortgage, lease, or revoke the interest without the beneficiary's consent, an underwriter may read it as an ordinary life estate instead. That changes everything, because an ordinary life estate requires the remainder beneficiary to sign off on any later sale.
  • Vague remainder language. If it's unclear who the beneficiaries are, or the deed doesn't clearly state that their interest is contingent and doesn't vest until death, a title examiner has to guess.
  • No homestead or marital status disclosure. A homestead owned by a married person cannot be conveyed or devised without the spouse joining. If the original deed doesn't address marital status, or the file doesn't show whether the property was homestead at signing and at death, that's a gap an underwriter will want closed.
  • Execution defects. Florida requires two witnesses and a notary on every deed, properly recorded in the county where the land sits. A deed missing a witness signature or notary acknowledgment is a real defect, not a formality.

These are exactly the kinds of problems that generic online deed templates tend to create. A form pulled from a general website may not include Florida's specific reserved-powers language at all.

What actually cured Nick's closing

Fortunately, Nick's mother's lady bird deed had been prepared by an attorney, so the underlying language was sound. The fix was procedural, not a rewrite of the deed. Nick's closing team:

  • Recorded a certified copy of his mother's death certificate in the official records of St. Johns County, where the home is located, completing the public record trail showing title passed to Nick automatically at her death.
  • Provided a short affidavit, sometimes called an affidavit of no probate or a homestead affidavit, confirming that no probate was necessary for this asset, that Nick was the sole surviving beneficiary named in the deed, and addressing the home's homestead status at the time of his mother's passing.
  • Pulled the original recorded deed and confirmed, on its face, that it reserved the full powers to sell, mortgage, and revoke, which told the underwriter this was a true enhanced life estate deed and not a limited life estate requiring anyone else's signature.

Once that documentation was in the file, the underwriter cleared the title, the lender funded, and Nick closed only a short time later than planned.

Good to know: Title vests in the named beneficiary automatically at the owner's death, by operation of law. Recording the death certificate and any supporting affidavit doesn't create that ownership. It simply proves it to a buyer, lender, or title company reviewing the public record.

Why attorney-prepared deeds tend to close cleaner

Nick's experience shows the value of a deed drafted with Florida's title standards in mind from the start. When the reserved powers, remainder language, and homestead and marital disclosures are handled properly at signing, the only work left after death is assembling the death certificate and a short affidavit. When the original deed is vague or copied from a generic template, a title company may instead require a quiet title action, a costly and slow court process, before it will insure a sale.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did Nick's lady bird deed fail?
No. The deed itself was valid and properly worded. The delay happened because the file needed additional documents, a recorded death certificate and a short affidavit, to prove to the title company that title had passed to Nick.
What is an affidavit of no probate, and is it always required?
It's a sworn statement confirming that probate wasn't necessary for the property because it passed by the lady bird deed, and often addressing homestead status and the identity of the beneficiaries. Not every title company requires it, but many do as a routine part of clearing title after the owner's death.
Does a lady bird deed avoid the need to record anything after death?
No. While title passes automatically at death with no probate required, someone still needs to record a certified death certificate (and often a supporting affidavit) in the county where the property sits, so the public record and any future title company can confirm the transfer.
Why do some title companies treat lady bird deeds differently than others?
Because there's no Florida statute prescribing the deed's exact form, each title insurance underwriter sets its own policy for how much documentation it wants before insuring title. Florida's title standards offer persuasive guidance, but they aren't binding law, so practices vary somewhat by underwriter.
Can a generic online lady bird deed template cause this problem?
Yes. If the template doesn't clearly reserve the owner's right to sell, mortgage, and revoke the property without the beneficiary's consent, an underwriter may treat it as an ordinary life estate, which requires the beneficiary to join in any future sale and can complicate or delay a closing.
What if the property was the owner's homestead?
A homestead owned by a married person cannot be conveyed or devised without the spouse joining, so the original deed and the post-death documentation both need to address marital status and homestead facts clearly. This is one of the most common items a title underwriter asks about.

The Truestead Takeaway

Nick's closing wasn't derailed by a bad deed, it was slowed by a title company doing its job: confirming, with proper documentation, that ownership had truly passed to him. A recorded death certificate, a clear affidavit, and a well-drafted original deed resolved it within days. The lesson for any Florida homeowner using a lady bird deed, or any adult child helping a parent set one up, is that the deed's wording at signing determines how smoothly a future sale will go. If you're relying on a lady bird deed already in your family, or considering one, it's worth having a Florida attorney review the language now rather than finding out its limits at the closing table.
