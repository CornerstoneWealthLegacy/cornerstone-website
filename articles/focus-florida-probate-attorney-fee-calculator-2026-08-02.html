Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator: What You'll Actually Pay

Quick Answer

Florida's statutory fee schedule (F.S. § 733.6171) is a starting point, not a fixed bill: it runs $1,500 for estates of $40,000 or less, plus set amounts up to $100,000, then 3% on the next $900,000 — but the law itself says this schedule is only 'presumed reasonable,' and many straightforward Florida probates are handled for a negotiated flat fee below that number.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 2, 2026

How a Florida Probate Attorney Fee Calculator Actually Works

In my practice, I get some version of this question almost every week: "How much is this going to cost?" A Florida probate attorney fee calculator is simply a tool that applies Florida's statutory fee schedule to your estate's value to give you a ballpark number. It's a good starting point — but it's not a quote, and it's not the law's final word on what you must pay.

The schedule lives in F.S. § 733.6171(3) and is based on the estate's compensable value — generally the inventory value of the probate assets plus any income the estate earns during administration. Here's how the tiers stack, based on the current statutory framework:

  • $1,500 for estates of $40,000 or less
  • An additional $750 for the value between $40,000 and $70,000
  • An additional $750 for the value between $70,000 and $100,000
  • 3% of the value between $100,000 and $1 million
  • Declining percentages on larger estates, down to about 1% for estates above $10 million

So a calculator plugging in a $250,000 estate would land you around $1,500 + $750 + $750 + (3% of $150,000, or $4,500) — roughly $7,500 as a presumed reasonable fee. That's the math. Whether it's the right fee for your family's situation is a separate question.

"Presumed Reasonable" Doesn't Mean Mandatory

This is the single most misunderstood part of Florida probate fees, and I want to be direct about it: the statutory schedule is presumed reasonable, not a mandatory fee. Florida law requires attorneys who intend to rely on that schedule to tell you, in writing, before you agree to anything, that:

  • There is no mandatory statutory attorney fee for estate administration, and
  • The fee is not required to be based on the size of the estate, and the statutory percentage may not be appropriate for every administration.

Florida law also expressly allows the attorney, the personal representative, and the people who bear the cost of the fee to agree to a different fee arrangement — including a flat fee — under § 733.6171(2)(a). If that alternative arrangement is properly disclosed and nobody objects under the Florida Probate Rules, it governs instead of the percentage schedule.

What I tell clients: Ask for the written fee disclosure up front, and ask whether your situation qualifies for a flat fee. In an uncontested estate with cooperative beneficiaries, many Florida firms — including mine — quote a flat fee well below what the statutory percentage would produce.

Who Pays, and What Else Is Included in Probate Costs

The attorney's fee, whether statutory-percentage or flat, is paid from the assets of the estate before beneficiaries receive their distributions — not out of pocket by the personal representative personally. The same is generally true of the personal representative's own compensation and the estate's court costs.

A calculator that only shows the attorney-fee percentage is telling you part of the story. Total probate costs for a typical Florida estate — attorney fees, personal representative compensation, court filing fees, required newspaper publication, bond premiums (if a bond is required), postage, and appraisal costs — commonly run somewhere in the range of a few percent of the gross estate value, though this varies significantly based on complexity, whether real property must be sold, and whether anyone contests the proceedings.

One important exclusion: Florida's constitutionally protected homestead property is generally left out of the compensable value used to calculate the statutory attorney fee, because homestead typically passes outside the probate estate to a surviving spouse or heirs under Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution and F.S. § 732.4015.

When Fees Go Beyond the Standard Schedule

The statutory percentages assume routine administration. Florida law separately allows additional, reasonable fees for extraordinary services under § 733.6171(4), which can include:

  • Will contests, will construction disputes, or other adversarial litigation involving the estate
  • Selling, leasing, or mortgaging estate real property
  • Ancillary administration needed for out-of-state assets
  • Litigation to enforce a personal representative's statutory authority

If your family's situation involves a disputed will, a difficult beneficiary, out-of-state real estate, or a business that needs to be wound down, expect the calculator's baseline number to understate the real cost. That's not a reason to panic — it's a reason to have a candid conversation with your attorney about scope before administration begins.

⚠ A calculator can't see your facts. No online tool knows whether your estate has a contested will, a home that needs to be sold, or an out-of-state asset. Treat any calculator result as a starting estimate, not a final number, and confirm the actual fee arrangement in writing with your Florida probate attorney.

Recent Changes That Affect Smaller Florida Estates

Florida periodically adjusts the thresholds that determine which estates qualify for simplified, less expensive procedures. Under recent legislative changes, the monetary threshold for summary administration — a faster, generally less expensive alternative to formal administration — has been increased, so more modest Florida estates may now qualify to skip full administration and its higher costs. Whether your family's estate qualifies depends on the value of the probate assets, how long ago the death occurred, and other facts specific to the estate.

Because these thresholds and procedures are updated by the Florida Legislature from time to time, the sensible move is to have a Florida probate attorney confirm which administration path — summary or formal — applies to your specific estate, rather than relying solely on a generic calculator's assumptions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Florida probate attorney fee calculator result the actual amount I'll pay?
No. It's an estimate based on the statutory 'presumed reasonable' schedule in F.S. § 733.6171. Many Florida attorneys, including our firm, offer flat fees for straightforward estates that come in below the statutory percentage, and the law requires attorneys to disclose that the percentage schedule is not mandatory.
Does the calculator include court costs, filing fees, and publication costs?
Typically no. Most online calculators estimate only the attorney's fee (and sometimes the personal representative's fee) based on estate value. They generally do not include court filing fees, required newspaper publication, bond premiums, postage, or appraisal costs, which add to the total probate bill.
Is homestead property included when calculating the attorney's fee?
Generally no. Florida's homestead property is usually excluded from the 'compensable value' used to calculate the statutory attorney fee because it typically passes outside the probate estate to a surviving spouse or heirs under Florida's homestead laws.
Can I negotiate a flat fee instead of the statutory percentage?
Yes. Florida law expressly permits the attorney, the personal representative, and those bearing the cost of the fee to agree on a different fee arrangement, including a flat fee, as long as it's properly disclosed and not objected to under the Florida Probate Rules.
What estates qualify for Florida's cheaper summary administration process?
Summary administration is generally available for smaller estates under a monetary threshold set by Florida law, or for estates where the death occurred more than two years ago, regardless of value. Because this threshold has recently changed, it's worth confirming the current figure and your eligibility with a Florida probate attorney.
Who actually pays the probate attorney's fee — me or the estate?
The estate pays the attorney's fee, along with the personal representative's compensation and court costs, out of estate assets before any remaining funds are distributed to beneficiaries. The personal representative does not typically pay these fees personally.

The Truestead Takeaway

A Florida probate attorney fee calculator is a genuinely useful first step — it gives you a realistic range based on Florida's statutory schedule under F.S. § 733.6171 — but it can't tell you the whole story, because Florida law itself says that schedule is only presumed reasonable, not mandatory, and many uncontested estates are handled for less through a negotiated flat fee. What I'd want my own family to do is get the calculator estimate, then ask a Florida probate attorney for the written fee disclosure required by law and a clear, honest conversation about whether a flat fee fits your specific estate. If you're facing a Florida probate matter, I'm glad to review the details with you and give you a straight answer.
