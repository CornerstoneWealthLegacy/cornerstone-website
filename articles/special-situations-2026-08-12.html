Blended Families and Snowbirds: Two Florida Situations That Need More Than a Simple Will

Quick Answer

A simple will often fails blended families and part-time Florida residents because Florida's spousal elective share, homestead rules, and domicile requirements can override what a will says. Both situations generally call for a coordinated plan — trusts, clear domicile documentation, and sometimes a marital agreement — reviewed by a Florida attorney.

By Arthur Simpson, Esq. · FL Bar #529265 Florida Estate Planning Attorney August 12, 2026

Why a "simple will" so often falls short for blended families

In my practice, I see this pattern constantly: a remarried client comes in with an old will that says "everything to my spouse, then to my kids," and they assume that settles it. It doesn't — not in Florida, and not for a blended family.

Florida law gives a surviving spouse rights that a will cannot simply write around. Under the elective share statute, F.S. § 732.2065, a surviving spouse is generally entitled to claim a share of the estate — a right that exists regardless of what the will says, unless it was validly waived. On top of that, Florida's homestead protections (Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, and the devise restrictions in F.S. § 732.4015) place real limits on how a homeowner with a spouse can leave the family home, especially if children from a prior relationship are involved.

Meanwhile, stepchildren have no automatic inheritance rights in Florida — none — unless they are named in your estate planning documents or legally adopted. So the same set of rules that protects a spouse can unintentionally disinherit the children you meant to provide for, or vice versa.

  • A surviving spouse's elective share can pull assets away from a trust meant for the first family's children
  • Homestead rules can restrict who inherits the house, even if the deed or will says otherwise
  • Stepchildren get nothing by default — love and daily caregiving don't create inheritance rights on their own

What actually holds up for blended families

What I tell blended-family clients is that the goal isn't to outsmart Florida law — it's to work with it deliberately instead of by accident. Plans that hold up in this situation typically combine a few tools rather than relying on one document:

  • Separate or marital trusts that provide for a surviving spouse during their lifetime while locking in the remainder for children from a prior relationship
  • A properly drafted prenuptial or postnuptial agreement that addresses property rights and, where appropriate, a waiver of elective-share rights
  • Clear, updated beneficiary designations on life insurance and retirement accounts — these pass outside your will and are one of the most common ways blended-family plans go sideways
  • Honest conversations, documented in the plan, about the family home and who has rights to live there or inherit it
Why this matters: A written spousal waiver, done correctly under Florida law with full financial disclosure, is one of the few reliable ways to modify elective-share and certain homestead rights. It has to be done right — informally telling your spouse "I'll leave the house to my kids" is not a waiver.

None of this is about mistrust. It's about making sure the people you love aren't left fighting over ambiguity during one of the hardest weeks of their lives.

Snowbirds: why "where you live" is a legal question, not just a lifestyle choice

For the many Floridians who split time between here and another state, the question of legal domicile is one of the most consequential — and most overlooked — parts of an estate plan. Domicile determines which state's laws govern your estate, where your will is probated, and whether another state can claim income or estate tax against you.

Florida has no state income tax and no state estate tax, which is exactly why so many part-time residents want Florida recognized as their true legal home. But wanting it isn't enough — you have to establish it, and be able to prove it if another state's tax authority ever challenges your residency.

Steps that support a Florida domicile claim generally include:

  • Filing a Declaration of Domicile with the county clerk
  • Registering to vote and obtaining a driver's license in Florida
  • Spending more than half the year in Florida, with records to back it up
  • Moving significant personal property, and shifting banking, healthcare providers, and professional relationships here

Tax auditors from other states typically look at where you spend the most time, which home is larger and more valuable, where sentimental belongings live, where your business interests are centered, and where a spouse or minor children reside — along with secondary clues like vehicle registration and where your mail and bank statements arrive.

⚠ Real estate in another state Owning property in a second state doesn't just raise tax questions — it can also mean a separate probate proceeding in that state after you pass, even if your Florida will covers everything else. Trusts and other titling strategies can often avoid that extra layer of cost and delay, but this needs to be built into the plan in advance.

How these two situations often overlap

I mention snowbirds and blended families together because, in my practice, they frequently show up in the same client: a remarried retiree who spends winters in Naples and summers up north, with children from two marriages and a home in each state. That combination multiplies the number of legal questions — whose homestead protection applies, which state's elective share rules govern, where does probate happen — and it's exactly the kind of situation where a generic online will template is the riskiest possible choice.

The good news is that Florida's legal framework, while it has real teeth, is also flexible enough to accommodate careful planning. Trusts, clear domicile documentation, updated beneficiary forms, and — where a marriage is involved — a properly executed marital agreement can address almost every complication I've described here.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I completely disinherit my spouse in Florida if we have a blended family?
Generally, no — not without a valid waiver. Florida's elective share law and homestead protections give a surviving spouse rights that a will alone typically cannot eliminate, though a properly executed prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can modify some of those rights.
Do my stepchildren automatically inherit from me in Florida?
No. Under Florida law, stepchildren have no automatic inheritance rights unless they are specifically named in your will or trust, or you have legally adopted them.
I own homes in Florida and another state. Will my Florida will cover both?
Not necessarily. Real estate is typically subject to probate in the state where it's located, so out-of-state property may require a separate probate proceeding unless it's titled in a trust or otherwise structured to avoid it.
How do I prove Florida is really my legal domicile if another state challenges it?
Documentation matters — a filed Declaration of Domicile, Florida voter registration and driver's license, time-tracking showing more than half the year spent in Florida, and evidence that your banking, healthcare, and personal ties are centered here all help support the claim.
Is a prenuptial agreement only about divorce, or does it help with estate planning too?
It does both. In blended-family planning, a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can also address property rights and inheritance expectations, including waivers of certain spousal rights, which complements your will and trust.
We're not married but have been together for years and share property — does Florida protect my partner the way a spouse would be protected?
No. Florida does not extend spousal inheritance rights, elective share protections, or homestead rights to unmarried partners, so a deliberate estate plan naming your partner is essential if you want them provided for.

The Truestead Takeaway

Whether you're blending two families under one roof or splitting your year between Florida and another state, the common thread is the same: Florida's default rules on spousal rights, homestead, and domicile will control your estate unless you plan around them intentionally. A simple will rarely does that job on its own. If your family situation involves a second marriage, stepchildren, a home in more than one state, or significant time spent outside Florida, it's worth having a Florida attorney review your current documents — not because something is necessarily wrong, but because these are exactly the situations where small gaps cause the biggest problems later.
